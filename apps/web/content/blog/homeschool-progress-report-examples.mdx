The blank progress report asks for a grade. You remember the book your child can now read more smoothly, the fractions they built with paper pieces, and the cardboard bridge that held the soup-can test. None of that fits neatly inside “B+.”

Before choosing a homeschool progress report template, it helps to know who will read it.

> This guide separates three jobs a homeschool progress report can do. A family planning report shows what looks solid and what to try next. A portfolio summary explains a few selected work samples. A report for an outside authority or program gathers the fields its current form names. These reports have different jobs, so they should not share one universal template.

## Example 1: a report for planning the next few weeks

This report stays inside the family. Its job is to help you remember what you have seen and choose a useful next step.

| Subject | What looks solid | Example | What to try next |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Reading | Reads short-vowel words in a familiar book | Read a short passage with a brief reminder | Try the same pattern in an unfamiliar passage |
| Math | Builds equal groups with objects | Made equal groups with buttons | Write the matching multiplication expression |
| Writing | Tells a complete story aloud | Described the museum trip in order | Write one sentence for each part |

The useful part is the example. “Reading is improving” is hard to act on. “Read a short passage with a brief reminder” gives future-you something concrete to recognize.

This kind of report can be short. One clear example and one next step per subject may be enough for the next planning decision.

## Example 2: a summary for a homeschool portfolio

A portfolio summary helps another reader understand why the selected work matters. The work sample remains the proof. The summary gives it context.

```text
Date: [date]
Subject: Science
Work sample: Seed observation page
Context: Observed the seeds across the week
What changed: Added measurements and labeled the emerging root
Why this sample is here: It shows a longer observation carried across several days
```

A portfolio does not become stronger because every worksheet is inside it. A small series can show change more clearly than a stack of near-duplicates:

1. an early attempt
2. a later attempt at the same kind of work
3. a short note explaining what changed

The reader can see change across time without reading the entire school year.

If the portfolio also serves a program, the pieces named in its current instructions belong in the set.

## Example 3: a preparation page for a required report

The current official instructions for your family decide this format. A blog post or homeschool app cannot replace them.

The form may ask for subjects, attendance, work samples, dates, or a narrative. Its field names and time period can become the headings in your preparation page. The source URL and date of your check can stay in the same folder as the working copy.

A simple preparation page can help:

| Required field | Record or sample that supports it | Still needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Field named by the official form | The matching dated record | Question or missing item |

The preparation page helps you gather the matching records without guessing.

When wording is unclear, the contact or help path named by the authority is the safest next stop. A local attorney who works with homeschool requirements can also help.

## What happened, not a verdict on the child

The clearest report names the work and the support around it.

Instead of “struggles with subtraction,” you can write what you saw: “Solved subtraction with blocks and needed a reminder when trading a ten.”

Instead of “excellent reader,” you can name the evidence: “Read the chapter aloud, paused at unfamiliar words, and explained why the character left.”

Specific language is kinder because it does not turn one day into an identity. It also makes the next choice easier. You can see what support helped, what the child did, and what remains worth trying.

## A report does not need to contain every kind of proof

Grades, checklists, narratives, photos, and work samples each answer different questions. The smallest combination that serves the report’s reader may be enough.

- A grade summarizes work using the grading rules you chose.
- A dated example shows the work itself.
- A short narrative connects several examples.
- A photo can preserve a project that cannot fit in a binder.
- A next-step note turns the report into a planning tool.

Adding every kind does not automatically make the report more useful. A clear purpose and dated examples matter more than decoration.

The same dated examples can also help you [notice whether homeschooling is working](/blog/how-to-know-if-homeschooling-is-working) without grading every day.

## The reader tells you which fields matter

The blank template gets easier once you know who needs it and what they need to understand.

For family planning, you can show what looks solid, an example, and a next step. For a portfolio, a small selection can show the work and why it belongs. For a required report, its form supplies the fields.
