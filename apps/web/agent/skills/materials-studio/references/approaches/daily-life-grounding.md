# Daily-Life Grounding (all ages)

Academic content feels real when the objects on the page are things the child touched today. This is a cross-cutting design principle, not a method: it applies to every subject, every format, and every age band.

- Count the child's shoes, not abstract circles. A counting mat that uses cups in the dishwasher, remote controls on the couch, or shoes by the door teaches the same skill as one that uses clip-art stars, but the child recognizes the objects and the parent does not have to explain what they are looking at.
- Name the technology the child already uses. A K-level literacy script about Mary's lamb encountering school technology (computers, headphones, tablets) grounds reading practice in a world the child already navigates. The technology is not a topic to be learned; it is the setting the child reads inside.
- Clothing as needs-vs-wants. A script about getting new clothes teaches economics vocabulary (need, want, choose) through something every child has direct experience with. The child does not learn "needs and wants" in the abstract and then apply it to clothing; they start from their own closet and discover the concept.
- Weather the child can verify. A reading passage about weather changes is stronger when the child can look out the window and check. Seasonal content should name what the child sees in their own climate, not a textbook climate.
- Forces in play. Pushes and pulls are taught through vehicles the child rides and balls the child throws, not through diagrams of inclined planes.

Source: Benchmark Education, Teatro del Lector Grado K (ref-2026-07-31-benchmark-teatro-del-lector), flagged by Aless with explicit commentary: "Take note on how this teaching manual focuses on day to day life for the students. Discussing technology they use and see as well as clothing. It educates students on their day to day surroundings."

When generating any material, prefer concrete daily-life objects over abstract or generic ones. This is not a theming choice (dinosaur vs. space); it is a grounding choice (the child's own cup vs. a drawn cup).
