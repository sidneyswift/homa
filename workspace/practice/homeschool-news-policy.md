# homeschool-news-policy

> ✅ FRESH — Last researched: 2026-07-02

US homeschool legal/policy landscape and how to find *your own* state's rules. Homeschooling in the US is governed **state by state** — there is no single national homeschool law. This file gives the general categories, a repeatable method to look up your jurisdiction, and the 2025–2026 national policy trends (federal + state school-choice money, microschools). It is intentionally location-agnostic: use the METHOD below to pin down the rules where *you* live.

## Sources / frameworks

- **HSLDA (Home School Legal Defense Association)** — per-state legal summaries + the standard "regulation level" classification used nationwide. [hslda.org/legal](https://hslda.org/legal/) (pick your state).
- **CRHE (Coalition for Responsible Home Education)** — neutral, research-oriented reference on homeschool options, umbrella schools, and policy. [crhe.org/research/current-policy/homeschool-options](https://crhe.org/research/current-policy/homeschool-options/)
- **Your State Department/Agency of Education** — the *authoritative* primary source for your jurisdiction's statute, forms, deadlines, and filing portal. Every state has one; search `"[your state] department of education homeschool"`.
- **Your state's compulsory-attendance statute** — the law that defines the ages a child must be educated and the legal "home instruction" exemption. This is the binding text; secondary guides paraphrase it.
- **IRS — Federal Scholarship Tax Credit (FSTC / IRC §25F)** — primary source for which states have opted into the new federal school-choice credit, updated as governors' elections come in. [irs.gov/.../federal-scholarship-tax-credit-fstc](https://www.irs.gov/government-entities/federal-state-local-governments/federal-scholarship-tax-credit-fstc)
- **JHU Institute for Education Policy — Homeschool Hub** — the most rigorous national data on homeschool growth. [education.jhu.edu/edpolicy/.../homeschool-hub](https://education.jhu.edu/edpolicy/policy-research-initiatives/homeschool-hub/)

## The core fact: requirements vary BY STATE

There is no federal homeschool statute. Each state sets its own rules across five recurring dimensions. When you research your own state, get an answer for each:

### The five things every state's law addresses

1. **Notification / registration** — do you file anything, with whom, and when? Ranges from *nothing* (e.g., TX, AK, ID require no notice at all) to a one-time letter of intent, to an annual filing (e.g., NY requires an Individualized Home Instruction Plan submitted each year). Deadlines matter: many states require filing a set number of days *before* you begin instruction.
2. **Required subjects** — most states list core subjects (language arts/English, math, science, social studies) and some add PE, health, and fine arts. A few (TX, AK) have minimal subject requirements. Match your curriculum to your state's list.
3. **Assessment / evaluation** — roughly 20 states require an annual academic assessment: a **standardized test**, a **portfolio review by a certified teacher**, or an **evaluation by a qualified evaluator** you choose. Many states let *you* pick which option. Many other states require no assessment at all.
4. **Portfolio / record-keeping** — some states mandate a portfolio of student work (e.g., PA, MD, OH, SC, FL among the stricter ones); many more expect records kept privately (attendance, samples, reading logs) that you may need to *produce on request* even if never routinely reviewed. Record-keeping of some kind is expected in a large majority of states.
5. **Instructor qualifications** — a minority of states require the teaching parent to hold a high-school diploma/GED or equivalent; most impose no credential.

### Compulsory-age reality check

Every state defines a **compulsory attendance age range** (commonly ages 5/6 to 16/18). *Below the bottom of that range, you generally have no legal obligation to file anything* — early years (PreK, ages 3–5) are typically voluntary homeschooling with no notification, portfolio, or oversight in most states. The legal clock starts when your child hits compulsory age (often keyed to their age as of a cutoff date like Sept 1/30). Confirm your state's exact bottom age and cutoff — it determines your first filing deadline.

> ⚠️ **Withdrawal sequencing.** If your child is *currently enrolled* in public school, do not simply stop sending them. File your notice of intent first, complete any required withdrawal step, and get confirmation *before* home instruction begins — otherwise absences can be logged as truancy and, in some states, trigger an educational-neglect/CPS referral. If your child was never enrolled, this doesn't apply. (Method, not a specific jurisdiction — check your district's withdrawal procedure.)

## The general regulation-level categories (HSLDA framework)

HSLDA classifies every state into one of four tiers. Knowing your tier tells you roughly how much work compliance is:

### No-notice states
No notification to any government entity is required. ~11 states fall here (2026). You just teach — keep your own records for your own benefit.

### Low-regulation states
Notice to the local district or the state, with **no** ongoing reporting, testing, or evaluation.

### Moderate-regulation states
Notice **plus** test scores and/or a professional evaluation of the student's progress (and often a portfolio requirement).

### High-regulation states
Notice **plus** evaluation/testing **plus** additional requirements — e.g., curriculum approval, teacher qualifications, or home visits. The consistently strictest states in 2026 are **Massachusetts, New York, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, and Vermont**.

*Use these as categories, not verdicts — always confirm against your state's current statute, since tiers shift as laws change (see "Recent trends").*

## How to find YOUR state's rules (the method)

A repeatable lookup, most authoritative first:

1. **Start with your State Department/Agency of Education.** Search `"[your state] department of education homeschool"` (or "home instruction" / "home education" — the legal term varies). This gives you official forms, the filing portal, deadlines, and the required-subjects list straight from the regulator.
2. **Read the compulsory-attendance statute itself.** Search `"[your state] compulsory attendance statute homeschool"` and find the actual code section. Secondary sites paraphrase; the statute is what governs. Note the compulsory age range and the cutoff date.
3. **Cross-check with HSLDA's state page.** [hslda.org/legal](https://hslda.org/legal/) → your state. Good plain-language summary of notice, subjects, assessment, and qualifications, plus the regulation tier. (HSLDA is advocacy-leaning, so verify specifics against the state source.)
4. **Cross-check with CRHE** for a neutral second read on options and umbrella schools: [crhe.org](https://crhe.org/research/current-policy/homeschool-options/).
5. **Find a local/state homeschool association or support group.** Statewide organizations track pending legislation and know local district practices (e.g., how withdrawal actually works, which evaluators are accepted). Search `"[your state] homeschool association"`.
6. **Confirm the assessment path you'll use** (if your state requires one): standardized test vs. certified-teacher portfolio review vs. independent evaluator — and line up the provider before the deadline.
7. **Check your school-choice funding status** (see below): your state's ESA/scholarship program page + the IRS FSTC opt-in list.

*Do this once when you start, then re-check annually — deadlines and opt-in status change every year.*

## Umbrella / cover schools

In some states, homeschooling is done **through an umbrella (a.k.a. cover, church-related, or "accountability") school** — a private school that supervises home-educated students for legal compliance. Parents teach at home; the umbrella maintains records, issues report cards/transcripts, tracks attendance, and satisfies state reporting.

- **When they're required:** a handful of states essentially require families to homeschool under a private-school/umbrella structure, or make it the simplest legal path. Umbrellas are most relevant in states like **Alabama, Tennessee, North Carolina, Florida, Texas, and Missouri**; South Carolina's "Third Option" (§59-65-47) accountability associations are a related model.
- **When they're optional-but-useful:** even where not required, an umbrella can provide transcripts, standardized testing, portfolio review, diplomas, and graduation services — handy for high-schoolers headed to college.
- **Scale:** roughly 60,000 US families operate under an umbrella school (2024 estimate).
- **Check yours:** search `"[your state] umbrella cover school homeschool"` and confirm what it does/doesn't cover before enrolling.

## National policy trends (2025–2026)

### 🆕 First-ever FEDERAL school-choice money — ECCA / EFTC / FSTC (§25F)
The **Educational Choice for Children Act (ECCA)** — branded the **Education Freedom Tax Credit (EFTC)**, codified as the IRS **Federal Scholarship Tax Credit (FSTC), IRC §25F** — was enacted **July 4, 2025** (OBBBA §70411). It's the nation's first federal tax-credit scholarship program and it **explicitly covers homeschooling expenses.**
- **How it works:** individual taxpayers get a **100% non-refundable federal income-tax credit up to $1,700/year** for donations to a qualifying **Scholarship Granting Organization (SGO)** (a 501(c)(3) that must spend ≥90% on scholarships). Those scholarships then fund eligible K–12 families' Coverdell-type expenses — tuition, books, supplies, tutoring, tech, special-needs services, **and homeschooling.** Permanent, no sunset; unused credit carries forward up to 5 years.
- **Family eligibility:** student must be K–12-public-school-eligible and from a household at **≤300% of Area Median Income** (covers most families).
- **The catch — state opt-in:** families only qualify if **their state's governor opts in** by submitting a list of qualifying SGOs to the U.S. Treasury by **Jan 1 each year.** The program starts **Jan 1, 2027.** Opt-in is a live, year-by-year political decision — some governors have opted in, others declined or vetoed (e.g., Arizona's governor vetoed participation). **Check your state's status on the IRS FSTC page each January.**

### State ESAs continue to expand
**30+ states** now run some private-school-choice or Education Savings Account (ESA) program; **~18 states** offer near-universal-eligibility ESAs open to most/all K–12 families regardless of income — public funds usable for homeschool curriculum, tutoring, and learning tools (AL, AZ, AR, FL, GA, IN, IA, LA, NH, NC, SC, TN, TX, UT, WV, WY, and others). 2026 saw major expansions: Arizona surpassed 100,000 ESA students; Texas's new program enrolled ~96,000 for 2026–27. **Check whether your state has an ESA** — search `"[your state] education savings account homeschool"`.

### Microschools going mainstream
Microschools (small, mixed-age learning hubs, typically 5–15 students, often blending homeschool families) now serve an estimated **~2% of US students (~750,000+ kids)** across roughly 95,000 sites, per the National Microschooling Center. The median microschool grew from 16 students (2024) to 22 (2025) — "less micro" every year, and a common bridge between full homeschooling and traditional school.

### Growth remains strong
Homeschooling is among the fastest-growing US education models: recent national estimates put homeschooled children at roughly **3.4 million (~6%)** of school-age kids, with 80% of reporting states seeing increases in 2024–25 (SC led at +21.5%). ~9 in 10 homeschool families use a **mix** of online and in-person supports — co-ops, tutors, online classes, hybrid schools.

### Deregulation vs. re-regulation — both directions
The dominant direction is **loosening**: Vermont ended mandatory year-end assessment submission (H.461, 2023); Ohio simplified notification and dropped mandatory assessments (late 2024). Countervailing pressure exists — a "Make Homeschool Safe" model bill (from CRHE) would add curriculum review, mandatory assessments, and home visits; HSLDA opposes it and **no state has enacted it.** Illinois's HB 2827 notification bill stalled in 2025. **Watch your own legislature** via your state homeschool association.

### Homeschoolers' access rights vary
Access to public-school sports/activities, the ability to take AP exams, dual-enrollment, and special-education services all vary by state (and for special ed, generally *end* once a child is withdrawn from public school, though a public evaluation can often still be requested). Confirm each with your state DOE and local district.

## Key takeaways

1. **There is no national homeschool law — your state governs.** Get a concrete answer for all five dimensions (notification, subjects, assessment, records, instructor qualifications) from your **State Department of Education** first. [hslda.org/legal](https://hslda.org/legal/)
2. **Below compulsory age, most states require nothing.** Early years are usually voluntary; the legal clock starts at your state's compulsory-attendance age and cutoff date — confirm both.
3. **If withdrawing an enrolled child, sequence it:** file intent → complete withdrawal → get confirmation → *then* start home instruction, to avoid truancy/CPS exposure. [crhe.org](https://crhe.org/research/current-policy/homeschool-options/)
4. **Know your regulation tier (no-notice / low / moderate / high)** to size the compliance workload — but verify against the current statute, since tiers shift as laws change. [everyhomeschool.com/guides/state-laws-2026](https://everyhomeschool.com/guides/state-laws-2026)
5. **In stricter or private-school-structure states, an umbrella/cover school** can carry the record-keeping, testing, and transcripts for you. [crhe.org/.../homeschool-umbrella-schools](https://crhe.org/guides/resources-for-homeschool-parents/support/homeschool-umbrella-schools/)
6. **Check school-choice money yearly.** Federal FSTC (§25F) starts Jan 1, 2027 but only for families in **opted-in** states — verify your state's status each January on the IRS FSTC page. Separately, check whether your state runs an ESA. [irs.gov/.../federal-scholarship-tax-credit-fstc](https://www.irs.gov/government-entities/federal-state-local-governments/federal-scholarship-tax-credit-fstc)
7. **Re-check annually.** Deadlines, assessment providers, ESA eligibility, and FSTC opt-in all change year to year — treat compliance as a yearly review, not a one-time setup.
8. **Join a state homeschool association** for legislative alerts and local, practical know-how (accepted evaluators, actual district withdrawal steps).
