# learning-science

> ✅ FRESH — Last researched: 2026-07-02

Evidence-based learning science for homeschooling across PreK–12 (ages 3–18): how memory, practice, and instruction actually work, and how to apply the most robustly replicated cognitive-science findings at each developmental stage. This is general pedagogical wisdom (Tier B), not per-child guidance.

## Sources / frameworks

1. **Bjork's Desirable Difficulties (1994; Bjork & Bjork 2020)** — Spacing, retrieval, interleaving, and generation are conditions that slow acquisition but strengthen long-term retention and transfer. Foundational because these are among the most replicated findings in cognitive psychology and apply from preschool through high school. [bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2021/01/RABjorkELBjorkJARMAC2020ForPostingSingleSpaced.pdf)
2. **Rosenshine's Principles of Instruction (2012)** — Ten research-informed teaching routines synthesized from cognitive science, effective-teacher observation, and cognitive-support research. The practical backbone for structuring lessons at every grade. [aft.org/Rosenshine](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)
3. **Deans for Impact — The Science of Learning, 2nd ed. (2026)** — Distills cognitive-science consensus into six questions every educator should be able to answer (how students understand new ideas, learn & retain, solve problems, transfer, stay motivated, and where they hold misconceptions), with concrete instructional implications. The 2nd edition (May 2026) adds a section on helping students self-regulate. [deansforimpact.org](https://www.deansforimpact.org/tools-and-resources/the-science-of-learning)
4. **Brown, Roediger & McDaniel — *Make It Stick* (2014)** — The definitive practitioner translation of the testing effect, spacing, interleaving, and desirable difficulties. [hup.harvard.edu](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674729018)
5. **Sweller — Cognitive Load Theory** — Working memory is severely limited (a handful of novel elements at once); instruction should manage intrinsic load, cut extraneous load, and free capacity for schema-building. NSW CESE's teacher summary is the cleanest applied version. [education.nsw.gov.au CLT](https://education.nsw.gov.au/content/dam/main-education/about-us/educational-data/cese/2017-cognitive-load-theory.pdf)
6. **Paivio's Dual Coding + Mayer's Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning** — Verbal and visual information are processed in separate channels; pairing well-aligned words and pictures builds richer, more retrievable memories — but only when the two are coherent and not redundant. [Mayer, Multimedia Learning research](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/225249172_Dual_Coding_Theory_and_Education)
7. **Ebbinghaus forgetting curve (1885), replicated Murre & Dros (2015)** — Forgetting is steep and predictable; spaced review flattens the curve. The 2015 direct replication landed within a few percentage points of the 1885 data. [PLOS ONE replication](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0120644)
8. **Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development + Scaffolding / Gradual Release ("I do, we do, you do")** — Target the edge of current ability, provide temporary support, then fade it. Fisher & Frey formalized the release model for instruction; it maps cleanly to 1:1 teaching. [structural-learning GRR](https://www.structural-learning.com/post/gradual-release-of-responsibility)
9. **Cepeda et al. (2008) — temporal ridgeline of optimal spacing** — The best gap between study sessions scales with how long you need to remember: roughly 10–40% of the target retention interval. Err longer, not shorter. [laplab.ucsd.edu](https://laplab.ucsd.edu/articles/Cepeda%20et%20al%202008_psychsci.pdf)
10. **Growth-mindset evidence, both sides** — Yeager et al. (2019, *Nature*) shows a short intervention helps lower-achieving students in supportive contexts; Sisk et al. (2018) shows overall effects are small and context-dependent. Read together, not selectively.

## The core principles

### 1. Retrieval practice (the testing effect)
Actively recalling information strengthens memory far more than re-reading or re-viewing it; Roediger & Karpicke's classic work shows retrieval can improve later recall by ~50% over restudy. Low-stakes, frequent, ungraded retrieval is the operative form — quizzes, "explain it back," flashcards, Rosenshine's daily review.
- **PreK (3–5):** Recall as play. "What letter does flamingo start with?" instead of "F is for flamingo." "What did we learn about butterflies yesterday?" Getting it wrong then hearing the answer still helps.
- **Lower Elementary (K–2):** Verbal recall of sight words, math facts, story events; simple flashcards; narration ("tell me back what happened").
- **Upper Elementary (3–5):** Brief written recall (brain dumps), exit tickets, self-quizzing math facts, "quiz the parent."
- **Middle (6–8):** Free-recall summaries, self-testing before checking notes, spaced cumulative quizzes.
- **High (9–12):** Practice tests under near-exam conditions, past-paper questions, self-explanation, Feynman-style teaching of the concept aloud.
> **Boundary (Kranz et al. 2024):** The testing effect is strongest for facts and vocabulary and weaker for complex cognitive *strategies* — for grades 5–6 learning a control-of-variables strategy, restudy actually beat retrieval. Motor/procedural skills (handwriting, an instrument, lab technique) improve through scaffolded repetition + feedback, not recall quizzing. Match the tool to the memory type.

### 2. Spaced practice (distributed practice)
Spreading study across days beats cramming the same total time; meta-analyses put spaced study at ~10–30% better retention than massed study. Cepeda's ridgeline: optimal gap ≈ 10–40% of the retention horizon.
- **PreK/Lower Elementary:** Short daily touches, cycling old material back in (5 new vocab today, revisit 3 tomorrow, mix 2 old ones the next day). A 3-pile Leitner routine (right → less frequent pile; wrong → sooner) is a no-app implementation.
- **Upper Elementary → Middle:** Cumulative homework that revisits prior units; weekly and monthly review (Rosenshine principles 1 and 10).
- **High:** Distributed revision schedules across weeks; spaced-repetition flashcard software (Anki) for vocabulary, formulas, and dates. For year-long retention, gaps of days-to-weeks are appropriate; short gaps hurt more than long ones.

### 3. Interleaving
Mixing related problem types or topics within a session (ABCABC) outperforms blocking one type at a time (AAABBB) for long-term retention and, crucially, for choosing the *right* method on a mixed test.
- **PreK:** Keep it gentle — 2–3 topics per session, not chaos (mix a little math, a letter, a Spanish word rather than 30 min of one thing).
- **Elementary → High (esp. math):** Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung (2020), a randomized controlled trial across 54 seventh-grade classes, found mostly-interleaved practice beat mostly-blocked on an unannounced test one month later, 61% vs 38% (Cohen's d ≈ 0.83 — a large effect). Practical form: worksheets that shuffle problem *kinds* so students must first decide which strategy applies.
> **Caveat:** Interleaving takes more time per item and feels harder in the moment (a desirable difficulty). Introduce a skill in a small blocked set first, then interleave it with older skills for durability.

### 4. Dual coding
Pair words with well-matched visuals — diagrams, timelines, sketches, graphic organizers, worked-example figures — so the same idea is encoded in two channels. Mayer's multimedia principles specify the conditions: keep graphics and words coherent and adjacent (contiguity), narrate over a diagram rather than duplicating on-screen text (modality/redundancy), and strip decorative clutter.
- **PreK/Elementary:** Picture-word cards, drawing what was read, acting concepts out, number lines and ten-frames.
- **Middle/High:** Concept maps, labeled diagrams, dual-coded notes (Cornell + sketches), annotated timelines and process flows. Avoid "multimedia overload" — extra visuals that don't carry meaning add extraneous load and hurt.

### 5. Cognitive load theory
Working memory holds only a few novel elements at once; long-term memory is effectively unlimited. Design instruction to respect this:
- **Intrinsic load** (inherent difficulty / element interactivity) — sequence from simpler to complex; teach components before combining them.
- **Extraneous load** (poor design) — remove: split-attention layouts, redundant text, distracting extras. This is the lever teachers most control.
- **Germane load** (schema-building) — what you want to protect.
Practical moves: **worked examples** for novices (fade to problem-solving as expertise grows — the *expertise-reversal effect* means techniques that help beginners can hurt experts), small steps (Rosenshine), and checking for a high success rate (~80%) before advancing. Younger learners and novices in any domain have the least working-memory headroom — chunk aggressively.

### 6. Desirable difficulties — and the undesirable kind
Bjork's key insight: **performance during learning is a poor indicator of actual learning.** Effortful conditions (spacing, retrieval, interleaving, varied practice, generation) depress in-session performance while boosting durable learning. But difficulty only helps when the learner has the prior knowledge to meet it.
- **Desirable:** effort that triggers deeper processing within reach.
- **Undesirable:** tasks beyond the ZPD, confusing instructions, overload, anxiety. If a learner is shutting down, the difficulty has become undesirable — scaffold down, don't push through.

### 7. Generation & pretesting
Having learners attempt an answer *before* being taught activates prior knowledge and creates a hook for the correct answer. Pan et al. (2026, NUS) confirmed the pretesting effect across four experiments in L2 vocabulary (guess-the-word-from-picture beats passive study). "What do you think happens when we plant a seed?" before the lesson outperforms telling first.
> **Boundary (Seabrooke et al. 2019):** Errorful guessing strengthens memory for *items* but not for arbitrary, unrelated cue–target *associations*. Keep guesses meaningfully connected to what the learner already knows; don't force wild guesses on random pairings.

### 8. Memory & the forgetting curve
Ebbinghaus showed forgetting is steep early and then levels off; the 2015 replication (Murre & Dros) confirmed the shape. The remedy is spaced, retrieval-based review timed before the point of forgetting — the basis of every modern flashcard system (Anki, Duolingo). Practical: schedule the first review within a day, then stretch the intervals.
- **Sleep matters:** Souabni et al. (2025) meta-analysis found naps give a *moderate* declarative-memory consolidation boost in preschoolers (Hedges' g = 0.60), and a missed nap isn't fully recovered by night sleep. Front-load new/hard material earlier in the day; protect sleep at all ages (adolescents especially — consolidation and shifted circadian rhythms make sleep a genuine learning input).

### 9. Growth mindset — with caveats
The belief that ability can grow with effort and strategy is worth cultivating, but the research demands nuance:
- **Where it works:** Yeager et al. (2019, *Nature*), a ~12,500-student national RCT, found a <1-hour online intervention raised grades for *lower-achieving* 9th graders and increased advanced-math enrollment — but *only* where school/peer norms supported it.
- **The caveat:** Sisk et al. (2018), two meta-analyses (365,915 + 57,000 participants), found overall effects are small (mindset ~1% of achievement variance; intervention d ≈ 0.08), with benefits concentrated in high-risk / low-SES students. Many interventions didn't even shift mindset as intended.
- **Takeaway:** Praise process and strategy over fixed traits ("you found a smart way to check that" not "you're so smart"), normalize struggle as part of learning — but don't treat mindset as a substitute for good instruction, spacing, and retrieval. It's a supporting condition, not a lever on its own.

### 10. Scaffolding within the ZPD, then fading
Find what the learner can *almost* do, provide just enough support to succeed, then gradually remove it (gradual release: I do → we do → you do). Not linear — learners revisit earlier stages on harder tasks or hard days.
- **All ages:** Worked examples and heavy modeling for novices; guided practice with immediate feedback; independent practice once success is reliable. Don't over-scaffold established skills — redundant support can sap motivation. The art is calibration: too easy = boredom, too hard = shutdown.

### The relationships underneath
The best-known learning *skill* is the responsive back-and-forth of teaching itself — Harvard's *serve-and-return*: following the learner's lead, naming what they're doing, building on their questions. For young children this responsive interaction is itself a primary driver of cognitive development; for older students, dialogic questioning (Rosenshine: "ask questions, check for understanding") plays the analogous role. Content delivery matters less than responsive engagement at every age.

## Key takeaways

- **Test to learn, don't just test to grade.** Frequent low-stakes retrieval (self-quizzing, brain dumps, "explain it back") beats re-reading; expect ~50% recall gains over restudy (Roediger & Karpicke; [Make It Stick](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674729018)).
- **Space it out and cycle it back.** Distributed review beats cramming by ~10–30%; set review gaps at roughly 10–40% of how long you need the material to last, erring long ([Cepeda et al. 2008](https://laplab.ucsd.edu/articles/Cepeda%20et%20al%202008_psychsci.pdf)).
- **Interleave problem types, especially in math.** Shuffling kinds of problems produced a large advantage (d ≈ 0.83) on a delayed test in a 7th-grade RCT — it teaches learners to pick the right method ([Rohrer et al. 2020](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED611830.pdf)).
- **Manage working memory: small steps, worked examples, high success rate.** Cut extraneous load, chunk novel material, and keep success near 80% before advancing ([Rosenshine 2012](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf); [CLT summary](https://education.nsw.gov.au/content/dam/main-education/about-us/educational-data/cese/2017-cognitive-load-theory.pdf)).
- **Pair words with meaningful visuals, then stop.** Coherent, adjacent word+picture combinations aid learning; decorative or redundant visuals add load and hurt (Mayer's multimedia principles).
- **Let learners guess before you tell them** — but keep the guess connected to prior knowledge ([Pan et al. 2026](https://link.springer.com/10.1186/s41235-026-00708-y); bounded by [Seabrooke et al. 2019](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749596X18300858)).
- **Match the method to the memory type.** Retrieval/pretesting for facts and vocab; scaffolded repetition + feedback for motor and complex-strategy skills ([Kranz et al. 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00220973.2024.2392684)).
- **Protect sleep; front-load hard material.** Consolidation depends on sleep at every age — naps for young children, adequate night sleep for adolescents ([Souabni et al. 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1389945725003247)).
- **Use growth-mindset language, not as a crutch.** Praise process; the evidence supports modest, context-dependent benefits — biggest for struggling learners in supportive settings — not a cure-all ([Yeager et al. 2019](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/a-national-experiment-reveals-where-a-growth-mindset-improves-ach/); [Sisk et al. 2018](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797617739704)).
