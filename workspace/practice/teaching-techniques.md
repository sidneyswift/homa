# teaching-techniques

> ✅ FRESH — Last researched: 2026-07-02

Concrete, research-backed instructional techniques for teaching **any subject across PreK–12** in a 1:1 or small-group homeschool setting. Covers scaffolding & gradual release, direct instruction, questioning & Socratic dialogue, formative assessment & feedback, differentiation, worked examples, think-alouds, narration & copywork, manipulatives / CPA, and spiral vs mastery sequencing — with effect sizes where the evidence supports them.

## Age bands (used throughout)
- **PreK (ages 3–5)**
- **Lower Elementary (K–2, ages 5–8)**
- **Upper Elementary (grades 3–5, ages 8–11)**
- **Middle (grades 6–8, ages 11–14)**
- **High (grades 9–12, ages 14–18)**

## Sources / frameworks

1. **Rosenshine, *Principles of Instruction* (2012)** — 10 evidence-based routines distilled from cognitive science, classroom observation of master teachers, and cognitive-support research: daily review, small steps, ask questions, models, guided practice, check for understanding, high success rate, scaffolds for hard tasks, independent practice, weekly/monthly review. The single most practical synthesis for K–12 explicit teaching. (aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)
2. **Hattie, *Visible Learning* / *Visible Learning: The Sequel* (2023)** — Synthesis of 2,100+ meta-analyses, 350+ influences. d = 0.40 is the "hinge point" (average expected growth); above it = above-average impact. Key effect sizes cited below (feedback ~0.62–0.73, direct instruction ~0.59, worked examples, spaced practice 0.71). Treat effect sizes as directional, not precise. (visible-learning.org)
3. **Fisher & Frey, Gradual Release of Responsibility (GRR)** — "I do / we do / you do" plus a collaborative "you do together" phase. Teacher control diminishes as learner competence grows. Roots in Pearson & Gallagher (1983). (Fisher & Frey, 2013/2021)
4. **Bruner, Concrete–Pictorial–Abstract (CPA) (1966)** — Enactive → iconic → symbolic representation. The backbone of Singapore-style math; also applies to science, grammar, and any abstract concept. Dienes' manipulatives sit at the concrete stage.
5. **Wiliam & Black, *Embedded Formative Assessment*** — Five strategies for using assessment to drive learning; classroom formative assessment can roughly double the rate of learning (Black & Wiliam 1998 review of ~600 studies).
6. **Tomlinson, Differentiated Instruction** — Differentiate content, process, product, and learning environment by readiness, interest, and learning profile, anchored in ongoing formative assessment.
7. **NAEYC Developmentally Appropriate Practice (DAP)** — 10 core teaching strategies for early childhood (acknowledging, encouraging, modeling, demonstrating, creating challenges, giving hints, providing information, giving directions, co-constructing, scaffolding). Grounds the PreK/K-2 end.
8. **Cognitive-science practice principles** — Retrieval practice, spacing/distributed practice, and interleaving (Roediger, Bjork, Rohrer & Taylor). These govern *sequencing* and *review*, not just delivery.

## Scaffolding & Gradual Release ("I do / we do / you do")

The organizing frame for almost every lesson. Provide support just above the learner's independent level (Vygotsky's ZPD), then **systematically fade it** — the fade is the active ingredient, not the support (Gou et al. 2025 meta-analysis, g ≈ 0.78 on problem-solving; the *fading mechanism* was the real moderator, not who scaffolds or for how long). Support that never fades is a crutch, not a scaffold.

The four GRR phases:
- **I do** — teacher models with a think-aloud (see below).
- **We do** — guided practice; teacher and learner solve together, teacher prompts and hints.
- **You do together** — collaborative/peer practice (or, in 1:1, learner leads while you observe).
- **You do alone** — independent practice, only once success rate is high.

**By band:**
- **PreK:** Heavy "I do / we do." Demonstrate-then-invite; hand-under-hand only briefly. Co-construction (building together as equals) is the highest DAP strategy — use it for science play and art.
- **Lower Elem (K–2):** Short "I do," longer "we do." Fade physical prompts on handwriting, blending, counting (manipulatives → pointing → mental).
- **Upper Elem (3–5):** Learners can hold more of "you do together." Scaffold multi-step procedures (long division, paragraph writing) with checklists and worked examples that you thin out over time.
- **Middle (6–8):** Fade toward independent work; scaffold *executive function* (planning long tasks, self-checking) more than content. Watch for over-scaffolding — a sign is the learner waiting for help before attempting.
- **High (9–12):** Minimal modeling for known skills; scaffold only novel/complex tasks (lab design, essay argumentation, proofs). Goal is a self-regulating learner who scaffolds themselves.

**Anti-pattern — over-scaffolding:** jumping in before productive struggle. Signs: learner is passive, can't perform alone, or waits for rescue. Keep struggle in the sweet spot (hard but achievable with backup) and hold back through the first wave of difficulty.

## Direct / Explicit Instruction & Rosenshine's routines

Direct instruction (explicit, structured, teacher-led) has a Hattie effect size of ~**0.59**. It is *not* lecturing at learners — it is small steps, high interaction, and constant checking. Rosenshine's 10 principles are the operational checklist:

1. Begin with a short **daily review** of prior learning.
2. Present new material in **small steps** with practice after each.
3. **Ask a large number of questions** and check all learners' responses.
4. Provide **models** and worked examples.
5. **Guide practice** (we do) before independent work.
6. **Check for understanding** frequently.
7. Aim for a **high success rate** (~80% on guided practice) before moving on.
8. Provide **scaffolds** for difficult tasks; remove as competence grows.
9. Require **independent practice**.
10. Engage in **weekly and monthly review** (spaced practice).

**By band:** the *structure* is constant; the *step size and pace* change. PreK/K-2 need very small steps and immediate checks; middle/high can hold larger chunks in working memory but still benefit from review and worked examples. Explicit instruction is especially high-leverage for novices and for foundational skills (phonics, math facts, grammar mechanics) at every age.

## Worked Examples

A worked example shows the full solution path so the learner studies *how*, not just *what*. It reduces cognitive load for novices so working memory is spent on the reasoning, not on flailing — the **worked-example effect** (Sweller, cognitive load theory). Especially powerful before learners have schemas; the benefit shrinks and can reverse for experts (the *expertise reversal effect*), so fade worked examples into completion problems ("faded worked examples") then full problems.

- **PreK–K:** Model letter formation / a counting routine step by step; child imitates.
- **Elem:** Pair every new algorithm (regrouping, long division, fraction addition) with 2–3 worked examples; then use *completion problems* (some steps filled in).
- **Middle/High:** Worked examples for algebra manipulations, proofs, chemical equations, essay structure. Use *self-explanation prompts* ("why is this step legal?") — self-explanation of worked examples is one of the most robust findings in the literature.
- **Comparison move:** show a correct and a flawed worked example side by side; have the learner find and fix the error (builds evaluative judgment).

## Think-Alouds & Modeling

Make invisible cognitive processes audible. This is the "I do" phase done well: narrate the *decisions, false starts, and self-checks*, not just the answer. "Hmm, I could add these fractions but the denominators differ — I need a common one first… is 12 right? Let me check."

- **PreK/K-2:** Think aloud during blending ("/c/ … /a/ … /t/ … cat!"), letter choice, and simple counting.
- **Upper Elem:** Model reading-comprehension strategies (predicting, questioning, summarizing, clarifying — reciprocal teaching) and multi-step math.
- **Middle/High:** Model expert problem-solving heuristics, annotation of a primary source, planning an experiment, drafting a thesis. Then hand the think-aloud to the learner ("now you narrate your thinking") — self-talk is a strong metacognitive lever.

## Questioning & Socratic Dialogue

Questioning is one of Rosenshine's pillars and a core NAEYC/DAP strategy. Two levers:

- **Wait time:** After asking, wait **3–5 seconds** (Rowe 1986). Most adults wait <1s. Longer wait time increases response length, correctness, and participation at every age. Wait again *after* the answer before responding.
- **Question mix:** Alternate questions with comments — roughly **1 question : 2–3 statements**. Nonstop questioning becomes an interrogation and kills engagement (a common cause of "reading resistance" in young children).

**Open-ended and higher-order questions** push prediction, explanation, and reasoning:
- **PreK/K-2:** "What do you think happens next? Why did you put that there?" Use CROWD prompts in reading (Completion, Recall, Open-ended, Wh-, Distancing — dialogic reading, PEER-CROWD).
- **Upper Elem:** "How do you know? What's your evidence? Is there another way?"
- **Middle:** Socratic seminar with texts — questions of clarification, assumptions, evidence, viewpoints, implications, and questions about the question.
- **High:** Full Socratic method: challenge premises, demand justification, surface contradictions, pursue implications. Learner-generated questions become the driver.

**Cold-call / no-hands + think-pair-share** (from Wiliam) keep all learners cognitively active in group settings; in 1:1, use "no opt-out" (return to a missed question after a scaffold).

## Formative Assessment & Feedback

Feedback is among the highest-impact influences (~**0.62–0.73**, Hattie) — *but* about a third of feedback studies show negative effects, so *how* you give it matters as much as *whether*. Formative assessment (assessment that changes the next teaching move) can roughly double learning rate (Black & Wiliam).

**Wiliam's five strategies:**
1. Clarify and share **learning intentions & success criteria**.
2. Engineer **discussions/tasks that elicit evidence** of understanding (hinge questions, exit tickets, mini-whiteboards).
3. Provide **feedback that moves learners forward** (actionable, task-focused, not just a grade).
4. Activate learners as **instructional resources for one another** (peer feedback — needs structure).
5. Activate learners as **owners of their own learning** (self-assessment, metacognition).

**Feedback that works:**
- Feedback about the **task and process/strategy** beats feedback about the *person* (Hattie & Timperley's levels). Avoid ability praise.
- Use **process praise** ("you kept going when it got hard / you tried a different strategy") over person praise ("you're so smart"). Process praise in early childhood predicts a growth mindset years later (Gunderson et al. 2013).
- **Errorful learning + pretesting:** have the learner *guess first, then reveal immediately*. A wrong guess followed by correction is remembered better than being told outright — the effect appears as young as age 3 and persists ~5 weeks. For young children, **immediate** feedback beats delayed. Flip flashcards from show-and-say to guess-then-reveal.
- **Expand, don't just correct.** Child: "The dog goed home." Adult: "Yes — the dog *went* home." Recasting/expanding preserves confidence and models the target.

**By band:** PreK/K-2 need immediate, concrete, oral feedback; upper-elem+ can use rubrics and self/peer assessment; middle/high can own success criteria and act on written, delayed feedback (delayed can even help older learners consolidate).

## Differentiation

Adjust the lesson to the learner (Tomlinson) along four dimensions — **content** (what), **process** (how they engage), **product** (how they show it), **environment** — by **readiness, interest, and learning profile**. Anchored in formative assessment so you differentiate on real evidence, not assumptions.

- **1:1 homeschool advantage:** differentiation is nearly automatic — pace, entry point, and interest can be tuned per lesson. Use this: accelerate mastered strands, slow down growth edges, thread the learner's interests through content.
- **Multi-age / co-op (small group):** tier the same task by challenge level; use flexible grouping; offer choice of product (write / build / present).
- **Watch for:** differentiating on *assumed* ability rather than demonstrated performance (a documented over-scaffolding trap). Match support to what the learner can actually do today.

## Narration & Copywork

Charlotte Mason techniques with cognitive-science backing:
- **Narration** = the learner tells back, in their own words, what was just read/heard. This is **retrieval practice** in disguise — one of the most robust learning boosters — plus it builds oral composition, sequencing, and comprehension. Oral narration (PreK–2) → written narration (upper elem+) → analytical/essay narration (middle/high).
- **Copywork** = carefully copying excellent sentences/passages. Builds handwriting/fluency, spelling, punctuation, and internalizes syntax and style through worked examples of good prose. Progress to **dictation** (studied passage recalled and written) — a spaced retrieval + transcription workout. Scale complexity by band: single words/short sentences (K-2) → paragraphs (elem) → passages with sophisticated structure (middle/high).

## Manipulatives & CPA (Concrete → Pictorial → Abstract)

Bruner's enactive → iconic → symbolic progression. Ground every abstract concept in something physical first, then a drawing/model, then symbols. Meta-analyses and multiple studies show CPA improves math achievement and motivation across levels.

- **Concrete:** physical objects — counters, base-ten blocks, Cuisenaire rods, fraction tiles, algebra tiles, balance scales.
- **Pictorial:** drawings, bar models (Singapore), number lines, area models, diagrams.
- **Abstract:** numerals, symbols, equations, formal notation.

**By band:**
- **PreK/K-2:** counting objects → ten-frames/number lines → numerals; sorting/patterning with objects.
- **Upper Elem:** fraction tiles & bar models → fraction diagrams → symbolic fraction operations; area models for multiplication.
- **Middle:** algebra tiles & balance model → bar/area models → solving equations symbolically; integer chips for signed numbers.
- **High:** graphing tools, physical models, and dynamic geometry software (concrete/pictorial) before formal algebraic/analytic manipulation — CPA doesn't stop at elementary; it re-runs for each *new* abstraction.

Manipulatives are scaffolds — **fade them** as fluency grows, or they become a crutch.

## Spiral vs Mastery Sequencing

Two curriculum shapes, best understood through cognitive science rather than as rival camps:
- **Mastery:** teach a topic to depth in a block before moving on. Feels efficient and builds confidence, but *blocked* practice creates an "illusion of mastery" and fades without return.
- **Spiral:** revisit topics repeatedly, going deeper each pass. Aligns with **distributed/spaced practice** (Hattie d ≈ **0.71**), which reliably beats massed practice.

**The evidence-based synthesis:** don't pick one dogmatically.
- **Reach mastery first**, *then* space and interleave the review. Interleaving (mixing problem types) generally beats blocked practice on delayed tests and improves discrimination between problem types (Rohrer & Taylor) — but tends to help *after* initial competence, not before.
- Build in **cumulative/spiral review** (Rosenshine's principle 10, and mixed review) regardless of core curriculum shape.
- **By band:** early skills (phonics, math facts) benefit from mastery-to-fluency *then* spaced retrieval; older/complex domains benefit from deliberate interleaving of related types (e.g., mixed problem sets, cross-topic writing prompts).
- **Practical rule:** teach in coherent blocks (mastery), review on a spaced schedule (spiral), and interleave practice once the pieces are individually solid.

## Key takeaways

- **Fade the scaffold on purpose.** The systematic *withdrawal* of support — not the support itself — is what drives the gain (Gou et al. 2025, g ≈ 0.78). Withdraw first on the learner's strongest skills. [doi.org/10.1007/s40299-025-01022-9]
- **Structure delivery with Rosenshine + GRR.** Daily review → small steps + models → guided practice → check → independent practice → spaced review; move only when success is ~80%. Direct instruction ≈ 0.59 effect size. [aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf]
- **Feedback and formative assessment are top-tier levers (~0.62–0.73), but form matters** — task/process feedback beats person praise, and a third of feedback studies show harm. Use Wiliam's five strategies to make assessment change the next move. [visible-learning.org]
- **Lead with worked examples and think-alouds for novices**, then fade to completion and full problems as expertise grows (expertise-reversal effect). Add self-explanation prompts. [structural-learning.com/post/visible-learning-a-teachers-guide]
- **Ask, then wait 3–5 seconds; keep ~1 question to 2–3 comments.** Move up Bloom's from open-ended (elem) to full Socratic dialogue (high) as reasoning matures. [aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf]
- **Ground every abstraction in CPA** — concrete → pictorial → abstract, re-run for each new concept from counting to algebra; treat manipulatives as scaffolds to fade. [thirdspacelearning.com/blog/concrete-pictorial-abstract-maths-cpa/]
- **Make them guess before you tell** (errorful learning + pretesting): warm wrong guess → immediate reveal beats show-and-say; works from age 3 up, persists weeks. [sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0885201416301824]
- **Sequence with both mastery and spacing:** master to fluency, then space and interleave review (spaced practice d ≈ 0.71). Narration and copywork are retrieval practice and worked-example study in Charlotte-Mason clothing. [nwea.org/blog/2025/what-does-research-say-about-spiral-review-in-mathematics/]
