## What Homa should do first

Lead with "your child isn't the problem, the level may be wrong." That is
the cleanest emotional wedge found across homeschool, reading, parent-tech,
and education discourse.

Do not lead with "AI assistant." The current AI-learning content is noisy
and often associated with generic tech demos or school cheating. For Homa,
AI should be the invisible engine behind a very human promise: stop
guessing where your kid is.

- Primary positioning: Homa helps homeschool moms find the real level
  before choosing the next lesson.
- Emotional angle: relief, not panic: "I was teaching the wrong level."
- Proof angle: show worksheets, screen recordings, and level changes.
- Avoid: "AI built my curriculum" as the opening hook.

## The main pattern to bet on

The winning format is a discovery confession: mom thought one thing, then
realized the real problem was different. This showed up in parenting,
homeschool, reading, and education videos this week.

For Homa, the best version is not "my kid is behind." It is softer and more
trust-building: "I thought my kid hated math. Turns out I was teaching the
wrong level."

- Best first hook: I thought my kid hated math. Turns out I was teaching
  the wrong level.
- High-upside hook: Nobody warned me homeschool curriculum could skip
  entire skill gaps.
- Safe proof hook: Before I buy another workbook, I'm checking what my kid
  actually knows.

## What adjacent brands and creators are doing

### 1. Reading apps win when the child proves the result

The strongest app-style example is Reading.com-style content where the
child reads in one continuous shot. The product is not explained first. The
proof comes first: a kid reading out loud, slowly, with minimal parent
help.
https://www.tiktok.com/@reading.with.joey/video/7655328868991126815 (child proof)

This works because it feels hard to fake. The viewer sees the tablet, hears
the child sounding out words, and watches the moment happen in real time.

Another Reading.com post uses the same mechanism: "watch my 5-year-old read
this sentence with no help." The child taps each word, self-corrects, and
the parent barely intervenes.
https://www.tiktok.com/@reading.with.joey/video/7647969355539434766 (proof format)

What Homa should borrow: not "look at our app," but "watch what happened
after we changed the level."

### 2. Homeschool trust comes from visible teaching, not credentials alone

The best homeschool teaching videos show the actual lesson. A former
educator guides a child through phonics and writing, and the proof is the
child correcting his own work.
https://www.tiktok.com/@tionnateaches/video/7650358684773649695 (teaching proof)

This matters for Homa because curriculum recommendations are abstract. You
need to make the recommendation visible: old lesson too hard, Homa finds
the missing skill, new lesson fits.

### 3. Curriculum sellers build trust by acting like consultants

The homeschool supply account does something Homa should copy: it frames
curriculum recommendations as a diagnostic intake, not a product push. The
creator asks about the family, goals, number of kids, learning needs, and
then narrows the options.
https://www.tiktok.com/@thehomeschoolsupplyco/video/7659879502880787743 (consultative angle)

This is very close to Homa's product truth. Homa should feel like a calm
curriculum consultant, not a tech tool.

### 4. Parent-tech ads work when the app is inside a lifestyle story

Pok Pok's sponsored mom content did not open with app features. It opened
with "small moments of connection" and summer parental fatigue. The app
entered naturally as part of a family moment.
https://www.tiktok.com/@shawnathemom/video/7658406684024425758 (soft product entry)

For Homa, this means: don't make every video a demo. Some posts should
start with mom overwhelm: too many workbooks, not knowing what level to
teach, worrying she's missing something.

### 5. Literacy crisis is hot, but it is risky

The "kids can't read" / literacy crisis conversation is very active right
now. The NowThis video used Love Island clips as a cultural entry point,
then pivoted into systemic literacy issues.
https://www.tiktok.com/@nowthisimpact/video/7657984280215227661 (trend context)

Another recent video used a direct literacy-crisis argument around phonics
and public education.
https://www.tiktok.com/@thealyxgreen/video/7659456127934991630 (reading discourse)

Homa can use this trend, but carefully. Do not shame parents or say "your
kid is behind." The safer angle is: "Everyone's talking about literacy, but
homeschool moms need a way to check skills without guessing."

## The TikTok strategy: six pillars

### Pillar 1: the "wrong level" confession

This should be the first and most repeated pillar. It is emotional,
specific, and does not sound like an ad.

- Post this first: I thought my kid hated math. Turns out I was teaching
  the wrong level.
- Variation: My kid wasn't lazy. The lesson was just two steps ahead.
- Variation: I kept buying curriculum when I really needed a level check.

Film it as a mom talking to camera for the first 2 seconds, then cut to the
worksheet/app proof. The hook should be text on screen immediately.

Basic structure:

1. "I thought my kid hated math."
2. Show a too-hard worksheet or frustrated blank page.
3. "Then I checked her actual level."
4. Show Homa finding a missing skill.
5. Show a simpler generated lesson.
6. End: "We weren't behind. We were guessing."

### Pillar 2: screen-proof assessment reveal

This is Homa's most product-native format. The video should feel like a
reveal, not a walkthrough.

- Hook: I uploaded one worksheet and found the skill we'd been skipping.
- Hook: This is why her math lessons kept ending in tears.
- Hook: I checked her level before buying another curriculum.

Show the worksheet first, then the assessment result. Blur child details.
The viewer needs to see a real artifact, not a polished app animation.

What to show: child's worksheet or quiz with name blurred; Homa identifying
the missing skill; generated lesson at the right level; one simple next
step for tomorrow.

### Pillar 3: old curriculum vs. right-level lesson

This is the best way to make "custom curriculum" concrete.

- Hook: This workbook was technically her grade level. It still wasn't her
  level.
- Hook: Grade level and actual level are not always the same thing.
- Hook: The lesson wasn't bad. It was just the wrong next step.

Make this a split-screen: left side old workbook, right side Homa-generated
lesson. The point is not that Homa is magic; the point is that sequence
matters.

### Pillar 4: comment-based diagnostic funnel

Use comment prompts to turn TikTok into lead generation without sounding
like lead gen.

- Hook: Comment your kid's grade + hardest subject and I'll tell you what
  I'd check first.
- Hook: If math always ends in tears, comment the grade level.
- Hook: Reading, writing, or math: which one feels hardest right now?

This fits because tutoring and parent-help videos are already using
"comment the struggle" calls-to-action. Homa can then reply with short
videos: "For 3rd grade multiplication struggles, check these 3 prerequisite
skills first."

### Pillar 5: "nobody warned me" homeschool gaps

This is the highest-upside hook family. It is not yet saturated in
homeschool, but the broader parenting format is working hard right now.

- Hook: Nobody warned me homeschool curriculum could skip entire skill
  gaps.
- Hook: Nobody told me I'd need to assess my own kid before choosing
  curriculum.
- Hook: Nobody warned me that "grade level" doesn't always mean "ready."

Keep the tone confessional, not scary. Avoid "your child is behind" as the
opening line. Say "I was guessing" or "the level was off."

### Pillar 6: timely literacy / back-to-school response

Use the literacy crisis and July/August planning as context, but not as the
whole content strategy.

- Timely hook: Everyone's talking about the literacy crisis. I'm starting
  with one question: what can my kid actually read?
- Back-to-school hook: Before August curriculum, I'm checking the level,
  not just the grade.
- Reading hook: If reading feels stuck, check the missing skill before
  buying another program.

This should be 10-15% of posts, not the main feed. Trend reaction alone is
less reliable than proof and confession.

## What not to post

### Do not lead with AI

"AI built our homeschool plan" is the wrong opening. It sounds like a tech
demo, and the current AI education space is polluted by
cheating/humanizer/study-app content.

Say this instead:

- Bad: AI built our homeschool plan.
- Better: We finally stopped guessing her level.
- Better: The plan changed when we checked what she actually knew.

### Do not make generic homeschool routine content

Routine videos can work for established mom creators, but for a zero-follow
product they are too weak unless there is a strong problem/reveal. "Day in
the life of homeschool" is not enough.

### Do not fearmonger

Avoid hooks like "Your child might be years behind and you don't know it."
That may get attention, but it destroys trust with homeschool moms.

Use blame-shifting language instead:

- Not "your child is behind" → "the lesson may be ahead."
- Not "you missed this" → "most curriculum doesn't show this clearly."
- Not "AI knows better" → "Homa helps you stop guessing."

## The exact filming style

Keep it plain. Homeschool TikTok is not rewarding overproduced SaaS ads
here.

- Mom face in first second
- White/yellow text overlay
- Real worksheet or workbook
- Screen recording only after the hook
- End with a comment prompt

The strongest proof formats are slow enough to see the learning happen.
Reading.com's child-reading clips work because the viewer watches the child
sound it out in real time. Homa should create the equivalent for
benchmarking: the viewer watches the guess turn into a specific next step.

## How Homa should sound

The voice should be calm, mom-to-mom, slightly relieved. Not "AI-powered
personalized curriculum." Not "unlock your child's potential." Not
"revolutionizing homeschool."

Use these lines repeatedly:

- I didn't need more curriculum. I needed the right starting point.
- The lesson was too far ahead, so of course she was frustrated.
- Grade level tells you the label. Homa shows the next step.
- We stopped guessing and started from what she actually knew.

## Strongest recommendation

Build Homa's TikTok around diagnostic relief.

The market already has curriculum lists, homeschool hauls, phonics rants,
reading apps, and mom overwhelm content. The gap is that almost nobody is
owning the moment before curriculum: "Where is my kid actually at?"

That is Homa's wedge. Own that question before you talk about AI,
curriculum generation, or features.
