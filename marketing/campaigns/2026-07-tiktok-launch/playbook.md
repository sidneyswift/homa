## The wedge

Homa helps homeschool moms find the real level before choosing the next
lesson. Lead with "your child isn't the problem, the level may be wrong."
The market has curriculum lists, hauls, phonics rants, reading apps, and
mom overwhelm content; almost nobody owns the question before curriculum:
"where is my kid actually at?" Own that question before mentioning AI,
materials generation, or any feature.
(Signals: `2026-07-08-own-the-moment-before-curriculum`, `2026-07-08-dont-lead-with-ai`.)

## Craft laws for every post

1. Never lead with AI. AI is the invisible engine behind a human promise:
   we stopped guessing her level. "AI built our homeschool plan" is banned
   as an opener (and reads like the cheating/demo content polluting the
   space).
2. Confession over demo. The winning format is discovery confession: mom
   thought one thing ("she hates math"), found the real problem was
   different (the level). The moment a post becomes a walkthrough,
   engagement dies.
3. Proof is artifacts, never children. Reading apps win by filming the
   child; Homa never puts a member child's face, name, or voice in
   marketing (journeys/07 §6.7). Our proof: the worksheet with details
   blurred, the level check on screen, the next-step materials, slow
   enough to watch the guess turn into a specific next step.
4. Blame the lesson, never the child, never the mom. "The lesson may be
   ahead", "most curriculum doesn't show this clearly", "Homa helps you
   stop guessing". No fear hooks, ever; they buy views and burn trust.
5. Plain over produced. Mom face in the first second, text hook on screen
   immediately, real worksheet or workbook in frame, screen recording only
   after the hook, end on a comment prompt. Homeschool TikTok punishes
   SaaS-ad polish.

## The six pillars

### 1 · The wrong-level confession (lead pillar, most repeated)

Signal: `2026-07-08-the-wrong-level-confession`.

- Post first: "I thought my kid hated math. Turns out I was teaching the
  wrong level."
- "My kid wasn't lazy. The lesson was just two steps ahead."
- "I kept buying curriculum when I really needed a level check."

Beat structure: (1) confession to camera, (2) the too-hard worksheet or
blank page, (3) "then I checked her actual level", (4) the level check
finding the missing skill, (5) simpler materials that fit, (6) close: "It
was never her. It was the level."

### 2 · The level-check reveal (most product-native)

Signal: `2026-07-08-proof-is-watching-the-level-change`. Feels like a
reveal, never a walkthrough. Artifact first, result second.

- "This is why her math lessons kept ending in tears."
- "I checked her level before buying another curriculum."
- "One worksheet showed the skill we'd been skipping." (waits on the
  worksheet-upload flow; see claims guardrails)

Show: the worksheet with details blurred, the level check on screen, the
right-level next step, one simple thing to try tomorrow.

### 3 · Old workbook vs right-level lesson (split screen)

Signal: `2026-07-08-grade-level-is-the-label-not-the-level`.

- "This workbook was technically her grade level. It still wasn't her
  level."
- "Grade level and actual level are not always the same thing."
- "The lesson wasn't bad. It was just the wrong next step."

The point is sequence, not magic.

### 4 · The comment funnel (engagement + reply loop)

Signal: `2026-07-08-comment-the-grade-and-subject-funnel`.

- "Comment your kid's grade + hardest subject and I'll tell you what I'd
  check first."
- "If math always ends in tears, comment the grade level."
- "Reading, writing, or math: which one feels hardest right now?"

Every comment seeds a reply video ("for 3rd grade multiplication
struggles, check these 3 prerequisite skills first"). This pillar's CTA is
the comment itself; the reply videos carry the thankshoma.com pointer.

### 5 · "Nobody warned me" gaps (highest upside)

Signal: `2026-07-08-nobody-warned-me-skill-gaps`.

- "Nobody warned me homeschool curriculum could skip entire skill gaps."
- "Nobody told me I'd need to check the level myself before choosing
  materials."
- "Nobody warned me that 'grade level' doesn't always mean 'ready'."

Confessional, never scary. The speaker was guessing; the level was off.

### 6 · Timely: literacy talk + back-to-school (10-15% of posts, max)

Signals: `2026-07-08-literacy-crisis-enter-gently`,
`2026-07-08-before-august-check-the-level`.

- "Everyone's talking about the literacy crisis. I'm starting with one
  question: what can my kid actually read?"
- "Before August curriculum, check the level, not just the grade."
- "If reading feels stuck, check the missing skill before buying another
  program."

## Claims guardrails (binding, per journeys/07 §6 and house style)

- Product-true today: the free level checklist at thankshoma.com/onboarding
  (questions for the parent, never a test for the child; national
  benchmarks; free), the waitlist. Film these freely and truthfully.
- Not live yet, so not filmable as product proof: worksheet/photo upload
  that finds a missing skill, generated right-level materials, the weekly
  plan. Until each ships, those posts run in educator voice ("what I'd
  check first") with no product claims, or wait. Never fabricate an app
  screen (precedent: the rejected takes logged in
  `content/drafts/2026-07-05-ugc-homa-intro/video/script.md`).
- Never: outcome promises, aggregate efficacy claims, "behind"/"catch up"
  framing, sibling comparisons, clinical words (say "level check", never
  "assessment" or "diagnostic"), a member child on camera, invented
  testimonials. UGC presenters introduce the product in their own words;
  they never fake usage history or results.
