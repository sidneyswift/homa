# State homeschool assessment requirements, all 50 states

> Historical legal-research snapshot, 2026-07-06. This file preserves the evidence and analysis captured on that date. It is not current legal, provider, product, or production guidance. Re-verify every jurisdiction against primary sources before use.

**Not legal advice. Product surfaces re-verify statutes before display, per repo law.**

**Date:** 2026-07-06
**Lane:** State assessments 16, part of the ages 2 to 16 national benchmark corpus (see README.md in this folder). Supersedes lane 10 section 7's starter table. Not legal advice; product re-verifies before display.
**Method:** HSLDA's per-state legal pages (hslda.org/legal and its "how to comply" compliance articles) used as the maintained index compilation; threshold states verified against fetched statute text (ORS 339.035, Va. Code 22.1-254.1, WV Code 18-8-1, NDCC 15.1-23, F.S. 1002.41) or state education department pages (NYSED, TN DOE, OSSE, AR DESE) where fetchable; Coalition for Responsible Home Education's March 2026 policy overview fetched as the labeled opposite-viewpoint cross-check. Every cell was transcribed from a fetched page; unverified cells say "not verified this pass".

**Key findings:**

- All 51 jurisdictions (50 states plus DC) are covered; every cell traces to a page fetched 2026-07-06. 26 jurisdictions attach SOME compulsory assessment artifact (test, evaluation, portfolio, or maintained evaluation records) to their default homeschool route; 25 attach none. The count reconciles exactly with CRHE's independent pro-oversight tally of 29 states with no evaluation requirement (section 6 cross-check).
- Only 8 states write a numeric cut line into law or regulation: North Dakota (30th percentile trigger, 50th monitoring), Oregon (15th), Colorado (13th), Virginia and West Virginia (4th stanine, the 23rd percentile), New York (33rd percentile or one year's growth), Minnesota (30th percentile or one grade below age-peers), Iowa (above 30th percentile per subject plus a growth condition, test route only). Florida's statute has NO percentile: lane 10's unconfirmed "35th percentile" line does not appear in F.S. 1002.41.
- Thresholds are written exclusively in percentiles and stanines against national norms, never scaled scores, and most attach to a COMPOSITE score, not subject subscores (NY's regulation-backed guidance says subscores "should not be considered").
- Consequences escalate gently almost everywhere: retest, remediation plan, probation year. Forced school enrollment is the endpoint only in Colorado (below 13th twice), Oregon (sustained decline), Virginia (probation fails), North Dakota (no remediation plan filed), Tennessee (2 consecutive years 1+ grade behind), and Iowa (2 failed assessments without approved remediation).
- Umbrella and cover school routes (Alabama church schools, Tennessee CRS, Maryland umbrellas, Florida private school enrollment, Texas private school posture, Washington private school extensions, Maine REPS, Iowa IPI) let families in many "assessment" states opt out of state-run scrutiny entirely; the umbrella sets its own testing policy.
- Deregulation is the recent direction: Ohio abolished its assessment requirement (Oct 2023 rewrite), Vermont stopped collecting year-end assessments (July 2023), Arkansas repealed testing (2015), Nebraska eliminated dormant test-demand powers (2025), South Dakota dropped testing (2021). Counter-current bills to add regulation appeared in Illinois (HB 2827, failed 2025) and Connecticut (HB 5468/SB 6, pending as of this pass).

## 2. Threshold states in detail

Confidence: High for ND, OR, VA, WV, FL (statute text fetched); Medium-high for CO, NY, MN, TN, IA (state education department or HSLDA compliance text fetched, statute itself not fetched this pass). All quotes below are transcribed from pages fetched 2026-07-06.

### North Dakota: 30th percentile trigger, 50th monitoring line

FACT, quoted from [NDCC 15.1-23-11](https://ndlegis.gov/cencode/t15-1c23.pdf) (accessed 2026-07-06): "If the child's basic composite score on a standardized achievement test is less than the thirtieth percentile nationally, a multidisciplinary assessment team shall assess the child for a potential learning problem under rules adopted by the superintendent of public instruction." If no disability is found, the parent "with the advice and consent of an individual who is licensed to teach... shall prepare a remediation plan"; per 15.1-23-12 the plan stays in effect "until such time as the child achieves on a standardized achievement test a basic composite score at or above the thirtieth percentile or a score, which when compared to the previous year's test score, demonstrates one year of academic progress." Failing to file a plan means the parent "may no longer supervise the home education of the child."

FACT, the 50th percentile line, quoted from 15.1-23-06 (same source): a parent without a diploma/GED "must be monitored... for the first two years. If a child receiving home education obtains a basic composite standardized achievement test score below the fiftieth percentile nationally, the parent must be monitored for at least one additional school year and until the child receives a test score at or above the fiftieth percentile." Testing itself: grades 4, 6, 8, 10, ND-licensed teacher administers, scores filed with the district superintendent (15.1-23-09 and -11), with exemptions for philosophical/moral/religious objection or parent credentials (teacher license, bachelor's degree, national teacher exam cutoff).

### Oregon: 15th percentile, the slowest escalation ladder

FACT, quoted from [ORS 339.035(4)](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_339.035) (accessed 2026-07-06): "If the composite test score of the child places the child below the 15th percentile based on national norms, the child shall be given an additional examination within one year." A declining second score lets the education service district superintendent place the child "under the supervision of a person holding a teaching license... at the expense of the parent"; continued decline lets the superintendent "order the parent or legal guardian to send the child to school for a period not to exceed 12 consecutive months." Tests at grades 3, 5, 8, 10 from a state-approved list, administered by a "qualified neutral person"; results go to the ESD only "upon request of the superintendent." Children with disabilities on an IEP or privately developed plan are assessed per the plan instead (subsection 5).

### Virginia: fourth stanine composite (23rd percentile)

FACT, quoted from [Va. Code 22.1-254.1(C)](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title22.1/section22.1-254.1/) (accessed 2026-07-06): the parent must provide by August 1 "(i) evidence that the child has attained a composite score in or above the fourth stanine on any nationally normed standardized achievement test, or an equivalent score on the ACT, SAT, or PSAT test or (ii) an evaluation or assessment which the division superintendent determines to indicate that the child is achieving an adequate level of educational growth and progress," including an evaluation letter from any state-licensed teacher or master's-degree holder, or a report card/transcript from a college or correspondence program. If evidence is not provided, "the home instruction program for that child may be placed on probation for one year" with a remediation plan; if that fails, "home instruction shall cease." Children under 6 as of September 30 are exempt. Subsection G bars the superintendent from disclosing the data outside the school division. (Stanine 4 begins at the 23rd percentile; see lane 10 section 6.)

### West Virginia: fourth stanine mean, or year-over-year improvement

FACT, quoted from [WV Code 18-8-1(c)(2)(C)(i)](https://code.wvlegislature.gov/18-8-1/) (accessed 2026-07-06): "The child is considered to have made acceptable progress when the mean of the child's test results in the required subject areas for any single year is within or above the fourth stanine or, if below the fourth stanine, shows improvement from the previous year's results." Test must be "published or normed not more than 10 years from the date of administration" and covers reading, language, mathematics, science, social studies. Alternatives: the state testing program at a public school, a certified-teacher portfolio review with written narrative, or a mutually agreed alternative. Results are submitted at grades 3, 5, 8, and 11 by June 30 (18-8-1(c)(2)(E)); after two consecutive years without acceptable progress the parent "shall submit to the county superintendent additional evidence that appropriate instruction is being provided." The 2021-era learning pod and microschool exemptions (18-8-1(n)) carry the identical fourth-stanine language.

### Colorado: 13th percentile, the hardest cliff

FACT, from [HSLDA's Colorado compliance page](https://hslda.org/post/how-to-comply-with-colorados-homeschool-law) (accessed 2026-07-06), describing C.R.S. 22-33-104.5: "If a child does not score above the 13th percentile on a nationally standardized achievement test, he or she can be given an alternate version of the same test or a different nationally standardized achievement test. If the score is still below the 13th percentile, the school district will require the parent to place the child in a public, independent, or parochial school until the next testing period." Testing or qualified evaluation in grades 3, 5, 7, 9, 11; results go to the notified district OR to any independent/parochial school in Colorado (a well-known softening: private schools receive the score instead of the district). Statute text itself not fetched this pass (justia blocked); flagged in dry wells.

### New York: 33rd percentile or one year of growth

FACT, quoted from the [NYSED home instruction Q&A](https://www.nysed.gov/nonpublic-schools/home-instruction-questions-and-answers) (accessed 2026-07-06), interpreting CR 100.10(h): "If parents submit test scores for an achievement test, the program will be placed on probation only if the composite score of the student is below the thirty-third percentile on national norms or the score fails to reflect one academic year of growth when compared to a prior test. The student's score on individual test subscores should not be considered in determining whether the program should be placed on probation." The apparatus around it: annual IHIP (individualized home instruction plan), quarterly reports, and an annual assessment; a written narrative evaluation may substitute for the test every other year in grades 4 to 8 ("Thus, grade 4 could be the first year in this pattern"). Probation brings a remediation plan and permits home visits on three days' notice. Failure to submit any evaluation at all can be reported "to the central registry as a case of suspected educational neglect." The often-cited "RQ" (required quarterly) reports are the subdivision (g) quarterly reports; the test-vs-narrative alternation above is the regulation's cadence for upper grades.

### Minnesota: 30th percentile, evaluation-only consequence

FACT, from [HSLDA's Minnesota compliance page](https://hslda.org/post/how-to-comply-with-minnesotas-homeschool-law) (accessed 2026-07-06): "You are required to test your child annually using a nationally norm-referenced standardized achievement test. The test and the testing location must be agreed on by you and the district superintendent. If your child scores at or below the 30th percentile or one full grade below children of the same age, you must have your child evaluated for learning difficulties." Results stay with the parent (recordkeeping duty, discoverable by a county prosecutor); accredited homeschools are exempt from testing entirely.

### Tennessee: grade-level lag test, not a percentile

FACT, from the [TN DOE independent home school requirements sheet](https://www.tn.gov/content/dam/tn/education/nonpublic/Independent_Home_School_Requirements.pdf) (accessed 2026-07-06): "If the student performs one (1) year or more below grade level on two (2) consecutive tests, the superintendent may require parents or legal guardians to enroll the student in a public or private school. If the test indicates that a home school student is one (1) year or more behind their appropriate grade level, the same test shall be administered not more than one (1) year later." Independent home school students take the same state-board-approved secure tests as public school students (TCAP) in grades 5, 7, and 9; per [TCA 49-6-3050 via FindLaw](https://codes.findlaw.com/tn/title-49-education/tn-code-sect-49-6-3050/) (accessed 2026-07-06), church-related school enrollees "are exempt from this section."

### Iowa: 30th percentile per subject plus a growth condition (test route only)

FACT, from [HSLDA's Iowa compliance page](https://hslda.org/post/how-to-comply-with-iowas-homeschool-law) (accessed 2026-07-06): under the annual-assessment choice, "To show adequate progress, your child needs to have a score above the 30th percentile in each required test subject area plus either (a) show an overall score at grade level or (b) show six months' progress from the previously submitted test." The first assessment is an explicit no-stakes baseline. Two failed assessments require school enrollment "unless the Iowa Department of Education approves a remediation program." Families using IPI or opt-out routes face no assessment at all.

### Hawaii, Pennsylvania, Washington, Florida: mandatory artifact, no numeric line

FACT (sources in the master table, all accessed 2026-07-06): Hawaii requires a test at grades 3, 5, 8, 10 that "demonstrates grade-level achievement appropriate to the child's age" but sets no percentile. Pennsylvania requires tests in grades 3, 5, 8 inside an evaluator-certified portfolio; the standard is the evaluator's certification of "sustained progress." Washington requires an annual test or certificated-person assessment whose results stay in the family's records. Florida requires an annual evaluation (5 parent-selected methods) against a "level commensurate with her or his ability" standard; F.S. 1002.41 contains no percentile, and failure triggers a 1-year probation.

## 5. Statutory changes 2024 to 2026 (and the tail of 2023)

Confidence: Medium-high. Each item from a fetched page (access date 2026-07-06); effective dates as stated by the source.

- Ohio, effective October 2023: complete rewrite. Homeschooling moved to a notice-based exemption under new ORC 3321.042; the old assessment/evaluation regime became "obsolete" and there are now "no teacher qualifications, number of hours, or assessment requirements" ([HSLDA OH](https://hslda.org/post/how-to-comply-with-ohios-homeschool-law)). The single largest recent removal of a homeschool assessment mandate.
- Vermont, effective July 1, 2023: 16 V.S.A. 166b amended; enrollment notices no longer include a minimum course of study or "evidence of academic progress for the previous school year"; the "completeness" review and hearing powers were repealed. Year-end assessment survives but stays with the parent ([HSLDA VT](https://hslda.org/post/how-to-comply-with-vermonts-homeschool-law)).
- Nebraska, signed mid-April, effective July (year per HSLDA news, article undated on page; the at-a-glance page also lists LB 1112/LB 1224 coverage): eliminated the education department's dormant powers to "arbitrarily demand that homeschool students take achievement tests," demand parent competency tests, inspect homes, and review curriculum ([HSLDA news](https://hslda.org/post/nebraska-sees-biggest-change-to-homeschool-law-since-1984)). Exact bill number and effective year not verified this pass.
- West Virginia: the Hope Scholarship program (18-31) and learning pod/microschool exemptions (18-8-1(m), (n)) are now embedded in the compulsory attendance statute; pods and microschools carry the same fourth-stanine assessment menu as notice-based homeschooling ([WV Code 18-8-1](https://code.wvlegislature.gov/18-8-1/)). HSLDA's WV news feed headlines "Homeschool Freedom Takes a Hit after Wave of Bad Legislation" (2025 era); specifics not fetched this pass.
- Tennessee, 2024: H.B. 1729 / S.B. 2636 signed into law per HSLDA's TN page news feed ("Another Victory for Tennessee Homeschoolers: H.B. 1729 Signed into Law!"); content not fetched this pass, flagged as a dry well since it may touch testing logistics.
- Arkansas (context, pre-window): Act 832 of 2015 repealed home school testing; the Education Freedom Account program (2023 era LEARNS Act) reintroduces testing as a CONDITION OF FUNDING only ([AR DESE fact sheet](https://dese.ade.arkansas.gov/Files/AR_Home_School_Fact_Sheet_2024_OSCPE.pdf), [Homeschool Start Guide](https://homeschoolstartguide.com/blog/arkansas-homeschool-testing-requirements)).
- Regulation-adding attempts that failed or are pending: Illinois HB 2827 (registration/records mandate) declared "officially dead" in the 2025 session per HSLDA's IL news feed; Connecticut HB 5468 and SB 6 (2025 to 2026 sessions) would add oversight in the last no-notice New England state, status pending as of this pass.

INFERENCE: ESA/voucher-style funding programs (WV Hope, AR EFA, and peers) are becoming the main NEW vector for homeschool testing mandates: the state buys the right to demand data. Homa should expect testing obligations increasingly tied to funding enrollment rather than to homeschool status itself.

## 6. What this means for Homa

Derivations below use only this file's master table (jurisdiction counts) and are INFERENCE where extrapolated.

FACT (count derived from the master table): 26 of 51 jurisdictions attach some compulsory assessment artifact to their default homeschool route: submission-to-authority states (ND, OR on request, VA, WV, CO, FL, NY, HI, PA, SC option 1, ME, TN independent, IA assessment route, LA home study, MD portfolio, DC portfolio, MA/RI district-dependent) plus keep-private-but-mandatory states (WA, NC, GA, MN, NH, VT, MO records, KS periodic).

FACT (count derived from the master table): 8 jurisdictions put a numeric line in law (ND, OR, CO, VA, WV, NY, MN, IA); 2 more use a grade-level-lag rule (TN; MN has both forms).

INFERENCE (derivation shown, population figures are approximate 2020 census values from general knowledge, not fetched this pass, re-verify before quoting): the 26 artifact-requiring jurisdictions include 7 of the 10 most populous states (FL, NY, PA, NC, GA, VA, WA among them; TX, CA, IL require nothing), so roughly half the US population lives under SOME homeschool assessment artifact rule. But homeschoolers concentrate in low-regulation states, and umbrella routes (section 4) drain the mandate pools in FL, TN, MD, AL, SC, so the share of US homeschoolers actually bound to produce a normed test score for the state is much smaller: plausibly on the order of a tenth to a quarter of homeschooling families, not half. A citable homeschool-per-state count (Washington Post index or JHU Homeschool Hub) was not fetched this pass; without it this range is soft. Logged as a dry well.

INFERENCE (segment mapping): the families for whom Homa's benchmark layer is compliance-critical, not just reassuring, are (a) the numeric-threshold states, especially VA and WV (composite must clear the 4th stanine yearly or trigger paperwork) and ND (testing plus filing at 4 fixed grades), (b) NY families using the test years (33rd percentile line plus quarterly report cadence), (c) TN independent home schools (TCAP at grades 5, 7, 9 with a real enrollment consequence), and (d) IA test-route families (per-subject 30th percentile plus growth). Everywhere else the artifact is either private to the family or evaluator-mediated.

### Cross-check: Coalition for Responsible Home Education

FACT (labeled cross-check; CRHE is a pro-oversight advocacy organization founded by homeschool alumni, the counterweight viewpoint to HSLDA): CRHE's policy overview, last updated March 2026, states "More than half of US states (29) do not require that homeschooled students be evaluated for educational progress at any point in their schooling" and sorts states into four tiers: 12 no oversight, 17 minimal oversight (notification only), 19 mixed oversight ("some provisions in place to ensure homeschooled kids are assessed for progress, but these are not consistently enforced"), and 2 moderate oversight. Source: [CRHE, Inside Homeschooling Policy](https://responsiblehomeschooling.org/policy-issues/current-policy/) (accessed 2026-07-06).

FACT (reconciliation, derivation shown): CRHE's 29-of-50 no-evaluation count matches this file's table. This lane counts 26 of 51 jurisdictions with SOME compulsory artifact; subtract DC (not a state in CRHE's 50) and the four artifact-without-review jurisdictions this lane counts but CRHE's "evaluated for educational progress" framing would not (MO maintained records never reviewed, KS parent-kept periodic testing, MA and RI district-discretion regimes) and you get 21 states requiring evaluation, i.e. 29 states without: exact agreement from independent compilations with opposite advocacy postures. That convergence raises confidence in the master table's yes/no column.
