# World languages, computer science, and digital literacy benchmarks, ages 2 to 16

**Date:** 2026-07-06
**Lane:** Languages, CS, and digital 09, part of the ages 2 to 16 national benchmark corpus (see README.md in this folder)
**Method:** Web research against primary standards bodies (actfl.org, csteachers.org, iste.org, commonsense.org, UNESCO, ai4k12.org, state departments of education) and peer-reviewed second-language-acquisition sources; every number was verified against a fetched page and carries an inline link with access date. Grade-to-month anchors use the folder's end-of-grade convention and are marked as convention, not fact.

**Key findings:**

- ACTFL Proficiency Guidelines 2024 is the current edition: five major levels (Novice, Intermediate, Advanced, Superior, Distinguished), with Low/Mid/High sublevels for the first three, 11 named ratings. Nearly the entire K-12 journey happens inside Novice and Intermediate.
- Published outcomes are humbling: after 4 years of traditional high school study, the majority of students land at Novice High to Intermediate Low (CASLS 2010), and only about 15% get near Intermediate Mid even after roughly 720 hours. The Seal of Biliteracy bar (most commonly Intermediate Mid) deliberately sits one rung above the typical 4-year outcome.
- Early childhood L2 research supports Homa's exposure-based Spanish design: Tabors's four stages (home language, silent period lasting weeks to months, formulaic chunks like greetings and counting, then productive use), vocabulary growth roughly proportional to exposure share, and code-switching as social competence rather than confusion.
- CSTA K-12 CS Standards Revised 2017 (levels 1A K-2, 1B 3-5, 2 6-8, 3A 9-10, five concepts) is still the published edition, but the 2026 PK-12 revision (renamed concepts, four new pillars, AI and five other high school specialty areas) launches at the CSTA conference in July 2026, days after this file's date: re-verify before porting.
- The CS tool ladder is consistent across publishers: ScratchJr ages 5 to 7, Scratch 8 to 16, Code.org CS Fundamentals K-5, text languages entering in grades 6 to 8, AP CS Principles at grades 9-12 with only algebra 1 recommended.
- Digital citizenship has an unusually clean scope: Common Sense Education covers six topics with one lesson per topic at every grade K-12; for AI literacy, UNESCO's 2024 framework defines 12 competency blocks (4 aspects x 3 mastery levels) and AI4K12 defines five big ideas across four grade bands.
- Classical homeschool Latin conventions are citable: gentle intro possible from grade 2, beginning formal Latin at grades 3 to 6, systematic grammar (First Form style) from about grade 5.
- CS has no shipped Homa domain; this lane flags it as the strongest candidate new domain, with 1A/1B/2/3A giving ready-made band anchors.

## 1. ACTFL proficiency guidelines: the scale and sublevels

**Framework edition:** ACTFL Proficiency Guidelines 2024, the current edition as of access date. Editions: 1986 original, 1999 revision, 2012 revision (Distinguished level added), 2024 revision launched after a process begun in 2022 ([ACTFL guidelines history](https://www.actfl.org/use-of-actfl-proficiency-guidelines-and-issuing-of-official-actfl-tests), accessed 2026-07-06). Overview: [ACTFL Proficiency Guidelines Overview](https://www.actfl.org/proficiency-guidelines-overview) (accessed 2026-07-06). Full text consulted via a state-hosted copy of the official 2024 PDF ([Oregon DOE mirror](https://www.oregon.gov/ode/students-and-family/equity/EngLearners/Documents/ACTFL-Proficiency-Guidelines-2024.pdf), accessed 2026-07-06).

**Scale structure (FACT):** five major levels: Novice, Intermediate, Advanced, Superior, Distinguished. Novice, Intermediate, and Advanced are each subdivided into Low, Mid, and High, giving 11 named ratings. The guidelines describe proficiency separately in four domains (listening, speaking, reading, writing), and each rating is defined by four criteria with the acronym FACT: Functions and tasks, Accuracy, Context and content, Text type ([ACTFL overview](https://www.actfl.org/proficiency-guidelines-overview), accessed 2026-07-06).

**What the sublevels mean (FACT, quoted framing):** Low is "baseline performance at the level", Mid is "solid performance at the level" with partial ability at the next level, High is "performance with quality and quantity at the level", functioning much of the time at the next major level but unable to sustain it ([ACTFL 2024 PDF](https://www.oregon.gov/ode/students-and-family/equity/EngLearners/Documents/ACTFL-Proficiency-Guidelines-2024.pdf), accessed 2026-07-06).

### Level descriptions through intermediate high (speaking domain, 2024 text, paraphrased close to source)

Speaking is the domain most relevant to Homa's exposure-based Spanish tracking. All rows sourced to the [ACTFL 2024 guidelines PDF](https://www.oregon.gov/ode/students-and-family/equity/EngLearners/Documents/ACTFL-Proficiency-Guidelines-2024.pdf) (accessed 2026-07-06).

| Level | Plain description of a speaker at this level |
|---|---|
| Novice Low | Beginning to learn basics; communicates with rehearsed common words and phrases. Given time and familiar cues, can exchange greetings, give identity, name familiar objects. Other-language patterns may interfere with communication |
| Novice Mid | Completes basic social communication with isolated words, rehearsed phrases, short sentences from contexts where the language was learned. Uses stock answers to direct questions, pauses often, recycles the partner's words to keep going |
| Novice High | Manages a number of uncomplicated tasks in straightforward social situations. Mostly short, sometimes incomplete present-tense sentences on familiar survival topics (basic personal info, objects, preferences, immediate needs). Answers simple direct questions, asks a few formulaic ones. Language resembles creating-with-language at times but leans on rehearsed material |
| Intermediate Low | Handles a limited number of uncomplicated tasks by using language creatively in straightforward social situations. Concrete, predictable survival topics (self, family, some daily activities, ordering food, simple purchases). Asks a few appropriate questions but is primarily reactive. Frequent hesitation, repetition, self-correction, but generally understood |
| Intermediate Mid | Handles a variety of uncomplicated tasks in social and transactional situations: personal information (self, family, home, daily activities, interests, preferences) and needs (food, shopping, travel, lodging). Asks a variety of questions to get information. Speech is strings of sentences with pauses and reformulations; generally understood and sometimes self-repairs miscommunication |
| Intermediate High | Converses with ease and confidence on routine tasks and social situations about work, school, recreation, interests. Handles a substantial number of Advanced-level tasks (narrating and describing across major time frames in paragraph-length connected discourse) but cannot sustain them all of the time without breakdown |

### Major levels above intermediate (one-line summaries, 2024 text)

| Level | Summary |
|---|---|
| Advanced | Engages fully in everyday conversation and routine work-related interactions; narrates and describes in past, present, and future; paragraph-length connected discourse; deals with unpredictable social situations |
| Superior | Discusses issues abstractly, supports opinions, hypothesizes; extended cohesive discourse without lengthy hesitation; no patterns of error in basic structures |
| Distinguished | Uses language skillfully with accuracy and efficiency on global issues and highly abstract concepts; advises, persuades, negotiates; tailors language and register to audiences; oral discourse resembles written discourse |

Source: [ACTFL 2024 guidelines PDF](https://www.oregon.gov/ode/students-and-family/equity/EngLearners/Documents/ACTFL-Proficiency-Guidelines-2024.pdf) (accessed 2026-07-06).

**INFERENCE for product framing:** the entire K-12 journey for most US learners plays out inside Novice and Intermediate; Advanced and above are top-of-ladder headroom, not expectations. This inference follows directly from the outcome targets in section 3.

**Confidence: High** (primary source, full 2024 text captured).

## 2. NCSSFL-ACTFL can-do statements: structure

**Framework edition:** NCSSFL-ACTFL Can-Do Statements, 2026 revision, which updates the 2017 edition and aligns with the ACTFL Proficiency Guidelines 2024 and the ACTFL Performance Descriptors for Language Learners. Edition history: original 2013 (NCSSFL LinguaFolio "I can" statements combined with ACTFL Guidelines 2012), 2017 update (added sample learning scenarios and Intercultural Can-Do Statements), 2026 revision. Sources: [ACTFL can-do statements page](https://www.actfl.org/educator-resources/ncssfl-actfl-can-do-statements) (accessed 2026-07-06) and the [Can-Do introduction and overview PDF](https://www.actfl.org/uploads/files/general/Professional-Learning/Can-Do-Intro-Overview.pdf) (accessed 2026-07-06).

**Structure (FACT):**

| Component | What it is |
|---|---|
| Three modes of communication | Interpretive (understand, interpret, analyze what is heard, read, or viewed), Interpersonal (interact and negotiate meaning in spoken, signed, or written conversation), Presentational (present information, concepts, ideas to inform, explain, persuade, narrate) |
| Proficiency benchmarks | Overarching "I can" goals for each mode at each of the five major levels (Novice through Distinguished); support long-term goal setting and program outcomes |
| Performance indicators | Unpack each benchmark by sublevel (low, mid, high) into three core elements: Function (what the learner can do), Context (the situations), Text type (length and complexity of language understood or produced) |
| Example scenarios | Sample learning scenarios per sublevel showing the indicator in a real task |
| Intercultural can-do statements | Describe developing Intercultural Communicative Competence via Investigate and Interact statements, with an Intercultural Reflection Tool |

Sources: [ACTFL can-do page](https://www.actfl.org/educator-resources/ncssfl-actfl-can-do-statements) and [Can-Do intro PDF](https://www.actfl.org/uploads/files/general/Professional-Learning/Can-Do-Intro-Overview.pdf), both accessed 2026-07-06.

**Worked example of sublevel progression (FACT, presentational mode, function element):** Novice High "I can express my preferences", Intermediate Low "I can express my preferences and opinions and tell why I feel that way", Intermediate Mid "I can state my viewpoint and give some reasons to support it", Intermediate High "I can state my viewpoint and give reasons to support it, often across various time frames" ([ACTFL can-do page](https://www.actfl.org/educator-resources/ncssfl-actfl-can-do-statements), accessed 2026-07-06).

**Why this matters for Homa (INFERENCE):** the can-do "I can" grain (parent-observable behaviors like "can exchange greetings", "can name familiar objects") is the same grain as Homa's map items, which makes the novice-range can-dos the natural source language for Spanish exposure-ladder steps.

**Confidence: High** (primary source pages and PDFs).

## 3. Expected proficiency outcomes by program model and years of study

### 3a. Years of high school study to proficiency (research-based)

**Primary research (FACT):** CASLS (Center for Applied Second Language Studies, University of Oregon), 2010 STAMP-based studies. "The majority of students studying a foreign language in a traditional high school program reach benchmark level 3 or 4 by end of the fourth year of study, regardless of the language studied. These levels are similar to the ACTFL levels Novice-High and Intermediate-Low" ([CASLS, What proficiency level do high school students achieve?](https://casls.uoregon.edu/wp-content/uploads/pdfs/tenquestions/TBQProficiencyResults.pdf), accessed 2026-07-06). Sample excluded home speakers and immersion students. Also (FACT): "only about 15% of students reach a proficiency level near Intermediate-Mid even after approximately 720 hours of study, which is about four years in a typical high school program" ([CASLS, hours to Intermediate-High](https://casls.uoregon.edu/wp-content/uploads/pdfs/tenquestions/TBQHoursToReachIH.pdf), accessed 2026-07-06).

**State guidance chart built on that research (FACT):** Minnesota Department of Education, credit-for-proficiency guidelines ([MN DOE guidelines](https://education.mn.gov/mde/dse/el/PROD069684), accessed 2026-07-06):

| Years of sequential study (course level) | Expected overall proficiency outcome | Anchor months if taken as HS levels 1 to 4 (convention: level 1 ends grade 9 = 180mo) |
|---|---|---|
| End of year 1 (level 1) | Novice Low to Novice Mid | 180 |
| End of year 2 (level 2) | Novice Mid to Novice High | 192 |
| End of year 3 (level 3) | Novice High to Intermediate Low | 204 (above scored range top) |
| End of year 4 (level 4) | Intermediate Low to Intermediate Mid | above range |
| End of years 5 to 6 | Intermediate Mid to Intermediate High | above range |
| Longer sequences such as K-12 | Intermediate High to Advanced | n/a |

Note the anchor-months column is a convention this lane added, not in the source; world language proficiency is a function of hours, not age, so months anchors are only meaningful for the default start-in-grade-9 sequence.

**CASLS year-by-year distributions (FACT, STAMP reading, percent of students at each CASLS benchmark level):** Year 1: 53.1% level 1, 30.0% level 2, 13.9% level 3, 2.6% level 4, 0.4% level 5+. Year 4: 9.4% level 1, 23.4% level 2, 32.1% level 3, 22.6% level 4, 12.5% level 5+ ([CASLS proficiency results PDF](https://casls.uoregon.edu/wp-content/uploads/pdfs/tenquestions/TBQProficiencyResults.pdf), accessed 2026-07-06). CASLS also reports students progress faster in speaking and writing than reading, and that high school students with three years of study are at about the level of university students with one year ([CASLS burning questions](https://casls.uoregon.edu/research/language-research-questions/), accessed 2026-07-06).

### 3b. K-12 grade-band targets in a state framework (New Jersey, statute-backed)

**Framework edition:** 2020 New Jersey Student Learning Standards for World Languages (NJSLS-WL), benchmarked by proficiency level rather than grade, with grade bands attached ([NJ DOE world languages page](https://www.nj.gov/education/standards/worldlang/) and the [2020 NJSLS-WL PDF](https://www.nj.gov/education/standards/worldlang/Docs/2020NJSLS-WL.pdf), both accessed 2026-07-06).

| NJ benchmark | For a K-12 sequence, expected by end of | Anchor months (convention) | If language started in middle school | If started in high school |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Novice Low | Grade 2 | 96 | n/a | n/a |
| Novice Mid | Grade 5 | 132 | Grade 8 | Grade 12 |
| Novice High | Grade 8 | 168 | Grade 12 | Grade 12 |
| Intermediate Low | Grade 12 | n/a (above scored range) | n/a | longer sequence |
| Intermediate Mid, High, Advanced Low | Grade 12, for longer or intensive sequences | n/a | n/a | n/a |

(FACT) NJ requires Novice High proficiency to satisfy its world language graduation requirement, and cites research that "programs offering a minimum of 540 hours of articulated instruction in classes that meet at least three times a week throughout the academic year produce a majority of students who can speak at the Novice-High proficiency level or higher" ([2020 NJSLS-WL PDF](https://www.nj.gov/education/standards/worldlang/Docs/2020NJSLS-WL.pdf), accessed 2026-07-06).

### 3c. Program-level end goals (Ohio)

(FACT) Ohio's Learning Standards for World Languages and Cultures (adopted March 2020, model curriculum revised 2025) span the first seven sublevels (Novice Low through Advanced Low) and "define a cumulative progression designed to enable learners to meet Intermediate High/Advanced Low by the end of high school in a vertically aligned, well-articulated language program." Proficiency targets by hours and program model are recommended, not mandated ([Ohio standards page](https://education.ohio.gov/Topics/Learning-in-Ohio/World-Languages-and-Cultures/Ohio-s-Learning-Standards-for-World-Languages-and) and the [standards grid PDF](https://education.ohio.gov/getattachment/Topics/Learning-in-Ohio/Foreign-Language/WLC-Standards-Grid_11x17_April-01-2020.pdf.aspx?lang=en-US), both accessed 2026-07-06).

(FACT) Ohio publishes classical-language (Latin, ancient Greek) proficiency targets by course level with cumulative hours: Level I is 135 to 150 hours, II 270 to 300, III 405 to 450, IV 540 to 600, V 675 to 750, VI 825 to 900. The presentational speaking row runs Novice Low (Level I), Novice Mid (II), Novice High (III), Novice High (IV), Intermediate Low (V), Intermediate Low (VI); interpretive reading is the lead skill, starting at Novice High for Level I and reaching about Advanced Low by Level VI ([Ohio Latin and classical language resources](https://education.ohio.gov/Topics/Learning-in-Ohio/World-Languages-and-Cultures/Language-Specific-Resources/Latin-and-Other-Classical-Language-Resources), accessed 2026-07-06; the reading-row endpoint is read from a linearized table, see dry wells).

### 3d. FLES and elementary program expectations

(FACT, from the sources above rather than a dedicated FLES study): NJ's K-12 articulated band targets (Novice Low by grade 2, Novice Mid by grade 5) are the clearest published expectation for elementary FLES-style programs. CASLS found eighth graders with 540 hours of instruction (a strong K-8 FLES sequence) score somewhat lower than third-year high school students with similar hours, and "most students in U.S. programs do not reach proficiency levels that allow them to effectively communicate in the language" ([CASLS K-12 vs university comparison](https://casls.uoregon.edu/wp-content/uploads/pdfs/tenquestions/TBQLevelofLanguage.pdf), accessed 2026-07-06). Immersion is the exception: CASLS reports immersion students frequently reach Intermediate or Advanced by grade 12 while less than half of traditional students do ([CASLS burning questions](https://casls.uoregon.edu/research/language-research-questions/), accessed 2026-07-06).

**INFERENCE for Homa:** the commonly repeated teacher folklore "Novice Mid to Novice High after level 1" is slightly optimistic against this evidence base; Novice Low to Novice Mid after year 1 (MN chart) and Novice High only after 2 to 3 years is the defensible published expectation. Weekly-exposure homeschool Spanish (well below 135 hours per year) should expect slower movement than all of these school-based tables, which is why Homa's spanish domain tracks exposure rather than proficiency-per-year.

**Confidence: High** for the tables quoted (state and CASLS primary sources); **Med** for the Ohio classical reading-row endpoint (linearized table).

### 3e. Cross-check: what the Seal pipeline implies

(INFERENCE, derivation shown): if 4 years of high school study typically ends at Intermediate Low, and most state Seals of Biliteracy require Intermediate Mid (section 5), then the Seal is deliberately set about one sublevel above the typical 4-year outcome, which matches CASLS's finding that only about 15% reach near Intermediate Mid in that time.

## 4. Early bilingual development, ages 2 to 10, and exposure milestones

Homa's spanish domain is exposure-based: it tracks what a child has been exposed to and can do, never a months-ahead or months-behind number. This section captures what the research says exposure-based growth looks like.

### 4a. Stages of sequential (child L2) acquisition (FACT)

Tabors (1997), the standard citation, observed four early stages in children encountering a new language, quoted via [NALDIC's summary of Tabors, p. 39](https://www.naldic.org.uk/Resources/NALDIC/Initial%20Teacher%20Education/Documents/Stagesofearlybilinguallearning.pdf) (accessed 2026-07-06) and Paradis's handbook chapter [Second Language Acquisition in Childhood](https://sites.ualberta.ca/~jparadis/Johanne_Paradis_Homepage/Publications_files/P_CSLA_Handbook.pdf) (accessed 2026-07-06):

| Stage | What it looks like | Duration notes (FACT, Paradis) |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Home-language use | Child uses the home language in the new-language setting | Very brief, often days |
| 2. Non-verbal (silent) period | Few or no utterances in the new language; gestures; actively listening and rehearsing | "Can last a few weeks or extend to several months, and younger children seem to stay longer in this stage than older ones" (Tabors 1997; Winitz et al. 1995) |
| 3. Formulaic and telegraphic use | Memorized chunks and short unanalyzed phrases; first utterances "mainly single-words like object and color names, or counting sequences" (Tabors 1997) | Overlapping, gradual |
| 4. Productive language use | Novel combinations of words and grammar; interlanguage that moves toward the target system | Ongoing |

**Receptive before productive (FACT):** the silent period is a normal receptive-buildup phase, not a stall; comprehension precedes and exceeds production throughout early L2 learning ([NALDIC/Tabors](https://www.naldic.org.uk/Resources/NALDIC/Initial%20Teacher%20Education/Documents/Stagesofearlybilinguallearning.pdf), accessed 2026-07-06).

### 4b. Vocabulary growth under bilingual exposure (FACT)

| Finding | Source |
|---|---|
| Bilingual toddlers (8 to 30 months) show no slower total lexical development than monolinguals when vocabulary is counted across both languages (conceptual vocabulary); single-language norms underestimate them | Pearson, Fernandez, and Oller 1993, [Lexical development in bilingual infants and toddlers](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-1770.1993.tb00174.x) (accessed 2026-07-06) |
| Early on, children learn vocabulary roughly in proportion to their exposure, "even if that exposure is as little as 20% of their input" | Hoff and Core 2015, [What Clinicians Need to Know about Bilingual Development, Semin Speech Lang 36(2):89-99](https://www.thieme-connect.com/products/ejournals/html/10.1055/s-0035-1549104) (accessed 2026-07-06) |
| About 60% exposure to a language was needed for bilingual 2.5-year-olds to score like monolingual peers on monolingual-normed tests | Cattani et al. 2014, [How much exposure to English is necessary for a bilingual toddler to perform like a monolingual peer?](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1043969) (accessed 2026-07-06) |
| A tentative threshold of roughly 20 to 30% of weekly child-directed exposure supports full grammatical acquisition; below about 20%, acquisition still happens but is delayed and unlikely to reach native-like competence | Meisel, Bilingual Children (Cambridge University Press), [chapter 6](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/bilingual-children/trilingual-and-more/37AF84AB30D856845F9410E61C42EC61) (accessed 2026-07-06) |
| Code-switching (mixing languages within a conversation or sentence) does not mean failure to differentiate languages; bilingual children show awareness of which listeners understand which language and adjust socially | Hoff and Core 2015, [same article](https://www.thieme-connect.com/products/ejournals/html/10.1055/s-0035-1549104) (accessed 2026-07-06) |

**INFERENCE for Homa's weekly-exposure model:** a homeschool Spanish routine of a few sessions per week sits far below the 20% exposure share where research expects native-like acquisition; the honest expectation is receptive-first, formulaic production (greetings, counting, colors, commands), and vocabulary proportional to exposure. That is exactly why the domain tracks exposure steps, not age norms.

### 4c. What exposure milestones look like (exposure ladder, INFERENCE grounded in FACTs above)

Ladder steps below are this lane's synthesis: each step's behavior type is grounded in a cited source (Tabors stages, the NCSSFL-ACTFL novice can-do grain, and the CDC-style parent-observable framing), but the ordering into a single ladder is Homa's, and no months anchors apply.

| Ladder step | Observable behavior | Grounding |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Attends | Attends to songs or read-alouds in Spanish, shows engagement without producing | Tabors stage 2 (non-verbal period), receptive buildup |
| 2. Responds nonverbally | Follows a simple command with gesture support (ven, mira, dame) | Tabors stage 2; TPR-style comprehension precedes speech |
| 3. Recognizes | Points to named colors, animals, body parts; recognizes greetings | Receptive vocabulary in proportion to exposure (Hoff and Core 2015) |
| 4. Produces formulas | Says greetings and courtesy phrases (hola, gracias); sings memorized song chunks | Tabors stage 3, formulaic use |
| 5. Counts and labels | Counts to 10; names colors and familiar objects | Tabors 1997: first utterances are often object and color names or counting sequences |
| 6. Answers | Answers simple direct questions with single words or stock phrases | ACTFL Novice Low to Mid can-do grain (section 1) |
| 7. Asks and combines | Asks a few formulaic questions; recombines chunks into two-to-four-word utterances | Tabors stage 4 onset; ACTFL Novice Mid to High |
| 8. Code-switches socially | Chooses language by listener; mixes languages inside sentences while staying understood | Hoff and Core 2015, listener-aware language choice |
| 9. Converses simply | Short present-tense exchanges on self, family, likes; creates with language | ACTFL Intermediate Low description (section 1) |

**Confidence: High** for 4a and 4b (peer-reviewed and professional-body sources); **Med** for 4c (synthesis, clearly labeled).

## 5. Seal of Biliteracy: the top-of-ladder reference

**What it is (FACT):** a state-conferred award recognizing high school graduates who attain proficiency in English and at least one other world language, noted on the diploma or transcript. All human languages qualify, including Latin and ASL. Nearly all states plus DC have adopted one (an LTI review counts 35+ states plus DC as of its 2019-era snapshot, with adoption continuing since) ([LTI state-by-state requirements](https://www.languagetesting.com/blog/state-by-state-requirements-seal-of-biliteracy/), accessed 2026-07-06; [South Carolina Seal guidelines](https://ed.sc.gov/index.cfm?LinkServID=7F4AF1DC-B846-FA0E-C6AD87D1C73B714D), accessed 2026-07-06).

**The proficiency bar (FACT):** per the joint ACTFL/NABE/NCSSFL/TESOL implementation guidelines, "The level of proficiency currently required by most states ranges from Intermediate Low to Advanced Low according to the ACTFL Proficiency Guidelines, with Intermediate Mid and Intermediate High as the most common level. Some states have established multiple levels for the seal. The actual standard is determined independently by each state" ([Guidelines for implementing the Seal of Biliteracy, actfl.org](https://www.actfl.org/uploads/files/general/SOBL_Guidelines.pdf), accessed 2026-07-06). LTI's state review states most requirements "range between Intermediate Mid and Intermediate High" ([LTI](https://www.languagetesting.com/blog/state-by-state-requirements-seal-of-biliteracy/), accessed 2026-07-06).

**Tiered-state examples (FACT):**

| State | Tiers and bars |
|---|---|
| South Carolina | Bronze = Intermediate Mid, Silver = Intermediate High, Gold = Advanced Low, all four modes ([SC guidelines](https://ed.sc.gov/index.cfm?LinkServID=7F4AF1DC-B846-FA0E-C6AD87D1C73B714D), accessed 2026-07-06) |
| Rhode Island | Silver = Intermediate Mid or High, Gold = Advanced Low or above ([RI council designations PDF](https://ride.ri.gov/sites/g/files/xkgbur806/files/2024-12/Council-Designations-Seal-of-Biliteracy-RI%20--%20Dec%202024.pdf), accessed 2026-07-06) |
| Minnesota | World Language Proficiency Certificate = Intermediate Low, Bilingual Gold Seal = Intermediate High, Platinum = Advanced Low, in all four skills ([MN DOE](https://education.mn.gov/mde/dse/el/PROD069684), accessed 2026-07-06) |
| Ohio | Seal aligned to Intermediate High ([Ohio standards grid](https://education.ohio.gov/getattachment/Topics/Learning-in-Ohio/Foreign-Language/WLC-Standards-Grid_11x17_April-01-2020.pdf.aspx?lang=en-US), accessed 2026-07-06) |

**Ladder position (INFERENCE):** Intermediate Mid in all four skills, around the end of high school, is the sensible "top rung" reference for any Homa language ladder; it is above what most 4-year course-takers reach (section 3a) and is the most common Seal floor.

**Confidence: High.**

## 6. CSTA K-12 computer science standards

**Framework edition (FACT):** CSTA K-12 Computer Science Standards, Revised 2017, still the published edition as of access date. A comprehensive revision is at Draft 3.0 (December 2025) with official launch of the 2026 CSTA PK-12 Standards scheduled at the CSTA Annual Conference in July 2026, days after this file's date ([csteachers.org standards page](https://csteachers.org/k12standards/) and [revision page](https://csteachers.org/k12standards/revision/), both accessed 2026-07-06). Re-verify this section after the July 2026 launch.

### 6a. 2017 structure (FACT)

Levels: 1A (grades K-2), 1B (grades 3-5), 2 (grades 6-8), 3A (grades 9-10) are "the computer science standards for all students"; 3B (grades 9-12) is for students pursuing CS beyond the core, in specialty or elective courses. Five concepts: Computing Systems, Networks and the Internet, Data and Analysis, Algorithms and Programming, Impacts of Computing. Seven practices (Fostering an Inclusive Computing Culture, Collaborating around Computing, Recognizing and Defining Computational Problems, Developing and Using Abstractions, Creating Computational Artifacts, Testing and Refining Computational Artifacts, Communicating about Computing). Identifier format: level-concept-number, for example 3A-IC-25 ([csteachers.org](https://csteachers.org/k12standards/), accessed 2026-07-06).

### 6b. Representative 2017 standards per level across the five concepts (FACT)

All standards text quoted from the CSTA K-12 Computer Science Standards, Revised 2017 (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0), via a [Connecticut SDE-hosted copy of the official document](https://portal.ct.gov/-/media/sde/cte/csta-k12-computer-science-standards-revised-2017.pdf) (accessed 2026-07-06). End-of-band anchor months per the folder convention: 1A ends grade 2 = 96mo, 1B ends grade 5 = 132mo, level 2 ends grade 8 = 168mo, 3A ends grade 10 = 192mo (convention, not in source).

| Level (band, anchor) | Concept | Identifier | Standard |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1A (K-2, 96mo) | Computing systems | 1A-CS-01 | Select and operate appropriate software to perform a variety of tasks |
| 1A | Computing systems | 1A-CS-03 | Describe basic hardware and software problems using accurate terminology |

## 9. Digital citizenship and media literacy scope by grade band

### 9a. Common Sense Education digital citizenship curriculum (FACT)

**Framework edition:** Common Sense Education K-12 Digital Citizenship Curriculum, six-topic structure introduced with the 2018-2019 revision (developed with Project Zero, Harvard Graduate School of Education) and still current in the June 2024 impact report. ### 9b. NAMLE media literacy framework (FACT)

**Framework edition:** NAMLE Core Principles of Media Literacy Education, 2023 revision (10 principles). NAMLE defines media literacy as "the ability to access, analyze, evaluate, create, and act using all forms of communication," and media literacy education as "the ongoing development of habits of inquiry and skills of expression necessary for people to be critical thinkers, thoughtful and effective communicators, and informed and responsible members of society" ([NAMLE media literacy defined](https://namle.org/resources/media-literacy-defined/) and [core principles](https://namle.org/resources/core-principles/), both accessed 2026-07-06). **Confidence: High** for both frameworks.
