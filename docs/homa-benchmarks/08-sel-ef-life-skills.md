# Social-emotional, executive function, and life skills benchmarks, ages 2 to 16

**Date:** 2026-07-06
**Lane:** SEL, EF, and life skills 08, part of the ages 2 to 16 national benchmark corpus (see README.md in this folder)
**Method:** Web-sourced from primary and near-primary references (CASEL, Illinois State Board of Education SEL PDFs, Harvard Center on the Developing Child, Dawson and Guare, AAP HealthyChildren, AASM, US DOL, the federal Child Welfare Information Gateway, and peer-reviewed developmental literature). Every factual claim carries an inline link and access date; nothing is recalled from memory, and unverifiable numbers are logged in "Dry wells" rather than guessed.

**Key findings:**

- CASEL (2020 wheel) defines the five competencies but deliberately publishes no graded-by-age progression; the Illinois SEL standards (2004, CASEL-assisted) are the canonical graded operationalization, captured here as a full 3-goal, 10-standard, 5-band grid (20 benchmark threads across 5 grade bands, 100 cells).
- Executive function has a two-surge shape (dramatic growth at ages 3 to 5, second remodeling across adolescence into the mid 20s), meaning EF is under construction across the entire 2 to 16 range (Harvard Center on the Developing Child).
- The popular "2 to 3 minutes per year of age" attention-span rule is unsourced folklore; attention is task-dependent and no defensible minutes-by-age norm was found. The shipped bank's minute-based focus items should be reframed or re-anchored.
- Life-skill and self-care ages are well sourced (AAP chores, pediatric OT dressing/hygiene, dietitian cooking progressions, CCSS money placement), giving clean parent-observable anchors.
- Only three states set a statutory home-alone minimum age (Maryland 8, Oregon 10, Illinois 14); most states judge case by case. Legal readiness ages (home-alone, first phone, FLSA work at 14, learner's permit 14 to 16) are facts but not developmental benchmarks and should stay out of the scored map.
- Classic developmental frameworks (Selman friendship, Hoffman empathy, Kohlberg/Damon moral, Erikson identity, Piaget cognition) are captured with ages and modern caveats; Piaget and Erikson are orientation only, not anchor sources.

## 1. CASEL framework: the five competencies

**Framework edition:** CASEL framework ("the CASEL wheel", "the CASEL 5"), 2020 update, as published on [casel.org, What Is the CASEL Framework?](https://casel.org/fundamentals-of-sel/what-is-the-casel-framework/) (accessed 2026-07-06). All definitions and example skills below are quoted or closely paraphrased from that page.

**CASEL's definition of SEL (FACT):** "SEL is the process through which all young people and adults acquire and apply the knowledge, skills, and attitudes to develop healthy identities, manage emotions and achieve personal and collective goals, feel and show empathy for others, establish and maintain supportive relationships, and make responsible and caring decisions."

**Important framing (FACT):** CASEL states the CASEL 5 "can be taught and applied at various developmental stages from childhood to adulthood" but does not itself publish a graded, per-age progression; it explicitly delegates that to states and districts ("Many school districts, states, and countries have used the CASEL 5 to establish preschool to high school learning standards"). That is why section 2 uses the Illinois standards as the graded operationalization.

| Competency | CASEL definition (2020) | Example skills listed by CASEL |
|---|---|---|
| Self-awareness | "The abilities to understand one's own emotions, thoughts, and values and how they influence behavior across contexts. This includes capacities to recognize one's strengths and limitations with a well-grounded sense of confidence and purpose." | Identifying one's emotions; linking feelings, values, and thoughts; identifying personal, cultural, and linguistic assets; demonstrating honesty and integrity; experiencing self-efficacy; having a growth mindset; developing interests and a sense of purpose |
| Self-management | "The abilities to manage one's emotions, thoughts, and behaviors effectively in different situations and to achieve goals and aspirations. This includes the capacities to delay gratification, manage stress, and feel motivation and agency to accomplish personal and collective goals." | Managing one's emotions; stress-management strategies; self-discipline and self-motivation; goal setting; planning and organizational skills; courage to take initiative; personal and collective agency |
| Social awareness | "The abilities to understand the perspectives of and empathize with others, including those from diverse backgrounds, cultures, and contexts. This includes the capacities to feel compassion for others, understand broader historical and social norms for behavior in different settings, and recognize family, school, and community resources and supports." | Taking others' perspectives; recognizing strengths in others; demonstrating empathy and compassion; showing concern for others' feelings; gratitude; identifying diverse social norms; recognizing situational demands and opportunities |
| Relationship skills | "The abilities to establish and maintain healthy and supportive relationships and to effectively navigate settings with diverse individuals and groups." Includes clear communication, active listening, cooperation, collaborative problem solving, constructive conflict negotiation, leadership, and seeking or offering help. | Communicating effectively; developing positive relationships; cultural competency; teamwork and collaborative problem-solving; resolving conflicts constructively; resisting negative social pressure; leadership; seeking or offering support; standing up for the rights of others |
| Responsible decision-making | "The abilities to make caring and constructive choices about personal behavior and social interactions across diverse situations. This includes the capacities to consider ethical standards and safety concerns, and to evaluate the benefits and consequences of various actions for personal, social, and collective well-being." | Curiosity and open-mindedness; making reasoned judgments after analyzing information; identifying solutions for personal and social problems; anticipating and evaluating consequences of one's actions; reflecting on one's role in personal, family, and community well-being |

CASEL also frames four key settings where the competencies develop (classrooms, schools, families and caregivers, communities), with families described as "children's first teachers" (FACT, same source). This is the hook Homa's parent-facing framing can lean on: the framework itself treats home as a primary SEL setting.

**Confidence: High** (primary source, full text captured).

(9-10, anchor 192mo), Late H.S. (11-12, anchor 216mo, beyond the product's scored range top of 204mo).

## 3. Developmental social-emotional milestones, ages 2 to 16

This section anchors the classic developmental-psychology progressions that sit underneath any SEL standard. Ages here are research consensus ranges, not test norms; individual variation is wide. Lane 01 carries the CDC 2022 social-emotional checklist items for 24 to 60 months; this section picks up the constructs and carries them to 16.

### 3.1 Emotion regulation: from co-regulation to self-regulation

| Age window | What develops | FACT/INFERENCE | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 to 24mo | Regulation is largely interpersonal: caregivers soothe, structure, and modify the environment (co-regulation). Kopp's foundational account frames self-regulation as emerging from caregiver-managed regulation | FACT (framework) | [Kopp 1982, Antecedents of self-regulation, Developmental Psychology 18(2):199-214](https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.18.2.199), cited via [Montroy et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0000159) (accessed 2026-07-06) |
| 24 to 60mo | Strategy shift measured longitudinally in delay tasks: self-soothing declines, bids to adults rise then decline, self-distraction rises; growth in distraction use predicts being rated well-regulated by age 5 | FACT | [Bailey, Denham et al. 2021, Longitudinal changes in young children's strategy use for emotion regulation, Developmental Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0001235) (accessed 2026-07-06); converging toddler evidence ages 2 to 4 in [Supplee et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1080/00221325.2010.510545) (accessed 2026-07-06) |
| 36 to 84mo (3 to 7y) | Most children show rapid growth in behavioral self-regulation across preschool to grade 1; three distinct timing trajectories (earlier vs later rapid gains) distinguished by gender, early language, and maternal education | FACT | [Montroy, Bowles, Skibbe, McClelland, Morrison 2016, The development of self-regulation across early childhood, Developmental Psychology 52(11)](https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0000159) (accessed 2026-07-06) |
| 48 to 72mo (4 to 6y) | "Reflective emotion regulation" consolidates: children begin planning and selecting regulation strategies; caregiver emotion talk and strategy scaffolding drive the gradual co-regulation to self-regulation handoff | FACT (model with empirical support) | [Internalization model of reflective emotion regulation, Journal of Self-Regulation and Regulation (Heidelberg)](https://journals.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/index.php/josar/article/download/34351/28034/106000) (accessed 2026-07-06), building on Kopp 1989 and Holodynski 2013 |
| 72mo to 16y | Regulation becomes increasingly covert and cognitive (reappraisal, self-talk); adolescence brings a second calibration period as reactivity rises before top-down control matures (see section 4 EF arc) | INFERENCE (bridge summary consistent with sources in 3.1 and 4.1) | see section 4 sources |

### 3.2 Theory of mind: the false-belief landmark

**FACT:** the canonical meta-analysis (178 study conditions) found false-belief performance moves from systematically below chance to above chance across the preschool years, with the crossover in the 4 to 5 year window (48 to 60mo); the pattern held across countries and task variants, supporting genuine conceptual change rather than task artifact. Source: [Wellman, Cross, and Watson 2001, "Meta-Analysis of Theory-of-Mind Development: The Truth about False Belief", Child Development 72(3):655-684](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8624.00304) (accessed 2026-07-06).

**Practical anchors (FACT per same source):** 3-year-olds systematically fail standard false-belief tasks; task manipulations (deception framing, child participation) shift performance but do not erase the age effect. INFERENCE for map use: "understands someone can believe something that is not true" anchors cleanly at 54 to 60mo.

### 3.3 Empathy: Hoffman's developmental stages

Framework: Martin Hoffman's stage model of empathic distress (as consolidated in his 2000 book Empathy and Moral Development). Stage list and ages below follow the summary in [Schaffer, Social Development (1996), as excerpted by the California Kindergarten Association](https://californiakindergartenassociation.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/09/Hoffmans-4-Levels-of-Empathy.pdf) (accessed 2026-07-06) and the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Empathy"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/sum2019/entries/empathy/) (accessed 2026-07-06).

| Stage | Typical age | Description (FACT, quoted or close paraphrase) |
|---|---|---|
| Global empathy (emotional contagion) | First year | Infant matches witnessed emotion involuntarily, e.g. crying at another infant's cry; unclear who is in pain |
| Egocentric empathic distress | Second year (12 to 24mo) | Knows the other is in pain but offers the comfort the child itself would want (e.g. fetching own mother to comfort a crying friend whose mother is present) |
| Empathy for another's feelings (veridical) | Third year onward (24 to 36mo+) | With role-taking, becomes aware others' feelings differ from one's own; help becomes appropriate to the other's needs |
| Empathy for another's life condition | Late childhood to early adolescence (about 10 to 14y) | Empathy extends beyond the immediate situation to another's lasting circumstances, and to whole groups (the poor, the oppressed) |

### 3.4 Friendship: Selman's five stages

Framework: Robert Selman's stages of friendship understanding (Selman 1980, The Growth of Interpersonal Understanding). Ages below per [Open Education Resource: Child and Adolescent Developmental Psychology, "Friendships, Peers, and Peer Groups"](https://openfl.pressbooks.pub/childandadol/chapter/friendships-peers-and-peer-groups/) (accessed 2026-07-06), cross-checked against [Psychology Today, Eileen Kennedy-Moore's summary of Selman](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/growing-friendships/201202/childrens-growing-friendships) (accessed 2026-07-06). Age bands overlap by design: they are ranges within which the stage is typical, not cut points.

| Stage | Name | Typical ages | Definition of a friend at this stage (FACT) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Momentary playmates (momentary physical interaction) | 3 to 6 | Someone you are playing with right now; based on proximity and convenience |
| 1 | One-way assistance | 5 to 9 | Someone who does nice things for you; child does not weigh own contribution |
| 2 | Fair-weather cooperation (two-way) | 7 to 12 | Someone who returns favors; strong fairness and reciprocity concern, friendships end over score-keeping |
| 3 | Intimate, mutually shared relationships | 8 to 15 | Someone you can tell things you would tell no one else; caring beyond score-keeping, trust violations end friendships, can be possessive |
| 4 | Autonomous interdependence (mature friendship) | 12 and up | Someone who accepts you and whom you accept as they are; tolerates the friend's other relationships, survives separations |

**INFERENCE for map anchors:** stage transitions worth anchoring are stage 2 entry near 84 to 96mo (fairness-based friendship) and stage 3 entry near 108 to 132mo (confiding friendship), with stage 4 marked from 144mo up.

### 3.5 Moral development landmarks: Kohlberg and Damon in brief

**Kohlberg (FACT):** three levels, six stages; preconventional morality (punishment avoidance, then instrumental exchange) is typical of children up to about age 9; conventional morality (good-boy/good-girl approval seeking, then law-and-order) is typical of adolescents and most adults; postconventional reasoning is rare and, where present, emerges from adolescence onward. Sources: [Lifespan Development: A Psychological Perspective (open textbook), section 5.12](https://opentextbooks.concordia.ca/lifespandevelopment/chapter/5-12-kohlbergs-stages-of-moral-development/) (accessed 2026-07-06); age table per [Verywell Mind, Kohlberg's Theory of Moral Development](https://www.verywellmind.com/kohlbergs-theory-of-moral-development-2795071) (accessed 2026-07-06). Standard caveats (FACT, same sources): stage timing varies, reasoning is context-sensitive, and Gilligan's care-orientation critique applies.

**Damon distributive justice (FACT):** working with children ages 3 to 9 sharing resources, Damon documented a progression from egocentric claims (want it all, no justification), through self-serving justifications, to a strict equality criterion (everyone gets the same), then merit (more for those who worked harder), then need/necessity weighing, approaching adult-like coordination of equality, merit, and need by about age 8 to 9. Source: [Wikipedia summary of Damon's levels within the Kohlberg stages article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Kohlberg%27s_stages_of_moral_development) (accessed 2026-07-06); primary work is Damon, The Social World of the Child (1977), not directly fetched (see dry wells).

| Stage | Ages (typical published range) | Crisis | Virtue if resolved | What a parent observes (INFERENCE, derived from stage descriptions) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 1 to 3 | Autonomy vs shame and doubt | Will | Insists on doing things "by myself", practices choices |
| 3 | 3 to 6 | Initiative vs guilt | Purpose | Initiates play and projects, asks endless questions, takes small risks |

**Confidence: High** for 3.2 (meta-analytic), 3.4, 3.6 (stable canonical frameworks with primary-adjacent sources); **Med** for 3.1 (active research area, ages are windows not norms), 3.3 and 3.5 (frameworks captured via secondary but reputable academic sources, primary texts not fetched).

## 4. Executive function development

### 4.1 The Harvard Center on the Developing Child arc

**Framework edition:** Center on the Developing Child at Harvard University, InBrief: Executive Function (2012) and Working Paper 11, "Building the Brain's Air Traffic Control System" (2011), both current on developingchild.harvard.edu as of access date.

| Claim | FACT/INFERENCE | Source |
|---|---|---|
| EF is a skill set relying on three brain functions: working memory, mental flexibility, and self-control ("air traffic control system" metaphor) | FACT | [InBrief: Executive Function](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/resources/inbriefs/inbrief-executive-function/) (accessed 2026-07-06) |
| "Children aren't born with these skills, they are born with the potential to develop them" | FACT (quote) | same InBrief (accessed 2026-07-06) |
| Test batteries show EF skills "begin to develop shortly after birth, with ages 3 to 5 a window of opportunity for dramatic growth in these skills. Development continues throughout adolescence and early adulthood," with proficiency declining in later life; the working paper's figure attributes the curve to Weintraub et al. | FACT (quote) | [InBrief PDF](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/InBrief-Executive-Function-Skills-for-Life-and-Learning-2.pdf) and [Working Paper 11 PDF](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/How-Early-Experiences-Shape-the-Development-of-Executive-Function.pdf) (both accessed 2026-07-06) |
| By age 5 children manage rule-switching games ("if it's the color game, put the red square here, but if it's the shape game..."), resist touching desirable objects, execute multi-step plans, and play inhibition games like Simon Says and Red Light Green Light; by age 7 some EF skills are "remarkably similar to those found in adults" | FACT | [Working Paper 11 PDF](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/How-Early-Experiences-Shape-the-Development-of-Executive-Function.pdf) (accessed 2026-07-06) |
| EF weakness in young children is usually protracted prefrontal development, not willful misbehavior; skill-building plus adjusted demands beat punishment | FACT | [What is Executive Function? infographic page, "Correcting Popular Misconceptions"](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/resources/infographics/what-is-executive-function-and-how-does-it-relate-to-child-development/) (accessed 2026-07-06) |
| Adolescence is a second period of significant EF/prefrontal remodeling (synaptic reorganization at puberty, then pruning), with measurable improvement in selective attention, decision-making, inhibition, and multitasking during the teen years; full maturation extends into the mid 20s | FACT | [Blakemore and Choudhury 2006, "Development of the adolescent brain: implications for executive function and social cognition", Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry 47(3-4)](https://acamh.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1469-7610.2006.01611.x) (accessed 2026-07-06); Harvard's framing of adolescent refinement in [From Best Practices to Breakthrough Impacts (2016)](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/From_Best_Practices_to_Breakthrough_Impacts-4.pdf) (accessed 2026-07-06) |

**The two-surge shape (FACT, synthesized from the above):** EF has a first dramatic growth window at ages 3 to 5 (36 to 60mo), reaches near-adult form for some skills by age 7 (84mo), then a second remodeling and refinement surge across adolescence (roughly 144mo up) that continues into the mid 20s. This is the single most important developmental fact for a 2-to-16 product: EF is under construction across the entire scored range.

### 4.2 Dawson and Guare executive skills (Smart but Scattered)

**Framework edition:** Peg Dawson and Richard Guare's 11 executive skills, as defined in Smart but Scattered and Executive Skills in Children and Adolescents (2nd ed. 2010, Guilford). Definitions and the young-child vs teenager illustrations below are quoted from Dawson's [Tier 1 Intervention guide, Table 1](https://www.smartbutscatteredkids.com/wp-content/uploads/Tier-1-Intervention.pdf) (accessed 2026-07-06). The skills are listed in the order the authors believe they emerge developmentally.

| Executive skill | Definition (FACT, quoted) | Young child looks like | Teenager looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| Response inhibition | "The capacity to think before you act" | Waits a short period without being disruptive | Accepts a referee's call without arguing |
| Working memory | "The ability to hold information in memory while performing complex tasks" | Holds and follows 1 to 2 step directions | Remembers the expectations of multiple teachers |
| Emotional control | "The ability to manage emotions in order to achieve goals, complete tasks, or control and direct behavior" | Recovers from a disappointment quickly | Manages test or game anxiety and still performs |
| Sustained attention | "The capacity to maintain attention to a situation or task in spite of distractibility, fatigue, or boredom" | Completes a 5-minute chore with occasional supervision | Attends to homework, with short breaks, for 1 to 2 hours |
| Task initiation | "The ability to begin projects without undue procrastination, in an efficient or timely fashion" | Starts a chore or assignment right after instructions | Does not wait until the last minute to begin a project |
| Planning/prioritizing | "The ability to create a roadmap to reach a goal or to complete a task" plus deciding what matters | With coaching, thinks of options to settle a peer conflict | Formulates a plan to get a job |
| Organization | "The ability to create and maintain systems to keep track of information or materials" | With a reminder, puts toys in a designated place | Organizes and locates sports equipment |
| Time management | "The capacity to estimate how much time one has, how to allocate it, and how to stay within time limits and deadlines" | Completes a short job within an adult-set time limit | Establishes a schedule to meet task deadlines |

## 5. Attention span by age: what the research supports

**Bottom line (FACT-checked):** the popular "2 to 3 minutes per year of age" attention-span rule is a widely circulated pedagogical rule of thumb with no identifiable peer-reviewed origin. It is repeated as fact in teaching outlets but is not traceable to a specific study, and attention researchers say "average attention span" is close to meaningless because attention is task-, interest-, and context-dependent. Homa should not anchor any product number to this rule.

| Claim in the wild | Verdict | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| "Children's attention span is about 2 to 3 minutes per year of age" | Rule of thumb, unsourced | Stated as if research-backed by an educator outlet ([Edutopia](https://www.edutopia.org/article/encouraging-young-children-develop-their-attention-skills/), accessed 2026-07-06) but with no primary citation; framed explicitly as "a good rule of thumb" not a finding |
| Students have a fixed 10 to 15 minute lecture attention span | Not supported | [Wilson and Korn 2007, "Attention During Lectures: Beyond Ten Minutes", Teaching of Psychology 34(2)](https://www.opencolleges.edu.au/blogs/articles/the-science-of-attention-how-to-capture-and-hold-the-attention-of-easily-distracted-students) summary (accessed 2026-07-06): evidence for the 10 to 15 min figure is "shallow and imprecise"; no consistent average was ever established |
| Human attention spans are shrinking / now shorter than a goldfish (8 seconds) | Debunked | [BBC News, "Busting the attention span myth" (2017)](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/health-38896790) (accessed 2026-07-06): traced to a 2015 Microsoft Canada report citing "Statistic Brain", whose own cited sources (NCBI, Associated Press) could not confirm the figures; researchers reject the premise |
| Attention is task-dependent, not a fixed personal length | Supported | Same BBC source quoting attention researchers; "how much attention we apply to a task will vary depending on what the task demand is" |

**What can be said honestly (FACT/INFERENCE):**

- Sustained attention improves with age and is under prefrontal control that matures into the mid 20s (see section 4.1, Harvard).
- Young children sustain far longer on preferred/self-chosen activities than on adult-directed non-preferred tasks; short focus on imposed tasks is developmentally normal, not a deficit (widely stated by clinicians; the strongest primary anchor is the EF developmental arc in section 4, not an attention-span-minutes table).
- A defensible product stance: report engagement and task persistence behaviorally ("stayed with a self-chosen task", "needed a movement break") rather than asserting a minutes-per-age norm.

**Confidence: High** on the debunk (multiple independent sources agree the specific numbers are unsourced); **Low** on any positive minutes-by-age table, which is why none is anchored here.

Dry-well note: a defensible per-age minutes table would need a normed continuous-performance-test dataset (e.g. Conners CPT, TOVA, or the NIH Toolbox attention measures). Not located in free primary form this pass; logged for a follow-up.

## 7. Staying home alone: legal landscape and guidance

**Key FACT:** only three US states set a statutory minimum age for leaving a child home alone. Most states classify inadequate supervision as neglect but give no age, deciding case by case on the child's maturity, the length of absence, and the environment. Authoritative maintained compilation: the federal Child Welfare Information Gateway factsheet [Leaving Your Child Home Alone](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-HE23_1200-PURL-gpo139637/pdf/GOVPUB-HE23_1200-PURL-gpo139637.pdf) (US Children's Bureau, accessed 2026-07-06). State-by-state guidance ages below from [TODAY's 50-state guide](https://www.today.com/parents/family/when-can-kids-stay-home-alone-rcna172938) (accessed 2026-07-06), which polled each state agency.

### 7.1 Statutory minimum ages (the only three)

| State | Statutory minimum age | What the law says (FACT) | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Maryland | 8 | Crime to leave a child under 8 unattended/confined without proper supervision; a reliable person at least 13 must supervise (Md. Code Family Law 5-801) | Child Welfare Gateway factsheet; [MD People's Law Library](https://www.peoples-law.org/unattended-children) (accessed 2026-07-06) |
| Oregon | 10 | A child under 10 may not be left alone "for a period that could endanger their welfare" | Child Welfare Gateway factsheet; Oregon DHS via TODAY |
| Illinois | 14 | Neglected minor defined in part as a minor under 14 left "without supervision for an unreasonable period of time" without regard for the minor's health, safety, or welfare | Child Welfare Gateway factsheet; Illinois DCFS via TODAY |

Note (FACT): Illinois' 14 is the highest statutory age in the country; it was historically higher and remains the strictest. All three statutes hinge on "unreasonable period" or "endanger welfare", not a blanket ban.

## 10. Piaget stage reference with modern caveats

**Framework:** Jean Piaget's four stages of cognitive development. Ages below per [Simply Psychology, Piaget's Theory and Stages](https://www.simplypsychology.org/piaget.html) (accessed 2026-07-06) and [NCBI StatPearls, Piaget](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sites/books/NBK448206/) (accessed 2026-07-06). Included because ages 2 to 16 span the top three stages.

| Stage | Ages | Hallmark (FACT) | Anchor months (spanning) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sensorimotor | Birth to ~2y (18 to 24mo) | Knows through senses and movement; object permanence | 0 to 24 (mostly below product scope) |
| Preoperational | 2 to 7y | Symbolic/representational thought, language, egocentrism, pre-logical (fails conservation) | 24 to 84 |
| Concrete operational | 7 to 11y | Logical thought about concrete objects; conservation, reversibility, decentration | 84 to 132 |
| Formal operational | 11y and up | Abstract, hypothetical, deductive scientific reasoning | 132+ |

**Modern caveats (FACT):**

- Piaget underestimated younger children's abilities; on age-appropriate, less language-heavy tasks children often succeed earlier than his stages predict (e.g. theory of mind at 4 to 5, section 3.2; false-belief-adjacent competence shown even in 3-year-olds under simplified conditions). Source: [Simply Psychology criticisms section](https://www.simplypsychology.org/piaget.html) and [Simply Put Psych, Criticisms of Piaget](https://simplyputpsych.co.uk/psych-101-1/criticisms-of-piagets-theory-of-cognitive-development-methodological-cultural-and-conceptual-limits) (both accessed 2026-07-06).
- The discrete, invariant-stage assumption is contested: children show mixed reasoning, not sharp stage jumps.
- Piaget underweighted culture, language, and social interaction (the Vygotskian critique).
- Not all adolescents (or adults) reach full formal-operational reasoning on all tasks.

**Product stance (INFERENCE):** treat Piaget as an orientation map for what kinds of thinking are typical, not as a source of hard age anchors. Homa's map anchors should come from the standards-based lanes (02 to 07) and the milestone frameworks in sections 3 and 4, not from Piaget stage boundaries.

**Confidence: High** (canonical framework; caveats are mainstream and well sourced).

- **Attention span minutes-by-age table.** No defensible normed table located in free primary form. The "2 to 3 minutes per year" rule is folklore (section 5). A real table would need Conners CPT, TOVA, or NIH Toolbox (Flanker/attention) normative data. Follow-up: check the NIH Toolbox norming papers and Cognition Battery age norms.
- **Damon primary text.** Distributive-justice levels captured via a reputable tertiary summary (Wikipedia's Kohlberg article) and general developmental sources, not Damon's The Social World of the Child (1977) directly. Ages 3 to 9 and the equality-to-merit-to-need progression are consistent across sources but should be confirmed against the primary before porting a specific age anchor.
- **Hoffman and Selman primary texts.** Captured via Stanford Encyclopedia, a peer-reviewed Japanese review (J-STAGE), and academic open textbooks that cite Selman 1980 and Hoffman 2000. Stage content is stable; exact age boundaries vary slightly by summarizer.
- **CASEL graded-by-age progression.** CASEL deliberately does not publish one; section 2 uses Illinois as the operationalization. If a cleaner 2020s state revision is wanted, candidates to check next: Washington State (2019 benchmarks), Pennsylvania, and Michigan; not fetched this pass.
- **Illinois late-H.S. band (11 to 12, anchor 216mo)** exceeds the product's scored top (204mo). Captured for completeness; only the K-3 through 9-10 bands are directly portable.
- **Volunteering minimum age.** No single statute; org-by-org. Not anchored.
- **Dawson/Guare Table 1.1** was captured from a faithful secondary reproduction (Institute of Progressive Education and Learning) and the smartbutscatteredkids.com Table 1, not Guilford's own copyrighted PDF. Text matches across the two hosts.

**Known tensions and honest caveats:**

- Milestone ages are research consensus windows, not norm-referenced cut points; individual variation is wide and the sources say so. Statistical vocabulary is fine in this internal file but must not leak into parent surfaces (per README and BUILD.md law 4).
- Erikson and Piaget are historically foundational but partly superseded; both are included as orientation, with modern critiques attached, not as anchor sources.
- Grade-to-month conversions are a convention, flagged wherever used.
