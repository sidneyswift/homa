## 5. WHO 2020 physical activity guideline for ages 5 to 17

FACT, verified against the WHO guideline document itself (WHO Guidelines on Physical Activity and Sedentary Behaviour, 2020), children and adolescents aged 5 to 17 years:

- "Children and adolescents should do at least an average of 60 minutes per day of moderate to vigorous-intensity, mostly aerobic, physical activity, across the week." Strong recommendation, moderate certainty evidence.
- "Vigorous-intensity aerobic activities, as well as those that strengthen muscle and bone, should be incorporated at least 3 days a week." Strong recommendation, moderate certainty evidence.
- Sedentary guidance in the same section: limit the amount of time spent being sedentary, particularly recreational screen time.

Source: [WHO 2020 guidelines PDF on iris.who.int](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/f3885485-e7eb-4504-8026-edd9bb53a6ee/content) (accessed 2026-07-06); same text mirrored at [NCBI Bookshelf recommendations chapter](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK566046/) (accessed 2026-07-06). The WHO physical activity fact sheet ([who.int fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/physical-activity), accessed 2026-07-06) confirms the 2020 guidelines are still current and states the next guideline edition is anticipated in 2030.

This upgrades lane 07 section 11's INFERENCE to FACT with the exact wording: the "average across the week" framing (not a hard daily minimum) that lane 07 inferred is exactly what WHO wrote, and it matches the HHS 60-minute daily guideline lane 07 verified. The evidence-summary paper for the 5 to 17 chapter ([Chaput et al. 2020, IJBNPA](https://ijbnpa.biomedcentral.com/counter/pdf/10.1186/s12966-020-01037-z.pdf), accessed 2026-07-06) explicitly notes the average-per-day framing replaced the every-day reading of the 2010 guideline.

Confidence: High. Verdict: RESOLVED.

## 6. AAP media policy, school-aged children and adolescents

The headline: the 2016 statement lane 07 cited via a slide summary has been REPLACED. FACT: the AAP published a new policy statement, "Digital Ecosystems, Children, and Adolescents" (Pediatrics, February 2026, doi 10.1542/peds.2025-075320, with a companion technical report, doi 10.1542/peds.2025-075321), and "the statement and technical report replace prior AAP guidance on communications and media," which includes the 2016 Media Use in School-Aged Children and Adolescents statement ([AAP policy statement page](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/157/2/e2025075320/206129/Digital-Ecosystems-Children-and-Adolescents-Policy), accessed 2026-07-06; [HealthyChildren announcement](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/news/Pages/creating-a-child-friendly-digital-world-AAP-releases-new-media-recommendations.aspx), accessed 2026-07-06; [AAP News summary](https://publications.aap.org/aapnews/news/34088/Beyond-screen-time-Policy-discusses-how-to), accessed 2026-07-06).

What the 2026 policy says that matters for lane 07's self_care items (all FACT from the above pages):

- Evidence is organized by age group: early childhood (0 to 5), school-age (6 to 12), teens (13 to 18). For school-age children: high-quality digital media with learning goals, in moderation, is associated with math and reading learning; excessive use is associated with lower academic achievement, weaker attention control, and sleep disruption.
- Family guidance keeps the Family Media Plan and adds the "5 Cs of Media Use": Child, Content, Calm, Crowding Out, Communication. The frame is displacement (media crowding out sleep, physical activity, in-person interaction), not a single hour cap.
- The AAP News provider guidance retains time boundaries as ranges, not limits: "Time limits might range from less than one hour/day for toddlers and preschoolers to one to two hours/day or more of entertainment (not school-related) media for school-age children and teens."

On the original target: the primary 2016 Pediatrics text (e20162592) returned 403 on direct fetch from publications.aap.org, so the specific "1 hour physical activity and 8 to 12 hours sleep" sentence lane 07 quoted from a slide summary was not re-verified from the 2016 primary. That is now moot for porting purposes: the 2016 statement is superseded, and any media item wording should target the 2026 ecosystem/displacement framing instead.

INFERENCE for the product: Homa's screen-related self_care wording should follow the 5 Cs displacement framing (is media crowding out sleep, movement, play) rather than a fixed daily minute number, which the AAP itself no longer leads with for ages 6 plus.

Confidence: High for the 2026 policy's existence, structure, and family recommendations (publisher pages fetched); Med on any 2016-specific numeric claims (primary still unfetched, now superseded anyway). Verdict: RESOLVED (with the material finding that the cited document is no longer current policy).

## 7. A modern graded state SEL standard (Washington 2019-2020)

FACT: Washington adopted statewide SEL standards, benchmarks, and indicators in January 2020 (developed 2019 by the legislatively appointed SEL Indicators Workgroup, adopted by the state superintendent). Structure: 6 standards, 17 benchmarks, with observable indicators laid out across four grade bands: early elementary (K to 3), late elementary (4 to 5), middle school (6 to 8), high school/adult (9 plus). Sources: [OSPI SEL page](https://ospi.k12.wa.us/index%2ephp/student-success/resources-subject-area/social-emotional-learning-sel) (accessed 2026-07-06) and the primary [OSPI SEL Standards, Benchmarks and Indicators PDF, long form](https://ospi.k12.wa.us/sites/default/files/2023-10/selstandardsbenchmarksindicatorslongform.pdf) (accessed 2026-07-06).

The six standards (FACT, verbatim short names): Self-Awareness; Self-Management; Self-Efficacy; Social Awareness; Social Management; Social Engagement. Notable vs Illinois 2004 and CASEL 2020: Washington splits Self-Efficacy and Social Engagement out as their own standards (CASEL folds these into self-awareness/self-management and relationship skills).

Example progression showing the band-benchmark structure (FACT, Benchmark 1A, "demonstrates awareness and understanding of one's own emotions and emotions' influence on behavior," first-person indicators): early elementary "With adult assistance, I can recognize, identify, and name my emotions, feelings, and thoughts"; late elementary "I can explain the different intensities of my emotions and feelings"; middle school "I can use self-reflection to determine if my emotions and feelings are proportional to the situation"; high school/adult "I can analyze how and why an emotion can trigger behaviors in different contexts."

Comparison against Illinois 2004 (lane 08's grid), INFERENCE: same ladder logic (adult-assisted naming, then intensity/causes, then reflection, then contextual analysis), so lane 08's Illinois-derived anchors are directionally confirmed by a 2020 framework. Two structural differences matter: Washington uses 4 bands where Illinois uses 5 (Illinois splits high school), and Washington's own document says the indicators "are not intended for use in formally assessing or labeling students" and are non-linear, band-spanning behaviors. That is even stronger anti-cutoff language than Illinois; port Washington wording as observation prompts only, never as expected-by ages.

Confidence: High (primary state PDF). Verdict: RESOLVED.

## 9. ELOF by-60-months indicators, four remaining domains

Extracted from the same official framework PDF lane 01 used (Head Start Early Learning Outcomes Framework: Ages Birth to Five, HHS/ACF 2015, [ECLKC framework PDF](https://eclkc.ohs.acf.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/elof-ohs-framework.pdf), accessed 2026-07-06, full PDF downloaded and text-extracted). All FACT; condensed per goal, wording from the "By 60 Months" indicator column. These are program outcome indicators for the end of Head Start, not norms.

**Approaches to Learning (P-ATL), 13 goals.** 1 emotions: expresses emotions appropriately to the situation, seeks adult help when most intense, uses coping strategies (words, deep breaths) with adult support (same goal as P-SE 8). 2 rules and routines: follows classroom rules most of the time, follows routines, responds to transition signals. 3 materials: handles materials appropriately, cleans up and puts materials away. 4 behavior: controls actions and words in challenging situations, waits for turn, refrains from aggression, begins to understand consequences of behavior. 5 impulses: stops an engaging activity to transition with guidance, delays desires (waits turn), waits to speak in a group without reminders, refrains from grabbing. 6 attention: maintains focus on activities for extended periods "such as 15 minutes or more," engages in purposeful play for extended periods, attends to adults in groups with minimal support. 7 persistence: completes challenging or less-preferred tasks despite frustration, returns to an activity after being away. 8 working memory: recounts recent experiences in order with detail, follows detailed multi-step directions (sometimes with reminders), remembers actions to stories or songs. 9 flexibility: tries different strategies, applies different rules in different contexts (indoor vs outdoor voice), transitions without getting upset. 10 initiative: engages in independent activities, makes and communicates choices, gathers what a task needs, plans play scenarios with roles. 11 curiosity: asks questions and seeks new information, joins new activities even if challenging, eager to discuss a range of topics. 12 creativity: asks questions showing new ways to do a task, creative problem solving, multiple means of expression. 13 imagination: engages in social and pretend play, creates stories or art with materials, uses objects to represent something else (paper plate as steering wheel).

Note the P-ATL 6 indicator is the closest thing ELOF offers to a focus-duration number ("such as 15 minutes or more" of maintained activity focus by 60 months, given as an example, not a cut). This is compatible with the shipped task-persistence rewording of `focus-15` and does not justify restoring minute claims (the phrase is an illustrative example inside one indicator).

**Social and Emotional Development (P-SE), 11 goals.** 1 adult relationships: interacts readily with trusted adults, some positive interaction with less familiar adults, seeks help when needed. 2 prosocial with adults: respectful language and greetings, attends when asked, follows adult expectations, asks or waits for permission when unsure. 3 peer relationships: maintains positive interactions, uses entry skills (suggesting a joint activity, joining, sharing), takes turns in conversation, develops friendships with one or two preferred children. 4 cooperative play: joint play with coordinated goals, planning, roles, and games with rules with at least one other child; includes others' ideas; reflects on past play. 5 conflict resolution: recognizes and describes basic social problems, uses sharing, turn-taking, and compromise, expresses feelings and opinions in conflict, seeks adult help when needed. 6 emotion knowledge: recognizes and labels basic emotions in books or photographs, uses words for own feelings and for others' feelings. 7 empathy: makes empathetic statements, offers support to distressed adults or children. 8 emotion regulation: same as P-ATL 1. 9 identity: describes self with several characteristics, knows own uniqueness (talents, interests, culture). 10 confidence: shows satisfaction or seeks acknowledgment when completing a task, expresses own ideas in groups, uses positive self-descriptions ("kind," "hard-worker"). 11 belonging: identifies self as part of family, community, culture, faith, or preschool groups; tells personal stories about group membership.

**Language and Communication (P-LC), 7 goals.** 1 attending: verbal and non-verbal signals to acknowledge others, stays connected to a conversation or group discussion. 2 comprehension: recalls multi-step directions in order, understands question types (yes/no, wh-, how/why), understands multi-clause and if-then sentences, understands talk about past or future (dual language learners may show this first in the home language). 3 informativeness: gives sufficient detail to get needs met, uses language to clarify when misunderstood, DLLs may code-switch. 4 conversational rules: maintains multi-turn conversations in groups with related questions and agreement, matches tone and volume to situation (whisper for a secret). 5 expression: communicates clearly enough to be understood by adults across situations with isolated, infrequent pronunciation and grammar errors; proficiency with prepositions, regular and irregular past tense, possessives, noun-verb agreement; sentences typically over 5 words with sequence and causal structures; produces and organizes multiple sentences on a topic, including past or absent things. 6 vocabulary: uses multiple (2 to 3) new words or signs a day during play, recognizes key domain-specific words from reading or discussion. 7 word relationships: shows understanding of word categories and relationships (for example provides rhyming words on request, per the adjacent P-LIT phonological block).

**Perceptual, Motor, and Physical Development (P-PMP), 6 goals.** 1 gross motor: balance in large-muscle movement (walking a log, balancing on one leg), combined coordinated movements (swinging, climbing a ladder, dancing), strength and stamina for running games like tag. 2 perception in action: aware of own body and others' space, positions body to task (kicks a ball), can place self in front of, beside, or behind on request, changes direction easily. 3 fine motor: coordinates hand and eye for puzzles and stringing beads, pincer grip on writing and drawing tools, complex coordinated tasks (cutting along a line, pouring, buttoning). 4 hygiene and self-care: washes hands with soap and knows when (before eating, after bathroom, after nose-blowing); increasing responsibility for tooth brushing and dressing. 5 nutrition: identifies healthy and unhealthy foods, understands variety helps the body grow, moderates eating based on own hunger and fullness. 6 safety: identifies, avoids, and alerts others to danger (safe distance from swings), follows basic safety rules with adult guidance (street safety).

INFERENCE for the bank: the P-PMP 3 indicator triplet (cutting along a line, pouring, buttoning at 60mo) and P-SE 4 (games with rules by 60mo) are the strongest new anchor candidates; the rest largely corroborates CDC and lane 01 items already shipped.

Confidence: High (federal framework PDF, text extracted directly). Verdict: RESOLVED.

## 10. A third state kindergarten-entry inventory (Illinois KIDS)

FACT, from the Illinois State Board of Education's own documents: the Kindergarten Individual Development Survey (KIDS) is Illinois's statewide kindergarten-entry instrument, observational (teacher rates naturally occurring behavior), statewide-required since fall 2017 after a five-year pilot, adapted from the California DRDP with WestEd, aligned to the Illinois Early Learning and Development Standards. ## 11. Music development norms: Gordon audiation and Rutkowski SVDM

**Gordon preparatory audiation (FACT, from the Gordon Institute for Music Learning's own framework page, [giml.org early childhood](https://giml.org/mlt/earlychildhood/), accessed 2026-07-06):** a child who has not yet emerged from music babble is in preparatory audiation, which has three types containing seven stages:

- Type 1 Acculturation, birth to age 2 to 4 (little consciousness of environment): stage 1 absorption (hears and collects music), stage 2 random response (moves and babbles without relation to the music; GIML says ideally between ages one and three), stage 3 purposeful response (relates movement and babble to the music).
- Type 2 Imitation, ages 2 to 4 up to 3 to 5 (conscious thought focused on environment): stage 4 shedding egocentricity (notices own singing does not match), stage 5 breaking the code (imitates tonal and rhythm patterns with some precision).
- Type 3 Assimilation, ages 3 to 5 up to 4 to 6 (conscious thought focused on self): stage 6 introspection (notices lack of coordination between singing and breathing/movement), stage 7 coordination (coordinates singing and chanting with breathing and movement).

Gordon's position (FACT, same source): children in preparatory audiation should receive informal guidance, not formal instruction. Ages are overlapping approximate ranges, explicitly exposure-dependent. Primary text: Gordon, A Music Learning Theory for Newborn and Young Children (GIA, 2003 edition; [publisher record via Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/A_Music_Learning_Theory_for_Newborn_and.html?id=UzJEIhSraKsC), accessed 2026-07-06, not read in full).

**Rutkowski Singing Voice Development Measure (FACT, from a peer-reviewed review by Rutkowski herself in the Phenomenon of Singing series, [Memorial University journal PDF](https://journals.library.mun.ca/index.php/singing/article/download/945/820/3266), accessed 2026-07-06):** the SVDM (Rutkowski 1990, revised through Version 3, 1991-1992) rates use of the singing voice, explicitly not intonation accuracy, on a 1 to 5 scale with half steps: 1 pre-singer (chants the song text); 1.5 inconsistent speaking range singer; 2 speaking range singer (sustains tones, stays in speaking range, usually A2 to C3); 2.5 inconsistent limited range singer; 3 limited range singer (usually D3 to F3); 3.5 inconsistent initial range singer; 4 initial range singer (usually D3 to A3); 4.5 inconsistent singer; 5 singer (extends beyond the register lift, B3-flat and above). Age anchor in the same paper (FACT, citing Davidson et al. 1981): "many children do not demonstrate accurate intonation within a phrase until approximately age 5.5 or 6 years of age." The SVDM itself is a stage scale, not an age-normed table; no per-age SVDM norm table appears in this source.

INFERENCE for lane 07's gap: these two frameworks give citable structure (stages and their logic) but only soft ages. They support lane 07's existing "sings with accuracy around 4 to 5" claims directionally (Gordon's coordination stage at 3 to 5 up to 4 to 6; intonation within a phrase around 5.5 to 6) without providing a norm table. Music items should stay exposure-flavored.

Confidence: High for stage structure and definitions (framework owner and author-written review); Med for any age use (approximate, exposure-dependent by the sources' own statements). Verdict: RESOLVED (structure and best-available ages captured; no age-normed table exists to find).

## 14. Monkey bars and playground climbing age reference

A citable federal reference exists: the US Consumer Product Safety Commission's Public Playground Safety Handbook (2025 update, Publication 325) and a CPSC staff age-determination memo.

- FACT, handbook section 5.3.2.4 (horizontal/overhead ladders, the equipment class that includes monkey bars): "Four-year-olds are generally the youngest children able to use upper body devices like these; therefore, horizontal ladders should not be used on playgrounds intended for toddlers and 3-year olds. The recommendations below are designed to accommodate children ages 4 through 12 years." The equipment age table lists "horizontal ladders less than or equal to 60 inches high for ages 4 and 5" in the preschool column and the full ladder/overhead-ring family in the 5 to 12 column ([CPSC Public Playground Safety Handbook 2025 PDF](https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/325_PublicPlaygroundSafetyHandbook2025_7-30-25.pdf), accessed 2026-07-06).
- FACT, CPSC staff memo on age designations (citing Aronson 1988 and the handbook): a "lock grip" (fingers and thumb wrapped around a handhold) develops around 2.5 to 3.5 years; climbing rung ladders around 4.5 to 5; toddlers are "probably not ready for most upper body devices, such as overhead ladders; and although 4- and 5-year-olds will begin experimenting with upper body devices, most children probably won't master the combination of upper body strength and coordination needed for such equipment until their school-age years" ([CPSC age designations for playground equipment memo](https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/Age-designations-for-playground-equipment-6b-cleared-Redacted.pdf), accessed 2026-07-06).
- Related handbook anchor (FACT): vertical sliding poles are "not recommended for toddlers or preschool-age children since they generally don't have the upper body and/or hand strength to slide" (same handbook PDF).

INFERENCE for the bank: this supports a two-rung observation ladder: hangs from a bar and experiments with monkey bars around 4 to 5 (48 to 66mo), traverses monkey bars rung over rung as a school-age skill, roughly 6 plus (72mo plus, "school-age years" per CPSC staff). This is safety-equipment design guidance, not a motor norm; frame any item as what the child enjoys trying, never a deadline.

Confidence: High that the CPSC guidance says this (federal primary documents); Med as a developmental anchor (design guidance grade). Verdict: RESOLVED (lane 07's "no citable source" is superseded).
