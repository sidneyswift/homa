# Neurodivergent and twice-exceptional trajectories

> Historical research snapshot, 2026-07-06. This file preserves the evidence and analysis captured on that date. It is not current product, legal, provider, standards, or production guidance. Re-verify volatile facts and source freshness before porting any claim into Homa.

**Date:** 2026-07-06
**Lane:** ND and 2e 12, part of the ages 2 to 16 national benchmark corpus (see README.md in this folder)
**Method:** Web search plus direct page and PDF fetches on 2026-07-06, prioritizing peer-reviewed primary sources (Ferrer 2010, Shaw 2007, Torgesen 2001, Crowe and McLeod 2020, Pokoski and Durkin 2025) and professional or government bodies (IDA, ASHA, CDC, NIMH, NAGC, Columbus Group). Advocacy sources are used for framing only, never for numbers. Cross-references to corpus lanes 01, 03, 08 and to `docs/homa-planning/icp-research/03-benchmarks-behind-kid.md`.

**Top note, read before using anything below.** This file uses clinical vocabulary (dyslexia, ADHD, autism, dyscalculia, dysgraphia, delay, deficit, percentile) because it is internal reference for calibrating tolerance and copy. NONE of this vocabulary may reach a parent-facing surface: the banned-words law in `packages/core/src/map/content.ts` and the anti-guilt display law (BUILD.md law 4) govern anything ported. Nothing here licenses a diagnosis, a screen, or a label about a specific child. Homa is a companion, not a diagnostician (founder decision D5). The product use of this lane is exactly two things: (1) widen "still in range" tolerances so a neurodivergent child is not implied to be behind, and (2) shape honest, asynchrony-respecting copy. It never classifies.

**Key findings:**

- Prevalence numbers are cut-point artifacts, not head counts. Dyslexia is quoted at 5 to 20 percent because reading is continuously distributed and the diagnostic line is arbitrary; the same is true, less dramatically, for dyscalculia (3 to 7 percent) and dysgraphia (7 to 15 percent). The honest product posture is a wide range, never a single "X percent are affected" claim.
- The dyslexic reading trajectory is parallel, not convergent. The gap is present by grade 1 and persists into adolescence without widening much (Connecticut Longitudinal Study; Ferrer et al. 2010). A later start alone predicts nothing bad (Suggate, lane 03), but a genuine skill deficit does not self-correct by waiting.
- Intensive intervention produces large, documented catch-up in accuracy and comprehension (Torgesen 2001: effect sizes 3.9 to 4.4, 40 percent exiting special education), but reading rate stays impaired for most. "Caught up" is domain-specific, not global.
- The famous "ADHD is a 30 percent developmental lag" rule is Barkley's own back-of-envelope clinical heuristic (a 25 to 41 percent range across measures, rounded), not a single peer-reviewed finding. The neuroanatomical delay it leans on IS peer-reviewed: Shaw 2007 found peak cortical thickness reached about 3 years late in ADHD, up to 5 years in the middle prefrontal cortex.
- Autism is defined by uneven profiles, not uniform delay: 25 to 35 percent remain minimally verbal at school age while others exceed age peers in language, and about 72 percent show motor milestone delays. A single whole-child number is meaningless for this group and reads as an insult (ICP file section 5).
- The 2e masking trap: a gifted plus dyslexic child often reads at exactly grade level because ability cancels disability, so both the gift and the difficulty go invisible and the child looks perfectly typical. Grade-level performance is not evidence of no asynchrony.

## 2. Dyslexia and reading trajectories

**Confidence: High** for prevalence-is-a-cutpoint and for the Torgesen and Ferrer findings (peer-reviewed primaries); **Med** for exact catch-up magnitudes generalizing to a homeschool setting.

### Prevalence: why the number is a range, not a fact

**FACT.** Dyslexia prevalence is quoted anywhere from 5 to 20 percent, and the spread is not measurement sloppiness, it is structural. Reading ability is continuously distributed in the population, so identifying dyslexia means placing a cut-point on a smooth curve, and where you put the line determines the rate. At a strict criterion (1.5 standard deviations below the mean) common estimates fall at 3 to 7 percent; at a looser criterion (25th percentile, or a regression-based IQ-achievement definition) Shaywitz et al. 1992 estimated 17.4 percent ([Wagner et al. 2020, "The Prevalence of Dyslexia: A New Approach to its Estimation"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8183124/), accessed 2026-07-06). A 2025 Delphi consensus puts the reported range at "about 5 percent to 20 percent, but these depend upon the cut-off criteria used" ([Carroll et al. 2025, JCPP](https://acamh.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jcpp.14123), accessed 2026-07-06). A systematic review pooled 7.10 percent (95 percent CI 6.27 to 7.97) ([Yang et al. 2022, meta-analysis](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3425/12/2/240), accessed 2026-07-06).

**FACT.** Because the boundary is arbitrary, one paper states flatly that "the notion of prevalence itself is thus an artifact of the assessment methods, and it does not reflect the population's distribution" ([Protopapas discussion, Frontiers 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.02171/pdf), accessed 2026-07-06). The estimate moves with the cut-point in a predictable way:

| Identification criterion | Prevalence estimate | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1.5 SD below the mean on reading | about 3 to 7 percent | Fletcher et al. 2019; Peterson and Pennington 2012, via [Wagner et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8183124/) |
| 25th percentile and/or regression-based 1.5 SD | 17.4 percent | Shaywitz et al. 1992, via [Wagner et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8183124/) |
| Meta-analytic pooled estimate | 7.10 percent (95 percent CI 6.27 to 7.97) | [Yang et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3425/12/2/240) |
| Reported range across the literature | about 5 to 20 percent | [Carroll et al. 2025 Delphi](https://acamh.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jcpp.14123) |

(all accessed 2026-07-06)

**INFERENCE for Homa.** Never state a prevalence figure to a parent, and internally never treat any single cut-point as "the" line. The product's job is to describe where a specific skill sits without deciding whether the child crosses a diagnostic threshold; that decision is a clinician's, per D5.

### The trajectory picture: parallel tracks, not convergence

**FACT.** In the Connecticut Longitudinal Study, reading and IQ develop as a coupled, mutually reinforcing system in typical readers, but in dyslexic readers the two "uncouple" and develop more independently ([Ferrer, Shaywitz, Holahan, Marchione, Shaywitz 2010, "Uncoupling of Reading and IQ Over Time," Psychological Science](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797609354084); [Yale PDF](https://dyslexia.yale.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/IQandDyslexiaUncoupled.pdf), accessed 2026-07-06). The practical consequence, from the same longitudinal cohort, is that the reading-achievement gap between dyslexic and typical readers appears early (by grade 1) and persists into adolescence and adulthood as a roughly parallel track: it is an early, stable difference rather than a gap that widens or, absent intervention, closes on its own.

**FACT.** Dyslexia is persistent, not transient. The Connecticut cohort was explicitly titled on this point: "Persistence of Dyslexia: The Connecticut Longitudinal Study at Adolescence" (Shaywitz et al. 1999, Pediatrics), and the broader literature notes dyslexia is dimensional (the low tail of a normal reading distribution), with a male-to-female ratio of about 1.5 to 1 (lower than historical 3 to 4 to 1 estimates) and strong heritability, but also that it "can be prevented in many children with early intervention" ([Shaywitz work summarized in "Dyslexia: The evolution of a scientific concept," PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3079378/), accessed 2026-07-06). Persistent plus preventable-with-early-help is the exact combination that makes the wait-and-see posture risky.

**FACT (secondary, sourcing chain noted).** The classic "developmental lag versus deficit" question was settled toward deficit: struggling readers do not simply bloom late and catch up on their own. Juel's longitudinal study is repeatedly summarized as finding that a child who was a poor reader at the end of grade 1 had about an 88 percent probability of still being a poor reader at the end of grade 4, and that the poor readers' distinguishing feature was a phonemic-awareness skill deficit, not slower maturation ([AFT American Educator summary of Juel 1988, quoted at WTM forums](https://forums.welltrainedmind.com/topic/322044-struggling-readers-never-catch-up-the-myth-of-late-bloomers/), accessed 2026-07-06). This is a secondary source quoting the primary; flagged in dry wells for primary verification.

### The late-bloomer versus struggling-reader distinction (extends lane 03 Suggate)

**FACT.** Lane 03 already carries Suggate et al. 2012: New Zealand children who started formal reading at age 7 caught up to age-5 starters by age 11, so a late START by itself does not predict weak outcomes (`03-reading-k-10.md`, accessed 2026-07-06). This lane adds the distinguishing predictors. The three strongest early predictors of later reading difficulty, replicated across orthographies, are phonological awareness, letter knowledge, and rapid automatized naming (RAN) ([Jyvaskyla Longitudinal Study of Dyslexia review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4624816/), accessed 2026-07-06). A kindergarten cohort found letter knowledge (odds ratio about 4.75) and phonological awareness (odds ratio about 4.17) each raised grade-1 reading-deficit risk roughly four to five fold ([JAMA Network Open 2026, kindergarten screening](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.3036), accessed 2026-07-06).

**INFERENCE for Homa.** The observable line between a "late bloomer" (wide-normal onset, no underlying skill gap) and a "struggling reader" (a real phonological or naming deficit) is whether the foundational skills are quietly developing underneath the not-yet-reading. A 6-year-old not decoding but who rhymes, hears initial sounds, and knows letters is inside the wide window (lane 03). The same age without those foundations is the signal that waiting is the wrong move. Homa cannot and must not diagnose this, but its per-skill map is exactly the right instrument to surface the phonological-awareness and letter-knowledge rungs so a parent sees whether the substrate is present, framed as "what is growing," never as risk.

### Intervention response: what catch-up looks like

**FACT.** Torgesen et al. 2001 gave 60 children ages 8 to 10 with severe reading disabilities 67.5 hours of one-to-one instruction (two 50-minute sessions per day for 8 weeks), comparing two intensive programs. Both produced very large gains in generalized reading, with effect sizes of 4.4 and 3.9 relative to the growth those same children had made during their prior 16 months in resource rooms. Accuracy and comprehension moved into the average range and stayed there over a 2-year follow-up; within 1 year, 40 percent (24 children) no longer needed special education. Crucially, reading RATE remained severely impaired for most of them ([Torgesen et al. 2001, Journal of Learning Disabilities](https://doi.org/10.1177/002221940103400104); [ERIC EJ621412](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ621412), accessed 2026-07-06).

**FACT (who catches up, within the same study).** The response was not uniform: the top quartile of the intervention group caught up to and in some cases passed typical peers, while the bottom quartile continued to struggle. The best predictors of long-term growth were not the child's initial phonological score but teacher ratings of attention and behavior, general verbal ability, and prior levels of component reading skills ([Torgesen et al. 2001](https://doi.org/10.1177/002221940103400104), accessed 2026-07-06). Attention, in other words, moderated reading gains, which is one concrete way ADHD and dyslexia compound.

**INFERENCE for Homa.** Three lessons. First, catch-up is real and can be dramatic, which validates the segment's hope and its self-funded intervention behavior (ICP file section 5). Second, "caught up" is skill-specific: a child can reach average accuracy and comprehension while fluency (rate) lags for years. A months-ahead or single-number readout would mislabel exactly this child. The product must report reading as separate skills (decoding, accuracy, fluency, comprehension) so an accuracy gain is visible even while rate trails, matching lane 03's caution that fluency stops discriminating past about 132 months anyway. Third, because response varies widely child to child, the product should anchor progress to the child's own prior state (before/after), which the segment already celebrates, rather than to any expected recovery curve.

### Typical progress under intervention (words correct per minute)

**DRY WELL, partially.** A clean, citable "typical WCPM growth per week under RTI" figure was not landed in this pass. Lane 03 carries the Hasbrouck and Tindal 2017 oral reading fluency norms (the typical end-state target by grade), and lane 10 carries growth framing, but a defensible intervention-specific weekly WCPM slope for reading-disabled children under RTI is logged as a follow-up (see dry wells). Do not invent one; use lane 03's normed ORF percentiles as the calibration reference and treat any intervention slope as unverified.

## 3. ADHD and executive function

**Confidence: High** for the Shaw 2007 neuroanatomy and for the provenance of the 30 percent rule; **Med** for translating either into a specific EF tolerance in months.

### The "30 percent developmental lag" rule: real provenance

**FACT.** The widely repeated claim that ADHD represents "about a 30 percent delay" in executive function, or the "rule of two-thirds" (multiply chronological age by 0.67 to estimate executive age), originates with Russell Barkley. In his own slide materials the claim reads: "ADHD appears to delay EF development by 25 to 40 percent, or an average of 30 percent" ([Barkley, Theory of Executive Function and Self-Regulation, slide deck PDF hosted by VA Psychological Association](https://www.vapsych.org/assets/docs/Theory%20of%20Executive%20Function%20%20Self%20Regulation%20-%20Barkley.pdf), accessed 2026-07-06).

**FACT.** Barkley has publicly stated the number is not from a single study. In his own words: "The rule is not based on a single scientific study of that issue... I went back into my own published research studies and that of many others and simply wrote down the extent to which the ADHD group's performance differed from that of typical children in the same study... The range of deficits was from about 25 to 41 percent or so. The average was 32 percent but that is not as good for clinical use as is using a nice round number when speaking to parents. So I rounded it down to 30 percent... It is clearly a rough (imprecise) rule based on a back of the envelope type of calculation" ([Barkley, "ADHD and Children's Delayed Executive Functioning Age," video commentary transcript](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v_6nU-i6tcY), accessed 2026-07-06).

**INFERENCE.** The 30 percent figure is a clinical communication heuristic, honestly labeled as such by its author, derived by eyeballing effect sizes across many studies. It is defensible as a "lower your expectations by roughly a third" counseling tool, and it is NOT a precise, independently validated developmental constant. Homa should treat it the way Barkley intended, as a reason to widen EF tolerance generously, and must never present it as a measured fact about a child.

**FACT (co-occurrence).** ADHD rarely travels alone, which matters for cross-domain tolerance: among US children with current ADHD, 77.9 percent had at least one co-occurring disorder and 58.1 percent had moderate or severe ADHD ([CDC, 2022 NSCH, Danielson et al. 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15374416.2024.2335625), accessed 2026-07-06). An ADHD profile in a Homa family is therefore a reason to widen tolerance across several domains at once, not just executive function.

### EF trajectory research: the delay is real and peer-reviewed

**FACT.** The neuroanatomical claim underneath Barkley's heuristic is well sourced. Shaw et al. 2007 estimated cortical thickness at over 40,000 cerebral points from 824 MRI scans on 223 children with ADHD and 223 matched controls. Maturation followed the same regional sequence in both groups, but was markedly delayed in ADHD: the median age at which 50 percent of cortical points reached peak thickness was 10.5 years in ADHD versus 7.5 years in controls (about a 3-year delay). The delay was most prominent in prefrontal regions governing attention and motor planning, and the middle prefrontal cortex reached peak thickness about 5 years later in the ADHD group. The authors frame this as a delay in maturation, not a deviation from the template ([Shaw et al. 2007, PNAS 104:19649](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0707741104); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2148343/); [NIMH/EurekAlert summary](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/882080), accessed 2026-07-06).

### What this means for EF benchmark tolerance by age

**INFERENCE.** Two peer-reviewed anchors (about 3 years of cortical delay overall, up to 5 in the prefrontal cortex) plus Barkley's honestly-hedged 25 to 40 percent heuristic converge on the same product decision: executive-function anchors need the widest tolerance of any domain, and the tolerance should GROW with age in absolute terms (a 30 percent proportional lag is about 1.5 years at age 5 but about 4.5 years at age 15). This dovetails with the corpus's existing correction 1 (`00-synthesis.md`): attention-minute claims are folklore and were already reworded to task-persistence framing. The ND read strengthens that: for a child whose family has mentioned attention or regulation struggles, EF rungs should read as "still developing, on its own timeline," never as "behind." Note also lane 08 owns the EF domain anchors; this is a tolerance overlay, not new anchors.

## 4. Autism: language, profile unevenness, motor

**Confidence: High** for the minimally-verbal proportions and the motor-delay prevalence (peer-reviewed primaries); **Med** for characterizing the "uneven profile" quantitatively.

### Language onset distributions

**FACT.** Autistic language trajectories are the widest of any group covered here. Historically (National Research Council 2001) over half of autistic children were thought never to acquire functional speech; more recent estimates put the minimally verbal proportion at about 25 to 35 percent by school age, the drop attributed to broader diagnostic criteria, earlier identification, and earlier intervention ([Tager-Flusberg and Kasari 2013, "Minimally Verbal School-Aged Children," PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3869868/); [Springer systematic review 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10567-024-00507-1), accessed 2026-07-06). A community early-intervention cohort found 26.3 percent exited using fewer than five spontaneous functional words ([Rose et al. 2016, JIDR](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jir.12284), accessed 2026-07-06).

**FACT.** Intervention shifts these odds substantially. In a 2025 study of 470 autistic children who received evidence-based early intervention, about two-thirds of initially non-speaking children acquired single words or more, and about half of those minimally speaking at baseline reached word combinations ([Plesa Skwerer et al. 2025, J Clinical Child and Adolescent Psychology](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15374416.2025.2579286); [Drexel summary](https://drexel.edu/news/archive/2026/January/Drexel-Study-Finds-Early-Intervention-Helps-Most-Autistic-Children-Acquire-Spoken-Language), accessed 2026-07-06). The corollary the same literature stresses: some autistic children, by school age, have language ABOVE their age peers.

### The uneven-profile point (peaks and valleys, not uniform delay)

**FACT.** The defining feature is intra-individual scatter. A systematic review states that autistic language profiles "vary across the lifespan to such an extent that, by school age, some individuals display advanced language skills compared to age-matched peers, while some do not develop functional expressive language, even as adults" ([Springer review 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10567-024-00507-1), accessed 2026-07-06). This bimodality within a single group, plus large gaps between domains within a single child, is the norm, not the exception.

**INFERENCE for Homa.** Autism is the strongest possible case for per-skill mapping and against any composite. A whole-child summary would average a peak and a valley into a middle number that describes neither and, per ICP file section 5, "reads as an insult in asynchrony culture." The product must be able to show a high language or reading rung and a low social or motor rung side by side without implying the child is "at" some blended level.

### Motor differences

**FACT.** Motor milestone delays are common and under-recognized in autism. A population-based study of 32,850 autistic 8-year-olds found 71.5 percent (about 72 percent) met criteria for motor milestone delays (fine or gross motor delay, hypotonia, or clumsiness); children with motor delays were evaluated for autism about 8 months earlier ([Pokoski and Durkin et al. 2025, JAMA Pediatrics](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40227744/); [Waisman Center summary](https://www.waisman.wisc.edu/2025/04/24/motor-delays-as-possible-key-indicator-for-autism-diagnosis/), accessed 2026-07-06). A meta-analytic estimate cited alongside it reached 88 percent with significant motor impairment, and across studies motor-deviation prevalence in autism spans a very wide 21 to 100 percent depending on measure and sample. Motor differences (atypical gait, coordination, posture) often appear before social and communication signs and scale with condition severity ([Nature Scientific Reports 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-85348-w), accessed 2026-07-06).

**INFERENCE for Homa.** Gross-motor and fine-motor tolerances should widen for families who mention an autism profile, and the product must not treat a motor lag as informative about cognitive or academic rungs, because the whole point of the profile is that domains dissociate. Lane 01 and 07 own the motor anchors; this is a tolerance and framing overlay.

## 5. Speech sound disorder versus late-normal

**Confidence: High** (cross-references verified corpus lane 01 primaries plus ASHA position).

**FACT (cross-ref lane 01).** Lane 01 already carries the objective norms clinicians use. Crowe and McLeod 2020 (18,907 US children, 90 percent criterion): most English consonants acquired by age 5;0, with r, voiced th, and zh by 5;11 and voiceless th by 6;11. Hustad et al. 2021: median child is 50 percent intelligible to strangers (multiword) at 34 months, 75 percent at 46, 90 percent at 62, with a floor of "at least 50 percent intelligible by 48 months" (`01-early-childhood-2-5.md`, accessed 2026-07-06). Lane 01 also notes the old clinical rule of thumb (50 percent intelligible at 2, 75 at 3, 100 by 4, from Coplan and Gleason 1988 parent ratings) overstates what strangers actually decode.

**FACT (the ASHA distinction).** ASHA and school-eligibility guidance distinguish late-normal from disordered not by a single age cut-point but by three combined tests: (1) errors persist beyond the typical acquisition window for the specific sound, (2) intelligibility or participation is affected, and (3) the errors are not explained by dialect or regional variation. ASHA explicitly cautions that speech sound acquisition "is too complex to be reduced to a single data point" and that best practice integrates the RANGE of acquisition ages, error patterns, intelligibility, and stimulability ([ASHA Practice Portal, Speech Sound Disorders](https://www.asha.org/Practice-Portal/clinical-Topics/Articulation-and-Phonology/); [ASHA developmental-norms guidance](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2018_PERS-SIG1-2018-0014), accessed 2026-07-06). A representative school-eligibility rule requires articulation errors "persisting at least 1 year behind expectancy compared to current developmental norms" plus documented adverse educational impact ([TN speech-or-language eligibility guidance](https://www.tn.gov/content/dam/tn/education/special-education/eligibility/se_speech_or_language_impairment_evaluation_guidance.pdf), accessed 2026-07-06). ASHA's own public rule of thumb: familiar people understand a child by age 3, most people by age 4 ([RCSLT clinical information](https://www.rcslt.org/speech-and-language-therapy/clinical-information/speech-sound-disorders/), accessed 2026-07-06).

**INFERENCE for Homa.** The "persists past the window AND affects being understood AND is not dialect" test is a clinician's call, not the product's (D5). What Homa can safely do is anchor speech and language rungs to the verified lane 01 norms (Hustad, Crowe and McLeod) with wide tolerance, and, if a family volunteers a concern, frame it as observable ("which sounds are still settling") without ever crossing into "your child has a disorder." The r-sound not being clean at age 6 is inside the documented normal window (acquired by 5;11 at the 90 percent criterion) and must never read as a problem.

## 6. Dyscalculia

**Confidence: Med-High** for prevalence; **Med** for the core-deficit account (the field openly disagrees).

**FACT.** Developmental dyscalculia affects about 3 to 7 percent of people. The German clinical review states "3 to 7 percent of all children, adolescents, and adults suffer from dyscalculia" ([Haberstroh and Schulte-Korne 2019, Dtsch Arztebl Int](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6440373/), accessed 2026-07-06); a PLOS Biology paper repeats "3 percent to 7 percent" ([Kuhl et al. 2021, PLOS Biology](https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pbio.3001407), accessed 2026-07-06). Butterworth's cross-national prevalence studies landed at 3.5 to 4 percent (Cuba), 5 to 6 percent (Israel), and about 7 percent (Australia), and he estimates 3.5 to 7 percent overall ([Made For Math interview summary of Butterworth](https://madeformath.com/number-sense-subitizing/), accessed 2026-07-06). Comorbidity with dyslexia is high: one Bavarian study found combined dyscalculia-plus-dyslexia prevalence of 5.98 percent, with about 45 percent of dyscalculic children also dyslexic ([Haberstroh and Schulte-Korne 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6440373/), accessed 2026-07-06).

**FACT (core-deficit research, with the disagreement shown).** The leading account is a core deficit in "number sense," the intuitive, non-verbal representation of quantity, plausibly seated in the intraparietal sulcus. Evidence: dyscalculic children show impaired magnitude comparison and slowed, effortful subitizing, with 10-year-olds sometimes performing like typical 5-year-olds on approximate-number tasks ([Wilson and Dehaene, "Number Sense and Dyscalculia"](https://www.unicog.org/publications/WilsonDehaene_inPress_Final_HBDB.pdf), accessed 2026-07-06). BUT this is contested. A large study found "no evidence for a core deficit in developmental dyscalculia," favoring a multiple-deficit model in which number sense, symbolic access, and working memory each contribute and no single deficit explains every case ([Mammarella et al. 2021, JCPP, as cited in PLOS Biology](https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pbio.3001407), accessed 2026-07-06).

**FACT and INFERENCE (trajectory).** Where documented, dyscalculic children are often described as running about 2 or more years behind peers in numerical skills, and the difficulty is persistent (the German review links poor math ability to leaving school early and lower employment) ([Haberstroh and Schulte-Korne 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6440373/), accessed 2026-07-06). INFERENCE for Homa: subitizing and magnitude-comparison rungs (lane 01's early-math trajectory levels, Clements and Sarama) are the observable substrate, and math tolerance should widen for a family that reports persistent number struggles, framed as "which number skills are building."

## 7. Dysgraphia and handwriting

**Confidence: High** for prevalence and overlap; **High** for the direction of the typing-bypass evidence, **Med** for its magnitude.

**FACT.** Developmental dysgraphia (impaired acquisition of writing, especially handwriting) is estimated at 7 to 15 percent of school-age children ([Dohla and Heim 2016, as cited in McCloskey and Rapp 2017, "Developmental Dysgraphia: An Overview and Framework for Research," PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6238209/); [USNH Pressbooks chapter](https://pressbooks.usnh.edu/understandingandsupportinglearnerswithdisabilities/chapter/dysgraphia/), accessed 2026-07-06).

**FACT.** It rarely occurs alone. Between 30 and 50 percent of children with dysgraphia also have dyslexia or ADHD, and it co-occurs with developmental coordination disorder and autism; the overlap is a major reason dysgraphia is under-diagnosed (teachers attribute the writing trouble to the other condition) ([Pediatric Communication Solutions summary of comorbidity studies](https://pcs-ok.com/why-writing-disorders-are-often-overlooked-in-school/); [MDPI 2025 review](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9067/12/5/542), accessed 2026-07-06).

**FACT (typing as bypass).** The accommodation research supports keyboarding as a way to bypass transcription difficulty: by making letter production simpler, keyboarding can free working-memory resources for composing, yields consistently legible text, and eases revision ([Berninger group work summarized in transcription-mode study](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/ab23/a1fa4514fc62b73d1b999e15642ebc8de4f7.pdf); [USNH chapter on assistive tech](https://pressbooks.usnh.edu/understandingandsupportinglearnerswithdisabilities/chapter/dysgraphia/), accessed 2026-07-06). The caveat in the same literature: typing is not an automatic fix and works best paired with explicit touch-typing instruction, since untrained keyboarding can be slower than handwriting.

**INFERENCE for Homa.** Handwriting and composition are separate skills (lane 04 already separates transcription from composition). A dysgraphic child may compose rich ideas while handwriting lags badly; a writing map that conflates the two would under-report the child's actual thinking. The product should let handwriting/fine-motor rungs and written-expression rungs move independently, and copy should treat typing as a legitimate way a child shows what they know, never as a lesser output.

## 8. Giftedness and twice-exceptionality

**Confidence: High** for the Columbus Group and NAGC framing; **Med** for the magnitude of documented spreads (illustrative expert examples, not population distributions).

**FACT (asynchrony).** The Columbus Group's 1991 definition centers asynchrony: "Giftedness is asynchronous development in which advanced cognitive abilities and heightened intensity combine to create inner experiences and awareness that are qualitatively different from the norm. This asynchrony increases with higher intellectual capacity" ([NAGC, Asynchronous Development](https://dev.nagc.org/resources-publications/resources-parents/social-emotional-issues/asynchronous-development); [NJ DOE](https://www.nj.gov/education/standards/stamp/gt_asynchronous_development.shtml), accessed 2026-07-06). The higher the ability, the wider the internal spread.

**FACT (documented spread, illustrative).** NAGC gives a worked example: "it is not unusual for a 7-year-old highly gifted child to be reading at a 6th grade level, performing math tasks at a 4th grade level, and have fine motor skills at a 2nd grade level" ([NAGC](https://dev.nagc.org/resources-publications/resources-parents/social-emotional-issues/asynchronous-development), accessed 2026-07-06). In grade terms that single child spans from about 5 grades ahead (reading) to about 1 grade behind (fine motor) at the same moment. A commonly cited illustration: an 8-year-old gifted child with the cognitive ability of a 14-year-old, gross motor of a 10-year-old, and fine motor of a 6-year-old ([Right Steps for Kids summary](https://rightstepsforkids.com/gifted-2e/), accessed 2026-07-06). These are expert illustrations of the pattern, not measured population spreads.

**FACT (2e masking).** NAGC recognizes three kinds of twice-exceptional students: identified gifted students who also have a disability; students identified with a disability whose giftedness is unseen; and students whose gifts and disabilities mask each other, leaving them looking average ([IDA, "Gifted and Dyslexic," summarizing NAGC](https://dyslexiaida.org/gifted-and-dyslexic-identifying-and-instructing-the-twice-exceptional-student/); [Davidson Institute](https://www.davidsongifted.org/gifted-blog/twice-exceptional-definition-characteristics-identification/), accessed 2026-07-06). The masking is bidirectional: "Dyslexia may mask giftedness, and giftedness may mask dyslexia," so a gifted-plus-dyslexic child can read at exactly grade level (ability compensating for the deficit) and be invisible on both counts.

**INFERENCE for Homa.** This is the single most important framing consequence in the whole lane. Grade-level performance is not evidence that a child is neurotypical or unremarkable; for a 2e child it can be the specific signature of two large forces cancelling out. The product must never treat "reads right at grade level" as "nothing to see here," and must be able to surface a high rung and a low rung in the same child without averaging them. This is also why months-ahead as a single number is actively harmful for the gifted and 2e segment: it collapses exactly the spread these families most want to see.

## 9. Anxiety and school refusal

**Confidence: Med** (clear direction of effect; magnitudes come from mixed primary and secondary sources).

**FACT.** Anxiety depresses measured performance below actual ability. Highly test-anxious students score roughly 12 percentile points below equal-ability peers, and trait anxiety impairs the working memory that reading comprehension and math problem-solving rely on ([processing-efficiency-theory study, Owens et al. 2008, Anxiety, Stress and Coping](https://doi.org/10.1080/10615800701847823); [Ignite Achievement Academy summary of Mazzone et al. 2007](https://igniteachievementacademy.com/blog/the-link-between-anxiety-and-academic-performance-solutions-for-parents), accessed 2026-07-06). Verbal working memory accounted for about half of the anxiety-to-achievement association in one child sample.

**FACT.** School refusal and absenteeism are linked to lower reading and math scores and grade repetition, forming a compounding cycle ([Gonzalvez et al. 2020, Frontiers in Public Health](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2020.598915); [Frontiers in Psychology 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.01471/full), accessed 2026-07-06).

**INFERENCE for Homa.** A measured position can UNDER-read a capable but anxious child, especially on a hard day. This is a strong argument for Homa's core mechanic (the parent answers from ongoing observation; the child never sits a timed test), which sidesteps the anxiety-depresses-scores problem entirely, matching the ICP file's finding that timed, stranger-administered, threshold-attached testing is the wounding pattern. It is also a reason to treat any single reading as provisional and to weight the parent's "what I have seen over time" over a one-moment snapshot.

### Copy guidance: the asynchrony-respecting frame this segment embraces

**Embraced (from the ND/2e segment's own mouths, ICP file section 5 and 7).** Per-skill positions ("blending," "regrouping," "which sounds are settling"), progress against the child's own earlier self ("he is about 1 to 1.5 years behind, that is HUGE progress"), and the word "asynchronous" itself (a 2e-homeschooling parent used it approvingly). The segment celebrates relative gains while still below grade level and treats evaluation as strengths-discovery when it is framed that way. Strengths named before any gap; gap named in skill terms, never position terms; exactly one nudge with material attached (ICP file section 11, E4 rubric).

**Wounds (ICP file section 7 DON'T list, sharpest for this segment).** Whole-child verdicts and single grade numbers (an "insult in asynchrony culture," ICP file section 5), the word "behind" or "delayed" from an outside voice, percentiles as verdicts, and any surprise deficit readout with no path. The asymmetry is total: a parent may say "behind" about her own child freely; the product sits permanently on the outside of that line and never says it.

**The two hard product rules this lane reinforces.** (1) Never render a whole-child summary or single number; the entire segment's reality is that domains dissociate, and a composite erases the exact information they want. (2) Grade-level performance is not "nothing to see": for a 2e child it is the signature of a masked gift and a masked difficulty, so the product must keep surfacing per-skill peaks and valleys even when the average looks unremarkable. Both rules also protect the neurotypical-behind mom, so they cost nothing to apply universally.

**The boundary to state before she does (ICP file section 5, D5).** This segment will correctly note that Homa's conversational mapping is not a diagnosis and cannot feed an IEP or accommodations paperwork. The product should say that plainly and treat real evaluation as the gift this community considers it, never positioning itself as a substitute for it.
