# Barton Reading & Spelling System

## Snapshot

The Barton Reading & Spelling System is the homeschool market's reference
answer for serious dyslexia: a one-on-one, fully scripted, ten-level
Orton-Gillingham program that an untrained parent can deliver because every
lesson procedure is demonstrated on video first. Susan Barton, who left a
twenty-year computer career after watching her nephew Ben reach tenth grade
reading at a second-grade level despite years of special education, built
the system and founded its publisher, Bright Solutions for Dyslexia, in
1998. Barton is designed for students age five through adult who have
dyslexia or a suspected reading disability, and it is deliberately a
tutoring system rather than a curriculum: two to four one-hour sessions per
week, one tutor and one student, for two to five years. In dyslexia forums
and Facebook groups it functions as the standard of care for home
intervention, the program families escalate to when gentler options (often
All About Reading) have not been enough.

## Reputation and rankings

- Reviewer verdict is remarkably uniform: the "gold standard" framing
  recurs across Homeschool Tools' review synthesis, Marianne Sunderland's
  Homeschooling with Dyslexia (the niche's most influential blog), and
  years of parent reviews. Its distinctive claim is professional-grade OG
  fidelity without professional certification: Wilson and mainstream OG
  practice expect trained and certified tutors, while Barton's built-in
  video training exists precisely so parents can skip that gatekeeping.
- The evidence record is thinner than the reputation. The What Works
  Clearinghouse identified 13 Barton studies (1989-2009) and found none
  that met its evidence standards, so WWC draws no conclusion either way.
  The method family it belongs to (structured literacy) is well supported;
  the specific product's independent evidence base is not. The publisher's
  research page argues its own case.
- Community sentiment (Reddit dyslexia and homeschool threads, Well-Trained
  Mind forums, long-form blog reviews) is positive with three standing
  complaints: it is expensive, it is dry (scripted, repetitive, no
  storybook charm; "boring" is the recurring child verdict, including from
  Sunderland's own profoundly dyslexic son), and the spelling-rule density
  from Level 3 onward overwhelms some children, especially those with
  memory or processing issues on top of dyslexia. A Simply Charlotte Mason
  forum thread records a family stopping mid-Level 3 for exactly that
  reason.
- Trajectory: stable to rising. The science-of-reading wave (see the
  language arts comparison doc) keeps validating explicit, structured
  literacy, and Barton's decades of word-of-mouth make it the default
  escalation target. Virtual charter schools increasingly hold site
  licenses and lend levels to enrolled home educators.

## Pedagogy and method

Orton-Gillingham lineage, adapted for zero-training tutors: explicit,
systematic, sequential, multisensory, and cumulative. Two design choices
define it. First, spelling leads: the publisher states the sequence follows
a logical spelling order, teaching students to read by teaching them to
spell through sounding out, never through memorizing word lists. Second,
everything is controlled: students only ever see words built from patterns
already taught, and the tutor manual contains enough material to teach
every lesson twice without repeating a word, sentence, or story. Grammar
basics, morphology, prosody, and fluency work are folded in along the way.
Sessions run about an hour, minimum twice a week (the publisher's stated
floor is two hours of tutoring per week); at that pace the full ten levels
take roughly 18 to 36 months for mild dyslexia, 36 to 48 for moderate, and
48 to 60 for profound, with more frequent sessions cutting the timeline
proportionally. Mastery gates everything: a student does not leave a lesson
until its procedures are solid, and level numbers are explicitly not grade
levels. A student who completes all ten levels reads and spells at roughly
a mid-ninth-grade level, which the publisher notes is above the average
adult reading level. Barton also instructs families not to run any other
reading or spelling program simultaneously.

## Structure and scope

Ten levels, purchased one at a time as the student progresses:

1. Phonemic Awareness
2. Consonants and Short Vowels
3. Closed and Unit Syllables
4. Syllable Division and Vowel Teams
5. Prefixes and Suffixes
6. Six Reasons for Silent-E
7. Vowel-R Syllables
8. Advanced Vowel Teams
9. Influences of Foreign Languages
10. Greek Words and Latin Roots

Each level contains 5 to 15 lessons (Level 1 has five; Level 6 has
fourteen), and lessons routinely take multiple sessions. Levels 1 and 2 are
short on purpose (phonemic awareness, then single letters and digraphs) and
some students clear each in weeks; Level 3 is where the program thickens
into syllable types and named spelling rules, and families report settling
there for many months. Each level ships as a box: tutor manual, five to six
hours of tutor training (DVD or streaming), color-coded letter tiles
(cumulative from Level 2), and student materials. A Barton Tiles app for
iPad and Android replaces the physical tiles for families who prefer a
tablet. Two gates precede purchase: a 5-minute tutor screening (the tutor
must discriminate sounds accurately; no degree or experience required) and
a 10-minute student screening. Students must be at least five, speak
English at a second-grade level or better, and have an IQ of 71 or higher;
the program is explicitly not for receptive or expressive language
disorders. A student who fails Part C of the screening (auditory
discrimination or memory) is referred out to Foundation in Sounds or a
LiPS-trained professional first (see the [LiPS profile](lips.md)).

## A lesson up close

The parent's preparation is front-loaded per level, not per lesson: watch
the training video for the level (Susan Barton demonstrates every lesson
procedure with a student, including the mistakes students typically make
and how to correct them), then open the manual and teach. Manual pages use
a two-column layout: a wide column with word-for-word scripting for tutors
who want full support, and a narrow column with just the summary steps for
tutors who have found their feet. A session works through a fixed sequence
of procedures that ramp from easiest to hardest: quick review of taught
phonograms and sight words, new teaching with the color-coded tiles built
and manipulated on the table (or in the app), controlled reading practice,
and controlled spelling practice, with the student writing words, phrases,
and sentences made exclusively of taught patterns. The multisensory loop
is constant: hear it, say it, build it with tiles, write it. There are no
decodable storybooks and no activity-book games; practice pages are plain
lists and sentences, which is where both the program's power (nothing
untaught ever appears) and its dryness live. A completed session produces
tile work, a few written lines, and forward motion through a lesson that
may take two or three more sessions to finish. This section describes
structure only; the publisher posts sample manual pages, a lesson-step
outline, and demo videos (linked below).

## Materials and visual design

Deliberately clinical. Tutor manuals are utilitarian workhorses built
around the two-column script; student pages are black and white,
list-driven, and
low-stimulation, closer to a therapy protocol than a curriculum with a
brand personality. The color in the system is functional: letter tiles are
color-coded by phonogram category, and tile organization on the whiteboard
is part of the method. Boxes are sturdy and the physical footprint grows
level by level (cumulative tiles, multiple manuals). The aesthetic signals
exactly what the product is: an intervention, not an invitation. Families
coming from All About Reading's storybook warmth consistently notice the
difference; families who need results at any cost read the plainness as
seriousness.

## Pricing

As of Jul 2026, from the publisher's
[orders and pricing page](https://bartonreading.com/price-3/):

- Levels 1 and 2: $350 each. Levels 3 through 10: $400 each.
- Bundle discount: $50 off each level when buying two or more at once.
- All-digital versions (streaming training plus digital materials plus the
  Barton Tiles app) cost the same as physical.
- Extra tile sets run $10 to $20 per level. Site licenses for schools and
  centers: $1,200 (Levels 1-2) or $1,700 (Levels 3-10) each.
- Full-system cost if purchased new, all ten levels: about $3,900 before
  bundle discounts. Spread over the three-to-five-year timeline, a typical
  year touches one to three levels ($400 to $1,200).
- The resale market is the price's escape valve and a community
  institution: completed levels routinely resell for about $50 under
  purchase price in Barton Facebook groups, making net cost per level
  modest for patient families. Reviewers also warn against selling too
  soon, since students sometimes need to revisit earlier levels.
- Reviewers report the publisher has historically swapped a quickly
  finished early level for the next one; treat that as goodwill practice,
  not policy, and confirm with the publisher.
- Comparison anchor that recurs in parent write-ups: professional OG
  tutoring at roughly $70 per session twice a week runs about $560 per
  month per child, so one Barton level costs less than a month of
  outsourced tutoring.

## Why families choose it

- It is the "we cannot afford $600 a month of tutoring" answer that still
  feels like the real thing: same OG substance, parent-delivered, with
  training included.
- Severity. When AAR or a school intervention has failed, Barton is the
  next rung that does not require finding and vetting a professional.
- The scripting removes fear: a parent who cannot spell (including
  dyslexic parents, whom the publisher explicitly welcomes as tutors) can
  still deliver the lesson by reading the wide column.
- The screenings, the free advice line, and Susan Barton's personal
  reputation for answering emails build unusual trust before purchase.
- Levels have no grade labels, which spares a twelve-year-old working on
  short vowels the daily reminder of being "behind."

## Why families leave it

- The child rebels against the dryness: no stories, no games, heavy
  repetition. Some families migrate back down to AAR for its readers and
  activity pages, accepting less intensity for more willingness.
- Rule overload from Level 3 up, especially for kids with comorbid memory
  or processing challenges; some settle for functional reading and stop
  formal intervention.
- Parent stamina. Two to five years of multiple weekly one-on-one hours is
  a marathon; when it breaks, families outsource to a certified Barton
  tutor (the publisher maintains referral lists) or a Lindamood-Bell style
  clinic if budget allows.
- Screen-outs: students who cannot pass Part C of the screening must do
  LiPS or Foundation in Sounds first, and some families stall in that
  detour.
- Price, upfront: even with resale, $400 per level filters out families
  who need intervention most, a tension the comparison doc takes up.

## Competitive notes for Homa

- Vocabulary to know: level numbers (never grades), tutor screening,
  student screening, tiles, the named spelling rules taught from Level 3
  onward, certification tiers (Certified, Advanced, Master) for
  professional tutors. "We are in Barton 4" states a precise skill
  position: syllable division in progress, vowel-r and advanced teams not
  yet taught.
- Respect the exclusivity rule. Barton instructs families to run no other
  reading or spelling program alongside. Homa should never suggest
  supplemental phonics content to a Barton family; the safe lanes are
  fluency-neutral supports (audiobooks for content subjects, scribing
  strategies for written output) and everything outside reading and
  spelling.
- The controlled-text constraint is the materials lesson: everything a
  Barton student reads is built only from taught patterns. If Homa ever
  generates practice or decodable text for structured-literacy families,
  the constraint engine (pattern inventory in, only-those-patterns out) is
  the entire product, and Barton proves buyers notice fidelity.
- Progress reporting gap: Barton families live in a multi-year tunnel with
  no dashboards, only finished lessons and levels. Reflecting non-linear
  progress back to the parent in skill terms (what Level 4 completion
  means against the map, without grade shaming) is squarely Homa's lane
  and matches the growth-only display law.
- The two-column script (full words vs summary) is a strong pattern for
  any parent-facing teaching support Homa generates: same content, two
  confidence levels, parent chooses.
