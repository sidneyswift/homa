# Handwriting Without Tears

## Snapshot

Handwriting Without Tears (HWT), from Learning Without Tears, is the
best-known handwriting curriculum in both classrooms and homeschools:
an occupational-therapist-designed, developmentally sequenced program
covering pre-writing through cursive, one workbook per grade. Its
signatures are a simplified upright letter style (no slant), teaching
letters in difficulty order rather than A to Z, memorable formation
language, double-line writing paper, and hands-on tools (wood letter
pieces, slate chalkboards). It is the default recommendation for young
writers generally and for fine-motor strugglers and left-handed kids
specifically. In LA stacks it is almost always the fourth purchase beside
reading, spelling, and writing programs. The 2025 edition refreshed the
whole line (and broke compatibility with older editions).

## Reputation and rankings

- Decades of classroom adoption plus a Johns Hopkins (Center for Research
  and Reform in Education) study the publisher cites showing significant
  handwriting gains; in homeschool space it is the perennial roundup
  winner and the OT community's household name.
- Community sentiment: praised as the program that works when a child
  physically struggles to write, and for being quick (10 to 15 minutes a
  day). Standing criticisms: the letterforms look plain or "unattractive"
  to traditionalists (upright cursive especially), the proprietary
  vocabulary and tools feel gimmicky to some, and grade-labeled covers
  bother families with older remediating kids.
- Trajectory: stable incumbent, with the 2025 edition modernizing art and
  adding multicultural representation; competitors (Zaner-Bloser
  traditional look, TGTB's in-house handwriting, Logic of English's Rhythm
  of Handwriting) chip at the edges.

## Pedagogy and method

Occupational-therapy-first: gross motor to fine motor, capitals before
lowercase (developmentally easier strokes first), letters grouped by stroke
family and taught with kid-memorable formation cues, and multisensory
tools (wood pieces to build letters, wet-dry-try on a slate chalkboard)
before pencil work. The double-line paper system is the visual scaffold:
a base line and a midline only, so children place letters by bumping the
lines rather than navigating three-line paper. Sessions are short and
frequent by design. Print runs PK through grade 2; the cursive transition
starts at grade 2 or 3 (Cursive Kickoff at 2, Cursive Handwriting at 3),
matching the national grade 2 to 4 cursive window. It is one of the only
LA product lines that is fully grade-labeled, one book per grade, no
placement test.

## Structure and scope

The ladder, per the publisher's family catalog: My First School Book
(Pre-K), Kick Start Kindergarten (transitional K), Letters and Numbers for
Me (K), My Printing Book (grade 1), Printing Power (grade 2) or Cursive
Kickoff (cursive start at 2), Cursive Handwriting (3), Cursive Success (4),
Can-Do Cursive (5), Cursive and Beyond (6 plus). Components per level: a
consumable student workbook, an optional teacher's guide, and optional
manipulatives (wood pieces set, slate, Stamp and See screen, double-line
paper pads). The grade table and the cursive-window finding are already
recorded in

section 11 (the bank already cites hwt as a source for writing items).

## A lesson up close

A K-year day: five to fifteen minutes. The parent demonstrates one letter
using the formation language while the child builds it from wood pieces or
traces it large on the slate (wet sponge, then dry, then chalk: the
wet-dry-try ritual), then the workbook page: a large model letter with
numbered stroke arrows, a row of gray letters to trace, a row to write
independently, and a small illustration to color if the child wants. Pages
are landscape, uncluttered, one skill each, with the double lines doing
the guidance. Upper books swap manipulatives for more writing volume:
words, sentences, and short passages in print or cursive, still one focus
per page. A completed week is a few workbook pages and visibly steadier
letters; there is no test apparatus anywhere in the program.

## Materials and visual design

Consumable workbooks around $12 to 20: black-and-white interiors with
simple, friendly line art (2025 edition adds updated covers and more
diverse characters), landscape orientation, heavy uncluttered pages, and
the double-line ruling everywhere. The wood pieces and slate are sturdy
classroom-grade objects. The letterforms themselves are the brand's most
polarizing design choice: simple, vertical, continuous-stroke letters
built for legibility, not beauty. The aesthetic signals therapeutic
practicality: this is the program you buy so writing stops being a fight,
not the one you buy for elegant penmanship.

## Pricing

As of Jul 2026: student workbooks about $11.85 to 20 depending on retailer
and edition; teacher's guides about $25 (useful but skippable for
experienced parents); manipulatives a la carte (slate chalkboard about
$5.25, double-lined blackboard about $15, wood pieces set about $33).
A minimal year is one workbook; a typical first year with tools runs $40
to 70, then $12 to 20 per grade after. Workbooks are per-child
consumables; tools last forever. 2025-edition incompatibility with older
workbooks matters for hand-me-down plans. Price pages:
[family catalog](https://www.lwtears.com/family-catalog),
[Rainbow Resource 2025 edition hub](https://www.rainbowresource.com/handwriting-without-tears-2025-edition).

## Why families choose it

- It works for the kid who cries over writing: OT design, big motor entry
  points, short sessions.
- Ten minutes a day, near-zero prep, done by breakfast.
- Left-handed guidance and flat-opening books are quietly excellent.
- The grade-labeled ladder makes buying brainless: one book, once a year.

## Why families leave it

- Families wanting classic slanted penmanship switch to Zaner-Bloser,
  cursive-first programs, or TGTB's handwriting line for the look.
- Once handwriting is fluent many families simply stop buying a program
  and fold practice into copywork (the Charlotte Mason and Brave Writer
  pattern).
- Tool cost and clutter for what is ultimately tracing practice pushes
  minimalists to free printable paper and any letter chart.

## Competitive notes for Homa

- Vocabulary to know: the book names double as grade markers (My Printing
  Book means grade 1; the crosswalk table maps them), double lines,
  wet-dry-try, wood pieces, Cursive Kickoff vs Cursive Handwriting as the
  print-to-cursive fork.
- Materials lesson: the double-line scaffold and one-skill-per-page
  layout are proven patterns for any handwriting practice Homa generates;
  formation cue language is proprietary expression and must not be copied,
  but the concept of naming stroke moves is free.
- Gap Homa can fill: HWT practice content is generic (letters, stock
  words). Generating double-line practice pages using the child's own
  spelling words, interests, or copywork sentences merges the handwriting
  slot with the rest of the stack, something no print workbook can do.
- Handwriting is where grade labels survive in LA; a family naming an HWT
  book has told you the working grade band, useful for onboarding
  inference.
