# Institute for Excellence in Writing (Structure and Style)

## Snapshot

IEW, founded in 1994 and fronted by Andrew Pudewa, is the dominant writing
program in US homeschooling. Its Structure and Style method (derived from James
Webster's Blended Structure and Style in Composition) teaches composition
through nine structural models and a checklist of stylistic techniques, taught
today mostly through Structure and Style for Students (SSS), a 24-week video
course in three levels where Pudewa teaches the child directly. The method's
signature move is the key word outline: students never face a blank page; they
take three-word-per-line notes from a source text and rewrite from the notes.
It is beloved by families who want a system, resented by families who find the
checklists mechanical, and essentially unavoidable in any writing-curriculum
conversation. IEW also publishes Fix It! Grammar and the Phonetic Zoo spelling
program, making it a multi-subject language arts publisher.

## Reputation and rankings

- Teaching Writing: Structure and Style has topped the Practical Homeschooling
  Reader Awards as best writing program for 17-plus consecutive years (per
  IEW's awards page, through the 2023 awards).
- Cathy Duffy Top Picks (2022 list); her SSS review credits Pudewa as "a
  fantastic teacher" and calls the program widely acclaimed.
- First place, Old Schoolhouse 2020 Excellence in Education awards for both
  writing and grammar.
- Community sentiment: strongly polarized in a stable way. Fans (including
  13-year users) report reluctant writers transformed by having a repeatable
  process. The standing criticism, in Reddit threads and long-form reviews, is
  that the output feels formulaic: every paper must contain the required
  dress-ups, so early IEW papers read templated, and some kids produce awkward
  prose to satisfy the checklist. Defenders answer that the formula is
  scaffolding that falls away. Both things are true at different stages.
- Trajectory: stable at the top. The 2019-2022 SSS video courses modernized
  delivery and the 2026 third edition of TWSS refreshed the teacher training.

## Pedagogy and method

Structure and style, taught incrementally and repeated. The nine structural
units (1 note making and outlines, 2 writing from notes, 3 retelling narrative
stories, 4 summarizing a reference, 5 writing from pictures, 6 summarizing
multiple references, 7 inventive writing, 8 formal essay models, 9 formal
critique) recur every year at rising difficulty, an explicit spiral. Style is
layered in as "dress-ups" (strong verb, quality adjective, -ly adverb, because
clause, who/which clause, www.asia clause), sentence openers, "decorations,"
and banned words (said, go, good), each required on the assignment checklist
once introduced. Source texts do the inventing so students can practice
structure; original content ("inventive writing") is deliberately delayed to
unit 7. Assessment is the checklist itself: a paper is complete when every
required element is present, which makes grading unusually objective and is
half the reason parents love it. Lineage: Webster's structure-and-style
composition research by way of Pudewa's Suzuki-method background (small
increments, mastery through repetition, cheerful drill).

## Structure and scope

Two ways in. The classic path is Teaching Writing: Structure and Style (TWSS),
a teacher-training seminar (third edition 2026) after which the parent teaches
using theme-based writing lesson books (American history, ancients, Bible
heroes, fables, and so on, roughly $50-80 each). The now-dominant path is
Structure and Style for Students (SSS): Level A (grades 3 to 5), Level B
(grades 6 to 8), Level C (grades 9 plus), each a Year 1 of 24 weekly video
lessons plus a Year 2 continuation. Year 1 Level A covers units 1 to 7; Levels
B and C cover all nine; Level C adds literary analysis. Placement is by
reading level and experience, with IEW's own guidance to start at or below
level; reluctant 6th to 8th graders commonly start in Level A. Primary Arts of
Language (PAL) covers K to 2 reading and writing upstream of the method. For
level-to-skill mapping (IEW A/B/C against the writing ladder), the repo
crosswalk already did the work: see
[14-curriculum-crosswalk-ela.md](../../../homa-benchmarks/14-curriculum-crosswalk-ela.md)
section 9 and the master table.

## A lesson up close

A typical SSS week from the parent's seat. Monday: the student watches Pudewa
teach for roughly 45 to 60 minutes (jokes, playacting, whiteboard work), with
the parent encouraged to watch along; the student packet has the week's source
text and blank key word outline page ready. The KWO page is a numbered
skeleton: Roman numerals for paragraphs, numbered lines below, three words per
line maximum (plus unlimited symbols and abbreviations, a rule kids enjoy
gaming). Tuesday through Thursday: the student rewrites from the outline, about
20 to 30 minutes a day, checking the composition checklist as they go. The
checklist is the page to understand: it lists the required structural elements
(topic sentence, clincher that echoes it) and the required style elements with
per-item point values; nothing subjective appears on it. Friday or the
following Monday: the parent edits (IEW instructs parents to fix mechanics
without grading creativity) and the student produces a final draft. A completed
week yields one polished paragraph or composition, one marked checklist, and a
binder that thickens satisfyingly across the year. Portable Walls (a tri-fold
desk reference of word lists and models) sits open the whole time so the
student never hunts for a strong verb.

## Materials and visual design

Function over beauty, and proudly so. Student binders with tabbed packets,
spiral-bound teacher's manuals, black-and-white worksheets dominated by lined
writing space, source texts set in plain serif type. Charts and word lists (the
famous banned-words list, dress-up definitions) do the visual work; there is
almost no illustration. The videos carry the personality: Pudewa in front of a
whiteboard with a live class, production values closer to a filmed seminar than
a produced course. The aesthetic signals rigor and system: this is the
curriculum that looks like it will get your kid to a five-paragraph essay, not
the one that photographs well on Instagram. Fix It! Grammar student books are
similarly utilitarian: one four-line passage per day with errors to hunt.

## Pricing

As of Jul 2026, from IEW's SSS FAQ and product pages:

- SSS Year 1 (any level): Basic $169 (teacher's manual, student packet and
  binder, 24 videos as Forever Streaming or DVD); Basic Plus $189 (adds
  Portable Walls and a Fix It! Grammar teacher/student combo); Premier $289
  (adds A Word Write Now thesaurus and the full TWSS seminar with streaming).
- Additional student: packet and binder only (roughly $45 to 65 depending on
  level; verify per product page).
- Fix It! Grammar: $29 per level as a teacher/student combo, $19 each
  separately, six levels.
- Theme-based writing lessons (the TWSS path): roughly $50 to 80 per book,
  teacher and student.
- Streaming is "forever" for the purchasing household but nontransferable,
  which suppresses resale of streaming packages; DVDs and print resell well.
- IEW famously never discounts products; it runs free-shipping promotions
  (late June to early July, and the Twelve Days of Christmas Giving after
  December 26) and honors an any-condition 100 percent refund policy when
  bought direct.

## Why families choose it

- It removes blank-page paralysis: the KWO gives every child a starting move.
- The checklist makes "is this done, is this good" objective for both kid and
  parent; grading stops being a fight.
- Video delivery means the parent does not have to be the writing expert, and
  Pudewa is genuinely funny; kids who dread writing tolerate him.
- It scales through high school and reads as college prep; transcripts and
  accreditation stories circulate in the community.
- One method for all children in the family, forever: parents learn TWSS once.

## Why families leave it

- The formula chafes. Kids with a natural voice feel strangled by mandatory
  dress-ups; the common destination for these families is Brave Writer, the
  philosophical opposite.
- Checklist fatigue and sameness: nine units every year reads as repetitive to
  some students by year three.
- Cost: $169 to 289 per student-year is real money next to a $30 writing book;
  budget families move to WriteShop, Writing With Skill, or nothing.
- The video hour is long for wiggly kids, and the source-text subjects
  (history, animals) bore some children.
- Charlotte Mason purists object on principle: the method is the opposite of
  natural narration (their alternative is WWE or Brave Writer).

## Competitive notes for Homa

- Vocabulary to know: KWO (key word outline), dress-ups, sentence openers,
  decorations, banned words, clincher, TWSS, SSS Level A/B/C, theme books,
  Portable Walls, Fix It. A family saying "we do IEW B" means units 1 to 9 at
  middle school difficulty; the crosswalk row anchors multi-paragraph
  organization as demonstrated and essay-evidence as in progress.
- The checklist is the single best materials pattern to learn: a
  student-facing, binary, per-element completion contract. Homa-generated
  writing tasks could ship an equivalent rubric line so parents never have to
  judge "good" unaided.
- The KWO template (three words per line, symbols free) is a proven
  scaffolding shape for summarizing-from-source tasks.
- Gap Homa can fill: IEW gives no help matching source texts to a child's
  interests (a horse-obsessed kid still outlines the provided article).
  Generating interest-matched source paragraphs at the right reading level,
  formatted for a KWO, slots directly into an IEW family's week.
- Direct overlap warning: IEW sells writing feedback through classes and
  accreditation; Homa's chat-based writing help competes there, so positioning
  should be "fits your IEW method" rather than "replaces it."
