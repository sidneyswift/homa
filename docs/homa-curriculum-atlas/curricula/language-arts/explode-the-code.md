# Explode the Code

## Snapshot

Explode the Code (ETC) is the workhorse phonics workbook series of American
homeschooling: three preliteracy primers (Get Ready, Get Set, Go for the
Code) followed by workbooks 1 through 8, with optional half-books (1.5 to
6.5) for extra practice. It comes from a classroom publisher (EPS Learning),
has been in print for decades, and holds its place not by beauty or method
novelty but by being cheap, systematic, and something a child does mostly
alone. In homeschool stacks it is usually the independent-practice layer
beside a taught reading program, or the entire phonics plan for an
easy-reading kid. There is also an adaptive online version. Grade labels are
deliberately absent from the covers so older remediating students feel no
stigma.

## Reputation and rankings

- A Cathy Duffy Top Pick historically and a permanent fixture of phonics
  roundups; she notes the online version may appeal more than the books
  because it tailors assignments and fights boredom.
- Literacy-adjacent reviewers praise its scope and sequence and heavy
  reinforcement; the universal criticisms are repetitive format and dated
  pencil-drawn art, with "so boring" the standing quote in parent forums.
  A secondary complaint: some picture prompts are ambiguous enough that
  kids need help deciphering what the item wants.
- Trajectory: stable legacy; the 2nd edition and the online product keep it
  current, and its price keeps it recommended.

## Pedagogy and method

Systematic phonics practice in print: each book drills a defined skill band
through repeated page formats until automatic. It is practice-first direct
instruction; there is minimal teaching text on the page and a companion
teacher's guide exists but many homeschoolers skip it. Mastery-based in
structure (books map to skills, half-books re-practice the same skills), and
explicitly not grade-locked: the publisher states grade approximations "are
not meant to be followed strictly." Parent role is startup-and-check: pick
the right book via placement test, then the child works pages independently.
This independence is the entire value proposition, and its limit: ETC
assumes decoding instruction is happening somewhere (a parent, another
program) and supplies the reps.

## Structure and scope

Primers teach consonants, print concepts, and letter formation. Books 1 to 8
run short vowels, blends, digraphs and silent-e, syllable types and endings,
word families and three-letter blends, r-controlled vowels and vowel teams,
soft c and g and silent letters, then a suffix and ending suite (-tion,
-sion, -ous and kin) with short reading passages and comprehension questions
appearing in books 7 and 8. Publisher placement tests coordinate with the
books. The full book-by-book skill table, publisher grade bands, and mapping
to bank skills are already transcribed in
[14-curriculum-crosswalk-ela.md](../../../homa-benchmarks/14-curriculum-crosswalk-ela.md)
section 4; that file also records the endpoint finding (ETC 8 converges with
AAR 4 and LOE Essentials on multisyllable decoding plus morphology).

## A lesson up close

A day is one to three workbook pages, five to fifteen minutes, at the
kitchen table or beside the parent doing something else. Page anatomy is the
brand: half-page items in fixed formats that repeat book after book: circle
the word that matches the picture; write the word under the picture;
silly yes/no comprehension questions about everyday things; match word to
picture; copy the word on a line. Item counts
run eight to twelve per page, black-and-white line art, wide-ruled writing
lines, difficulty ramping only through the phonics content, never the
format. The child finishes pages, the parent spot-checks. A completed week
is a visibly thicker stack of finished pages, which some kids find
satisfying and others find soul-flattening. The online version replaces
pages with adaptive item sets, audio, and typing.

## Materials and visual design

Digest-size (roughly 6 by 9) consumable workbooks, newsprint-quality paper,
simple black-and-white cartoon line art unchanged in spirit since the
1970s, no color anywhere. Covers are flat single-color with the book number
huge, no grade printed. The aesthetic signals cheap, serious reps: it looks
like the phonics equivalent of a math drill book, and families price-anchor
on that look. Nothing to store or assemble beyond pencils.

## Pricing

As of Jul 2026: individual workbooks retail around $10 to 18 depending on
outlet (Christianbook and Rainbow Resource commonly near $10, Amazon
higher); teacher's guides cover two books each and are optional in home
use. A typical year uses two to four books ($20 to 70 total per child, all
consumable). Explode the Code Online runs about $65 per student per year
with sibling discounts (best price historically via Homeschool Buyers
Co-op). No resale value to speak of (written-in consumables). Price pages:
[EPS product page](https://www.epslearning.com/products/explode-the-code-2nd-edition),
[Cathy Duffy's price comparison](https://cathyduffyreviews.com/homeschool-reviews-core-curricula/phonics-reading/phonics-reading-programs/explode-the-code).

## Why families choose it

- Under $15 a book, and the child does it alone: the cheapest real phonics
  reps money buys.
- It composes with anything: the practice layer under AAR, TGTB, 100 Easy
  Lessons, or school.
- No-grade covers make it safe for older strugglers.
- Placement tests and half-books make it easy to meet a child exactly where
  they are.

## Why families leave it

- Boredom: the fixed formats that make it independent also make kids groan;
  families rotate it out or switch to the online version.
- It cannot carry a struggling reader alone (no explicit instruction); those
  families move the teaching load to AAR or LOE and sometimes keep ETC as
  reps.
- Handwriting-heavy pages frustrate kids with fine-motor lag; the online
  version is the workaround.

## Competitive notes for Homa

- Vocabulary to know: book numbers (1 to 8), half-books, primers (Get Ready,
  Get Set, Go), ETC Online. "She is in ETC 3" is a skill statement (silent-e,
  digraphs, vowel teams beginning) per the crosswalk, never a grade.
- Materials lesson: ETC proves families will buy plain if it is cheap and
  self-running; the fixed-format-page pattern is also the thing Homa's
  generated practice can beat: same skill targeting, but varied formats,
  the child's interests in the content, and no per-child rebuy.
- ETC is the single most natural product for Homa-generated materials to
  substitute: a family asking for more silent-e practice pages is asking
  for an ETC-book-3-shaped artifact.
- When ETC appears in a stack, ask what teaches: if the answer is nothing
  and the child struggles, the complements pattern points to adding explicit
  instruction, not more ETC.
