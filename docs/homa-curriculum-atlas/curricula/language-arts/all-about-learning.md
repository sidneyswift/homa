# All About Reading + All About Spelling

## Snapshot

All About Reading (AAR) and All About Spelling (AAS) are sibling programs from
All About Learning Press, founded by Marie Rippel, who built the method while
teaching her severely dyslexic son to read. Both are scripted, mastery-based,
Orton-Gillingham programs taught one-on-one by the parent with magnetic letter
tiles and flashcard review boxes. AAR covers learning to read (a Pre-reading
level plus Levels 1 to 4); AAS covers spelling in 7 levels that reach
"the high school level" by the publisher's description. They are among the most
recommended reading and spelling programs in the homeschool market, the default
answer in dyslexia threads, and a fixture of curriculum award lists. The trade
they ask for is parent time: every lesson is teacher-led.

## Reputation and rankings

- Cathy Duffy names AAR one of her 103 Top Picks and calls the open-and-go
  scripting simple enough for "the inexperienced parent or teacher."
- Reviewers consistently rank it the top homeschool pick for struggling readers
  and dyslexia; Boldly Educated calls it "the most accessible and most complete
  OG program available at a reasonable price point."
- Community sentiment (Reddit homeschool threads, review blogs) is strongly
  positive with three recurring complaints: it is teacher-intensive (no
  independent work at all), the many components (tiles, three card decks,
  activity book, readers) feel chaotic without an organizing system, and some
  lessons run long enough that families split them over two or three days.
  Fluency practice sheets are the most-skipped component for kids who find
  them overwhelming.
- Trajectory: stable to rising. The science-of-reading wave (see the category
  comparison doc) keeps sending new families toward Orton-Gillingham programs,
  and AAR is the first name they hear.

## Pedagogy and method

Orton-Gillingham lineage, implemented for untrained parents: explicit,
systematic, sequential, multisensory, cumulative, and diagnostic. Phonograms
are taught directly (no guessing from context), practiced through letter-tile
building, flashcard review, and games, then read in fully decodable stories.
Both programs are mastery-based with no grade labels; the publisher states
plainly that "level numbers on our books do not refer to grade levels." Lessons
are fully scripted with essentially zero prep. Sessions run about 20 to 30
minutes, 4 to 5 days per week. Assessment is continuous and informal: cards
move forward in the review box only when mastered, and each level ends when its
skills are solid, not when a calendar says so. AAS applies the same phonograms
and tiles to encoding, with spelling rules taught explicitly and reviewed
permanently. The publisher recommends starting AAS Level 1 regardless of age.

## Structure and scope

AAR: one Pre-reading level (letter knowledge, phonological awareness, print
awareness, listening comprehension, motivation) plus Levels 1 to 4. Level 1 has
53 lessons and 52 decodable stories; Level 4 has 63 lessons and 29 stories.
After Level 4 there is no Level 5: the publisher's mastery evaluation says the
student has the phonics and word-attack skills to decode just about any
familiar word. AAS: Levels 1 to 7, from segmenting and short vowels through
Latin roots, Greek word parts, and loan words. Each AAR level ships as a
teacher's manual, a consumable student packet (activity book, flashcards,
stickers), and (Levels 1 to 4) three decodable readers. Free two-part placement
tests cover both programs. The repo's ELA crosswalk already maps every AAR and
AAS level to skills and bank months, including the publisher's own no-grade
stance: see
[14-curriculum-crosswalk-ela.md](../../../homa-benchmarks/14-curriculum-crosswalk-ela.md)
sections 1a and 1b rather than re-deriving it here.

## A lesson up close

The teacher's manual is the whole operating system. Each lesson opens with a
boxed preview (objective, materials list, cards to pull), then runs as a
script: plain text is what the parent says, instructions tell the parent what
to do with tiles or cards. A typical Level 1 day has four movements. First,
review: the parent flips through the phonogram and word cards currently
"behind the divider" in the Reading Review Box, a few minutes of retrieval
practice. Second, new teaching: the parent builds words from magnetic letter
tiles on a whiteboard while the child blends, swaps, and reads them. Third, the
activity book: one perforated page, usually a cut-and-play game (flip books,
matching, silly-sentence strips) or a fluency practice sheet (columns of words,
phrases, then full sentences using only taught phonograms, with a deliberate
difficulty ramp down the page). Fourth, reading: a short illustrated story from
the current decodable reader, followed by a light comprehension chat. The child
then moves a sticker on a progress chart. A completed day produces one
activity page, one story read aloud, and visible card-box progress. AAS days
mirror the shape with dictation onto paper or tiles instead of a reader. This
section describes structure only; the publisher posts full sample PDFs of every
component (linked below).

## Materials and visual design

Full color throughout AAR, and AAS moved to a color edition in 2023. Teacher's
manuals are softcover, 8.5 by 11, with a calm two-column script layout and
generous white space. Activity books are perforated cardstock-weight pages
designed to be cut apart; the aesthetic is warm, rounded, and slightly retro,
with friendly animal illustration rather than clip-art. The decodable readers
are the brand's pride: cloth-feel hardcovers with soft, storybook-quality
grayscale art that reads as a real book, not a phonics drill. The kit
components (letter tiles with magnets, divider-card review boxes, Ziggy the
Zebra puppet in Pre-reading) make the program physically bulky on a shelf; the
box-and-tiles ritual is part of its identity. The signal to the buyer:
professional, gentle, therapeutic-grade materials that take reading seriously
without looking like school remediation.

## Pricing

As of Jul 2026, from the publisher's
[price list](https://downloads.allaboutlearningpress.com/misc/AALP-Product-List-ISBN-SKU.pdf)
and product pages:

- AAR Pre-reading: $119.95. AAR Levels 1 to 4: $159.95 per level.
- AAS Level 1 materials: $64.95; upper levels run about $49.95 (Level 7).
- One-time purchases: Letter Tiles $25.95 per household (covers every level of
  both programs, including prefix, suffix, and Greek and Latin word-part
  tiles); a Reading Review Box and a Spelling Review Box per student (the old
  Interactive Kits were phased out; divider cards now come with the boxes).
- Per additional child: only the student packet ($67.95 for AAR Level 1,
  $45.95 for AAS Level 1). Manuals, readers, and tiles are reusable.
- A typical year running both programs (one AAR level plus one AAS level) is
  about $210 to 225 for the first child, roughly $115 for a sibling repeating
  the level later.
- The publisher advertises a one-year money-back guarantee (widely confirmed in
  reviews), and resale is strong: reviewers report completed levels selling at
  50 to 70 percent of retail in Facebook groups.

## Why families choose it

- A parent with no phonics knowledge can teach reading well on day one; the
  script carries them.
- It works for the hard cases. The dyslexia origin story plus OG fidelity make
  it the safe choice when a child is struggling or a sibling struggled.
- Mastery pacing without grade labels removes shame for late or older readers.
- The readers feel like real books, so early reading feels like reading, not
  drill.
- Reusability across siblings amortizes the price; only packets get rebought.

## Why families leave it

- Parent time. Every minute is one-on-one; families with many kids or jobs
  drift to independent-work options (Explode the Code for practice, online
  programs, or The Good and the Beautiful where an older child self-runs).
- Natural readers outrun it. A child who clicks with reading finds the pace
  slow and the games unnecessary; those families skim levels or exit early.
- Component fatigue: tiles, three card decks, and cut-apart games feel like
  clutter to minimalist parents (Logic of English families report the same).
- Cost per level stings against free TGTB PDFs or a $20 copy of 100 Easy
  Lessons.
- AAS is a separate purchase and a separate daily slot; some consolidate into
  an integrated program (Logic of English) or drop formal spelling entirely.

## Competitive notes for Homa

- Vocabulary to know: levels not grades, phonograms, leap words (their term for
  sight words), letter tiles, review box, fluency sheets, Ziggy. When a family
  says "she is in AAR 2," that is a skill position, not a grade; the crosswalk
  row licenses which map skills are likely demonstrated.
- The stack pattern: AAR plus AAS plus a separate handwriting program (often
  Handwriting Without Tears) plus separate math. This is the archetypal
  multi-purchase LA stack Homa should recognize and speak fluently.
- Materials lesson: the difficulty ramp inside a single fluency page (words,
  then phrases, then sentences) and the fully-decodable constraint are patterns
  worth learning for Homa-generated practice, as original work, never clones.
- The consumables tax (per-child packets) is a real pain point that
  Homa-generated per-child printables sidestep entirely.
- Gap Homa can fill: AAR gives the parent zero help deciding what to do when a
  lesson stalls mid-week or a child needs extra practice on one phonogram.
  Generating a targeted five-minute review game for the exact cards stuck
  behind the divider is squarely in Homa's lane.
