# Power Homeschool (Acellus)

## Snapshot

Power Homeschool is the parent-facing, unaccredited subscription wrapper
around the Acellus video course library: 250+ filmed courses, PreK-12,
where students watch short video lessons and answer auto-graded questions
with essentially no parent teaching required. Acellus is produced by the
International Academy of Science in Kansas City, Missouri, a nonprofit
chaired by Roger Billings; the same content powers Acellus Academy, the
organization's WASC-accredited online private school. Power Homeschool
strips the accreditation, teachers, transcripts, and diploma, and sells
the raw courseware to registered homeschool parents. It occupies a real
niche (maximum independence, video-first learners, up to 6-7 courses per
student) but carries two honesty-critical liabilities: a documented 2020
content-quality scandal, and a mid-2025 price roughly tripling from about
$31 to $99 per month that generated wide community anger.

## Reputation and rankings

- No major homeschool awards. Cathy Duffy covers Power Homeschool under
  the same listing as Acellus and, per a 2026 directory summary, still
  advises families to investigate content concerns before subscribing
  ([Homeschool Fox directory](https://homeschoolfox.com/curriculum/power-homeschool)).
- The accreditation confusion is chronic and load-bearing: Power
  Homeschool is not accredited and issues no diploma or transcript; the
  parent is the school of record. Families wanting the accredited
  wrapper must pay for Acellus Academy instead
  ([k12loop explainer](https://k12loop.com/is-power-homeschool-accredited/),
  [comparison blog](https://2lovelearning.blogspot.com/2025/11/acellus-academy-vs-power-homeschool-accreditation-difference.html)).
- The 2020 Hawaii controversy, stated plainly: after Hawaii public
  schools adopted Acellus for pandemic distance learning, a 56-specialist
  DOE review team found evidence of discrimination in lessons based on
  gender, race, national origin, religion, and other protected classes,
  an "unacceptable" amount of religious content presenting "Christian
  views as fact," and weak rigor, with at least 75% of reviewed K-12
  courses scoring 0-1 on all four quality rubrics ("repetitive tasks with
  a low cognitive demand"). The Hawaii Board of Education voted
  unanimously to phase it out; Acellus removed or edited flagged content
  during the phase-out ([Star-Advertiser](https://www.staradvertiser.com/2020/11/09/breaking-news/acellus-online-curriculum-promotes-religion-shows-racial-and-cultural-bias-hawaii-reviewers-find/),
  [Civil Beat](https://civilbeat.org/2020/11/doe-report-acellus-online-curriculum-violated-religion-discrimination-policies/),
  [official DOE summary report](https://www.hawaiipublicschools.org/DOE%20Forms/Parent%20Communication/Summary%20Report%20Review%20of%20Acellus.pdf)).
- Current community sentiment is split: Trustpilot reviews stay positive
  on the self-paced video format and engagement, while Reddit threads
  document glitches, dated content, no human to ask when stuck, and
  fury over the 2025 price change ([Homeschool Fox](https://homeschoolfox.com/curriculum/power-homeschool),
  [Brighterly review mining](https://brighterly.com/blog/power-homeschool-reviews/)).
- Trajectory: fading among value-driven families since the price change;
  the "cheap Acellus" identity that drove adoption no longer exists.

## Pedagogy and method

Filmed-teacher courseware: each lesson is a short video of a teacher
presenting, followed by auto-graded questions; failed items trigger
review. Acellus markets an adaptive engine (branded Prism Diagnostics)
that inserts remediation when a student misses concepts, making it
loosely mastery-flavored, though parents do not control the algorithm.
Courses run linearly through a fixed sequence; the parent picks up to
6-7 courses per student and monitors dashboards. There is no teaching
role for the parent and no live human help in Power Homeschool; a
frequently cited weakness is exactly that ("no human to ask when
stuck"). One documented usability complaint from families comparing it
to rivals: the video player restricts rewinding, so a distracted
student must answer wrong to be routed back ([community reviews via
Homeschool Base's Time4Learning thread](https://homeschoolbase.com/curriculum/time4learning)).
Writing instruction is thin across levels, a standard critique
inherited from the Acellus library ([Homeschool Fox](https://homeschoolfox.com/curriculum/power-homeschool)).

## Structure and scope

- PreK-12 catalog of 250+ courses grouped as elementary (PreK-5), middle
  school (6-8), and high school (9-12), covering core subjects plus
  electives, CTE-flavored courses, and special education supports
  ([homeschoolpicks review](https://homeschoolpicks.com/curriculum-reviews/power-homeschool-review/),
  [Brighterly](https://brighterly.com/blog/power-homeschool-reviews/)).
- Students take up to 6-7 concurrent courses; parents assign and can
  change course lineups monthly.
- Progress reports and gradebooks export for the parent's own records;
  weekly reports double as portfolio evidence. No transcripts or
  diploma; parents assemble those ([official FAQ](https://www.powerhomeschool.org/support/faq/)).
- Same videos as Acellus Academy; what the extra Academy tuition buys is
  accreditation, teacher grading, counseling, and the diploma.

## A lesson up close

The student logs in, opens the next lesson in a course, watches a
several-minute video of a filmed teacher at a desk or whiteboard, then
answers a short auto-graded question set. Miss enough and the system
loops in review video or re-asks variants. A day is 4-6 such cycles
across courses; everything scores itself; the parent's involvement is a
dashboard glance. The production style varies with course age: some
libraries are modern, others visibly dated, which is where the
"occasionally feels dated" complaints come from. A completed day
produces gradebook rows, no paper.

## Materials and visual design

Talking-head video courseware: filmed instructors, slide overlays,
question screens. No printables culture, no physical component. The
design vocabulary is institutional rather than playful, which reads as
"real school" to its fans and "credit-recovery software" to its
critics (Acellus's pre-2020 institutional niche was in fact credit
recovery, [Civil Beat](https://civilbeat.org/2020/10/hawaii-board-of-education-votes-to-end-controversial-distance-learning-program/)).

## Pricing

- $99/mo per student, $79/mo at the scholarship rate, as of Jul 2026;
  before mid-2025 it was about $31/mo, and the roughly 3x increase with
  little notice is its dominant recent controversy
  ([Homeschool Fox, pricing as of March 2026](https://homeschoolfox.com/curriculum/power-homeschool),
  [Brighterly](https://brighterly.com/blog/power-homeschool-reviews/),
  repo verification at $99/$79 in [08-segments-curricula](../../../homa-planning/icp-research/08-segments-curricula.md) §4).
- Acellus Academy (accredited, teacher-supported, diploma) starts around
  $249/mo ([Brighterly](https://brighterly.com/blog/power-homeschool-reviews/)).
- Monthly billing, cancel anytime; no consumables, no resale.

## Why families choose it

- Lowest-touch full courseware: even less parent involvement than
  Time4Learning, sold explicitly on independence.
- Video-first learners: filmed teachers work for kids who tune out
  animations and text.
- Course breadth at high school, including electives and CTE topics
  smaller platforms lack.
- Historically, price: at $31/mo it undercut everything comparable.
  That reason is gone.

## Why families leave it

- The 2025 price shock: multi-child families saw costs triple;
  Reddit threads from Aug-Sep 2025 document the exodus mood
  ([Homeschool Fox](https://homeschoolfox.com/curriculum/power-homeschool)).
- Content trust: families who discover the Hawaii findings re-evaluate;
  quality varies course to course and forums advise checking reviews
  per course ([homeschoolpicks](https://homeschoolpicks.com/curriculum-reviews/power-homeschool-review/)).
- Rigor doubts, especially for college-bound students: "Power Homeschool
  is incredibly weak educationally" is a representative community
  worry ([Brighterly's captured verbatim](https://brighterly.com/blog/power-homeschool-reviews/)).
- No human support: stuck students have nowhere to go, and customer
  service reviews are poor.
- Common destinations: Miacademy for engagement, Time4Learning for
  price, accredited online schools when credentials matter.

## Competitive notes for Homa

- Price-anchor role: at $99/mo Power Homeschool now prices above Homa's
  planned band, which is useful comparative context ("full courseware
  costs $99; Homa costs less and serves you, not the screen"). Before
  mid-2025 it was the budget option; the reversal shows how fast price
  identity can flip in this market.
- The controversy is a provenance lesson: Acellus content problems were
  invisible to parents until a state review surfaced them. Homa's
  content provenance and parent-control posture ("never surprise her")
  is the direct counter-position, and worth demonstrating rather than
  claiming.
- Vocabulary to know: Acellus vs Acellus Academy vs Power Homeschool
  (same videos, different wrappers), Prism Diagnostics, the 6-7 course
  cap, scholarship rate. If a family says "we do Acellus," clarify
  which wrapper: it changes whether transcripts exist.
- When a family uses it, the gaps Homa fills: writing instruction and
  feedback, human explanation when stuck, per-course quality vetting,
  and turning gradebook exports into a coherent record the parent owns.
- Watch the trust dynamics: this product retains users through inertia
  and independence, not love. Its churn moments (price change, content
  discovery, stuck kid) are Homa conversation openers.
