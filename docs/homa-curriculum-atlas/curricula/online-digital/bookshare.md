# Bookshare

## Snapshot

Bookshare is the world's largest accessible-ebook library and the free
tier of the print-disability ecosystem: over 1.4 million titles that
qualifying readers can consume as synthetic-voice audio with
karaoke-style highlighted text, large font, or refreshable braille. It
is an initiative of Benetech, the Palo Alto nonprofit run by social
entrepreneur Jim Fruchterman, who conceived it after watching Napster
and launched it in February 2002 as legal file-sharing for the blind
under the Chafee Amendment (17 U.S.C. § 121). Its load-bearing fact for
homeschoolers: membership is completely free for US students of any age
with a qualifying print disability, homeschooled students explicitly
included, funded by an award from the US Department of Education's
Office of Special Education Programs (OSEP). Dyslexia is a qualifying
condition, an IEP or 504 plan is not required, and certification comes
from any professional with relevant expertise. Where Learning Ally
sells human narration of the school canon, Bookshare gives away scale:
textbooks, bestsellers, and 40+ languages, read by a machine.

## Reputation and rankings

- Credibility is structural rather than reviewed: two decades of
  federal funding, formal partnerships with 900+ publishers who
  contribute files directly, and interoperation with the National
  Library Service and Learning Ally (each accepts the others'
  eligibility certification). In assistive-technology circles
  (Perkins, AFB) it is simply infrastructure.
- In dyslexia and homeschool communities it is the standing "did you
  know it's free" recommendation, usually mentioned in the same breath
  as Learning Ally with the tradeoff attached: free and vast, but
  synthetic voices.
- The honest friction reports: TTS narration wears on some children
  (especially for literature, where expression carries meaning), the
  catalog is text-first (image-heavy books adapt poorly), and the
  sign-up plus proof-of-disability flow is bureaucratic enough that
  families stall partway.
- Trajectory: stable. The collection keeps growing via publisher
  contribution, the reading tools keep consolidating around the web
  reader and mainstream reading apps, and the free-for-students
  funding has survived multiple award cycles (each renewal is worth
  re-verifying before parent-facing claims).

## Pedagogy and method

Bookshare's method is access engineering, not instruction. The model it
serves is the same ear-reading accommodation logic as Learning Ally
(decode with the intervention, comprehend with the ears meanwhile),
delivered through customization rather than human voices: members
choose voices and reading speeds, follow word-by-word highlighting that
anchors eyes to text while audio plays (the bimodal pattern reading
specialists recommend), enlarge fonts, change contrast, or read in
braille. Because everything is digital text underneath, the same book
serves a blind braille reader, a dyslexic ear reader, and a low-vision
large-print reader. The copyright architecture is the invisible half of
the method: the Chafee Amendment lets Benetech distribute copyrighted
works in accessible formats to certified print-disabled readers
without publisher permission, which is why eligibility gating is
non-negotiable and why the general public cannot join. Volunteers and
publisher files feed the collection ("scan once, share often" was the
founding mantra).

## Structure and scope

- Collection: 1.4M+ titles spanning K-12 and college textbooks
  (including Common Core lists), popular fiction and bestsellers,
  career materials, and children's books, in 40+ languages; about
  10,000 public-domain titles are open to anyone without membership.
- Formats: EPUB, DAISY text, DAISY audio, MP3, BRF (braille-ready),
  and Word, plus the in-browser Bookshare Web Reader.
- Reading tools: the Web Reader covers most families (Chromebook, PC,
  Mac); members also read through third-party apps (Voice Dream
  Reader, Dolphin EasyReader, Capti) and braille or DAISY hardware.
- Membership types: Individual (family-controlled, the homeschool
  path) and Organizational (schools assigning books to rosters);
  a homeschooler qualifies as a student for the free Individual
  membership.
- Eligibility: qualifying conditions include blindness and low vision,
  dyslexia and other reading disabilities, and physical disabilities
  that prevent handling print. Certification must come from a
  professional with appropriate expertise (educators, reading
  specialists, psychologists, physicians, and similar); Learning Ally
  or NLS membership also certifies. No IEP or 504 required, a point
  the publisher makes loudly because school families assume otherwise.
- Reading is unlimited: no due dates, holds, or per-book fees.

## A lesson up close

As with Learning Ally, there is no lesson; there is a workflow. Setup
is the real first unit: the parent creates the Individual membership,
obtains the proof-of-disability signature from, say, the family's
reading tutor or pediatric psychologist, and uploads it. From then on
the routine is search, add to reading list, open. A middle schooler
doing literature opens the assigned novel in Web Reader: sentence
highlighted in yellow, words spoken at the speed she chose, font
bumped to large, sepia background. For a history text she skims by
chapter headings and re-listens to the two sections that matter. A
braille-reading sibling downloads the BRF of the same book. What a
session produces: chapters covered and a child who did the same
reading as the co-op discussion group, with the parent's role limited
to the setup and the occasional format troubleshooting. The friction
families report is at the edges: finding the exact edition a
curriculum assigns, and pushing files to a specific device or app the
child prefers.

## Materials and visual design

Utilitarian and proud of it: the site and Web Reader look like a
library catalog with an accessibility-first reading pane, because
that is what they are. Customization is the aesthetic: voice, speed,
size, contrast, highlight color. Nothing about it is childish or
branded, which cuts both ways: it never embarrasses a teenager, and it
never delights a seven-year-old. Families who need warmth layer it on
themselves (or keep Learning Ally or commercial audiobooks alongside
for exactly that reason).

## Pricing

As of Jul 2026, from the publisher's pages:

- US students of any age, homeschoolers included: free, funded by the
  OSEP award, with unlimited reading.
- Non-student US adults: $79.99 per year (raised from $50 in May 2023,
  the first increase in the organization's history), with a one-time
  $59.99 hardship rate available on request.
- Public-domain subset: free to everyone, no membership.
- International members pay country-banded fees.
- The practical homeschool math: a family with a certified dyslexic
  student pays nothing, which makes Bookshare the default first move
  before deciding whether Learning Ally's human narration is worth
  $135 a year on top.

## Why families choose it

- Free and vast: the qualifying student reads unlimited books,
  including textbooks and the fiction series a kid actually wants, at
  zero cost.
- The braille and low-vision path: for blind and visually impaired
  homeschoolers it is not an alternative but the backbone, alongside
  NLS.
- Control: speed, voice, font, and format tuning fits kids whose needs
  do not match any single fixed presentation.
- No IEP required: families outside the school system qualify through
  any appropriate professional, which suits homeschoolers who avoided
  formal evaluations.
- It legitimizes: like Learning Ally, membership converts "she can't
  read the book" into "she reads it her way," with a federal program's
  weight behind it.

## Why families leave it

- Robot-voice fatigue: children who tolerate TTS for textbooks often
  refuse it for novels; families keep Libby and Audible for pleasure
  listening and let Bookshare go quiet.
- Setup stall: the certification step defeats a real share of
  intenders, who never finish activating the free thing they qualify
  for.
- Edition mismatch and tooling friction: the assigned curriculum's
  exact edition may be missing, and device workflows (which app, which
  format) exceed some families' patience.
- The need passes: as with Learning Ally, remediation success ends the
  accommodation, though the free price means accounts often just sit
  dormant instead of formally churning.

## Competitive notes for Homa

- Vocabulary to know: print disability, proof of disability,
  qualifying condition, Individual vs Organizational membership, Web
  Reader, DAISY, BRF. "We have Bookshare" implies a certified print
  disability in the family and an ear-reading or braille workflow.
- The free-tier map is the immediate value Homa can deliver: many
  eligible homeschool families have never heard that Bookshare is free
  for their dyslexic student, that an IEP is not required, that their
  reading tutor can certify, or that certification reciprocates with
  Learning Ally. Holding that map, and walking a family through the
  setup stall, is high-trust help that costs Homa nothing and never
  crosses into diagnosing (the lane-keeping rule in
  `../../comparisons/special-needs-intervention.md`).
- Records: same law as Learning Ally, ear-read and braille-read books
  are read books in Homa's logs and progress evidence, with format
  recorded neutrally.
- Materials lesson: one text, many renderings is the deepest pattern
  here. Homa's generated materials should similarly separate content
  from presentation (print, large print, audio-forward, low-visual-
  clutter) so one lesson serves the family's mixed needs, which fixed
  print curricula structurally cannot.
- Stack expectation: Bookshare rarely stands alone; expect it beside
  an OG program (content access during remediation), beside Libby and
  Audible (pleasure listening), and, in blind and low-vision families,
  beside NLS BARD and braille hardware. The comparison doc maps the
  whole ecosystem.
