# SchoolhouseTeachers.com

## Snapshot

SchoolhouseTeachers.com is the all-you-can-eat subscription of Christian
homeschooling: one family membership (currently $389 a year) opens 400+
self-paced courses across every subject, PK-12, for every child in the
household, with no per-student pricing and, for most courses, no
textbooks to buy. It launched in 2012 as an extension of The Old
Schoolhouse, the homeschool magazine Paul and Gena Suarez grew out of a
late-1990s eBay used-curriculum store, and the membership still bundles
the magazine plus record-keeping tools (Applecore), a report card
generator, transcript help, and parent support. It is the anti-academy
of this category: where live academies sell scarce teacher attention per
course, SchoolhouseTeachers sells unlimited material breadth and leaves
the teaching, pacing, and accountability entirely to the parent. Its
buyer is the large or budget-constrained family for whom per-course and
per-child pricing anywhere else is disqualifying.

## Reputation and rankings

- Value-for-large-families is the unanimous praise: hundreds of courses
  for one flat price recurs in every review, and the platform's course
  count and tooling have grown steadily since 2012.
- The standing criticism is coherence. Courses come from dozens of
  different contributing teachers, so review threads describe a
  fragmented buffet: no unified aesthetic or difficulty standard,
  variable production quality, and real work for the parent in choosing,
  sequencing, and sticking with a path. Curriculum "boxes" (pre-built
  grade-level lineups) are the company's own answer to that critique.
- Price history is its own story: from $5.95 a month at 2012 launch
  (grandfathered members still pay it, per the company) to $389 a year
  in 2026, documented on the publisher's own about page; reviewers
  still score it cheap per child for families of three or more.
- Award-circuit connection to read carefully: the publisher, The Old
  Schoolhouse, runs one of the market's award programs (the Blue Ribbon
  Awards, voted by its Homeschool Influencer Network review crew; see
  `../../market-overview.md` on that circuit's review-crew bias). Award
  glow around this platform and its vendors is partly house-run, so
  independent long-form reviews are the better reputation evidence.
- Trajectory: stable legacy platform; the magazine relationship and ESA
  era keep it visible, while design-forward competitors (The Good and
  the Beautiful free PDFs, Miacademy-style courseware) compete for the
  same budget buyer.

## Pedagogy and method

There is no single pedagogy; there is a container. Courses are
self-paced and text-or-video delivered: some are daily-lesson PDF
courses in a traditional workbook spirit, some are streaming video
series, some are unit-style or elective one-offs (filmmaking, violin,
economics). What unifies the platform is posture, Christian worldview
throughout, and mechanics: everything is included, everything is
printable or streamable, nothing is scheduled for you beyond the
course's internal lesson plan. The parent is the teacher of record in
the fullest sense: choosing courses, enforcing pace, grading with the
provided answer keys, and assembling records with the platform's tools.
Accountability is the known weak layer, exactly the inverse of the live
academies profiled alongside it.

## Structure and scope

- 400+ courses spanning Bible, language arts, math, history, science,
  geography, art, music, health, computer and technology, drama and
  speech, foreign languages (including Latin), economics, electives,
  and parent-facing courses.
- Curriculum boxes: pre-assembled grade-level course lineups
  (kindergarten through high school) for families who want a default
  path instead of the buffet.
- Focused learning centers group resources by need (preschool, special
  needs, high school, and similar).
- Tools: Applecore record-keeping, report card generator, transcript
  templates and help, tests, quizzes, answer keys, streaming video
  library, live parent chat support.
- Membership extras rotate: quarterly print magazine, back issues,
  planners, mentor time, tote bags (the Applecore Gold and Silver
  bundle tiers).
- Most courses need no outside purchases; some honors-level high school
  courses require textbooks (the company's own asterisk).

## A lesson up close

The parent picks a course (say, a grade 4 language arts course from a
curriculum box), downloads or prints the week's lesson PDFs or queues
the video, and runs it at the kitchen table like any workbook
curriculum; the child works the pages, the parent checks against the
answer key, and progress exists wherever the parent records it,
ideally Applecore. There is no external teacher, no submission, no
grading service, and no completion enforcement: a course abandoned in
week 7 simply stops. The daily experience therefore tracks the quality
of the specific course chosen, which is why reviews of the same
platform range from delighted to underwhelmed; the membership is a
library card, and the year is whatever the parent builds from it.

## Materials and visual design

Digital-first and printer-fed: PDFs of widely varying vintage and
polish, from clean modern layouts to visibly dated worksheet design,
plus a streaming video layer. There is no house visual language, which
is the trade-off of the many-authors model, and heavy users cite
printing cost as a real line item (the same complaint Gather Round's
digital families make, without the design payoff). The aesthetic signal
is thrift and abundance rather than beauty: the shelf this product
lives on is a binder, not a bookcase.

## Pricing

As of Jul 2026, from the publisher's join page: Ultimate annual $389,
Ultimate quarterly $129 (promo $119), lifetime $1,850, all covering
every child in the family PK-12 with Applecore bundle tiers included;
a $399 starter kit bundles a year with magazine extras. Reviewer
snapshots within the last year also record a PreK-8-only tier and
monthly options around $22.97-29.97, so tiers shift; the join page is
the live truth. The per-child economics invert every academy in this
category: at three students the effective cost is about $130 per child
per year for all subjects, roughly a fifth of one live academy course.

## Why families choose it

- One price, whole family, every subject: the strongest large-family
  economics in the paid market.
- Self-paced flexibility with no enrollment windows, semesters, or
  seat limits.
- Worldview trust plus breadth: a Christian default for every subject
  and elective a kid wanders into.
- The bundled tools (records, report cards, transcripts) flatter the
  compliance anxiety of newer homeschoolers.

## Why families leave it

- Buffet fatigue: choosing and sequencing from 400+ uneven courses is
  unpaid curation labor, and families drift to opinionated curricula
  that just say "do this next."
- Quality variance: a weak course in a core subject sends families to
  dedicated programs (a real math curriculum, a structured phonics
  program) and the membership stops earning its renewal.
- No accountability layer: self-paced plus no external teacher means
  the parent polices everything; working parents who needed relief
  discover they bought material, not help.
- Screen-and-printer friction: the same guilt documented across this
  category (see `../../comparisons/online-digital.md`).

## Competitive notes for Homa

- This is the closest existing product to "unlimited materials for one
  family price," and its 14-year lesson is that unlimited breadth
  without orchestration produces overwhelm, not relief. Homa generates
  materials on demand from observed need, which is the exact inverse
  failure profile; worth stating plainly in positioning.
- The curriculum boxes prove demand for a default path through a big
  library; Homa's planner is that idea done adaptively.
- Applecore and the report card generator are the incumbent
  record-keeping bar in this segment, and they are manual-entry tools;
  Homa's evidence-accumulating record is a generation ahead, and
  SchoolhouseTeachers families already believe records matter.
- Vocabulary: Ultimate membership, curriculum boxes, Applecore, focused
  learning centers, The Old Schoolhouse (the magazine and the award
  circuit). A family "on SchoolhouseTeachers" is budget-conscious,
  likely multi-kid, parent-orchestrated, and worldview-committed;
  their pain is choosing, sequencing, and follow-through, not access.
