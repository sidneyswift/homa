# Outschool

## Snapshot

Outschool is the live-class marketplace: a two-sided platform where about
10,000 independent teachers sell more than 140,000 live online classes to
over 1 million learners ages 3-18 in 183 countries. Founded in 2015 by Amir
Nathoo, Mikhail Seregine, and Nick Grandy (Y Combinator; $110M Series D at a
$3B valuation in Oct 2021), it was discovered first by homeschoolers and
exploded during the pandemic. Classes run on Zoom in small groups and range
from algebra and essay writing to Minecraft math and Pokemon drawing;
teachers set their own prices and compete on reviews. It is not a school
and not a curriculum: it is where homeschool families buy the pieces they
cannot or do not want to teach, plus social connection. Since 2025 its
defining product event is the migration toward a credit-based membership
model, which is also its defining source of customer anger. Founder CEO
Amir Nathoo stepped back in February 2026; COO Xueyan Wang and CTO Andy
Chiu co-lead while a successor is sought
([transition letter](https://outschool.com/articles/an-update-from-amir-a-leadership-transition-at-outschool)).

## Reputation and rankings

- Consumer footprint is large and split by topic: Trustpilot sits in the
  low 4s out of 5 across thousands of reviews (4.4 from 3,288 in the
  2026-07-06 repo capture, [05-competitor-user-voice](../../../homa-planning/icp-research/05-competitor-user-voice.md) §3),
  with praise concentrated on teachers and complaints concentrated on
  billing. Common Sense Media hosts 87 parent reviews whose consensus is
  sharply negative on billing and support: "the overall lack of reliable
  customer support and unclear billing practices leave families frustrated
  and feeling scammed" ([Common Sense Media](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/website-reviews/outschool)).
- Teacher quality is the loved thing: reviewers describe instructors as
  "knowledgeable, patient, and engaging" and value interest-matching
  ("classes that blend her interest in dolls with geography")
  ([Trustpilot capture in 05-competitor-user-voice](../../../homa-planning/icp-research/05-competitor-user-voice.md) §3).
- It is repeatedly recommended in homeschool communities for neurodivergent
  kids: small groups, interest-first topics, no grades unless requested.
- The critical thread is structural: because teachers are independent
  contractors, quality varies class to class. One directory's summary: the
  best classes are expert niche instruction, the worst are "an expensive
  Zoom call" ([Homeschool Tools guide](https://homeschooltools.net/offering/outschool)).
- Trajectory: stable on supply and brand, wobbly on trust. No new funding
  since 2021, founder stepped back in Feb 2026, and the credit-model anger
  is the loudest sustained complaint wave in its history. Its stated 2026
  direction is "using AI to make learning more personalized"
  ([transition letter](https://outschool.com/articles/an-update-from-amir-a-leadership-transition-at-outschool)).

## Pedagogy and method

There is no house pedagogy: Outschool is infrastructure for whatever each
teacher believes. The platform's own bet is interest-led learning in live
small groups: kids pick topics they care about, meet a real teacher and
peers on video, and motivation does the rest. Class sizes are intentionally
small (commonly 3-9 learners). Formats define the learning model more than
any method does ([how classes work](https://support.outschool.com/en/articles/19491-how-do-outschool-classes-work)):

- One-time classes (single session workshops)
- Multi-day camps (several sessions in one week)
- Semester and multi-week courses (a syllabus across weeks)
- Ongoing weekly classes (no end date, billed weekly, cancel anytime)
- Self-paced classes (pre-recorded, asynchronous teacher messaging)
- Groups (moderated asynchronous interest communities)
- 1:1 tutoring

Assessment is whatever the teacher offers; there are no platform-wide
grades, tests, or mastery gates. Parents can request progress reports and
certificates from some teachers for portfolio purposes. The parent's role
is curator and scheduler: finding classes, vetting teachers by review
history, managing the calendar and credits. The child's learning hour is
teacher-led; the parent's real labor is the logistics around it.

## Structure and scope

- Scope is the catalog: 140,000+ listed classes across academic subjects
  (math, English, science, history, languages, coding, test prep) and an
  enormous enrichment long tail (art, music, chess, Dungeons and Dragons,
  cooking, social clubs) ([about page](https://outschool.com/about)).
- There are no levels. Classes advertise an age band (for example 8-12)
  and prerequisites in free text. Continuity comes from following a
  specific teacher's sequence (many teachers run Part 1/Part 2/Part 3
  ladders) rather than from the platform.
- Some full-curriculum ladders exist inside the marketplace (semester-long
  algebra, year-long essay writing series), and membership includes a
  library of 100+ self-paced classes
  ([payment FAQ](https://support.outschool.com/en/articles/14171096-outschool-payment-options-faqs-for-parents)).
- Nothing is accredited; there are no transcripts. Homeschool parents log
  Outschool classes as their own course evidence.

## A lesson up close

From the child's seat: a class is a Zoom room at a scheduled time. The
teacher runs a screen-shared, discussion-heavy session for 25-55 minutes
depending on age band; small size means the child talks, types in chat,
and shows work to a real human. Between sessions of multi-week classes,
the Outschool Classroom page holds announcements, posted materials,
homework threads, and recordings. Worksheets and handouts, when they
exist, are teacher-made PDFs of wildly varying design quality: this is a
marketplace of individual craftspeople, not a designed materials system.
A completed class day produces experience and sometimes an uploaded
photo or document in the classroom feed, but no automatic record: the
parent screenshots, saves, and logs if their state requires evidence.
From the parent's seat, the work is before and after: search and filter
the catalog, read teacher profiles and reviews, check the credit balance
and the calendar, and afterward decide whether this teacher becomes part
of the family's permanent rotation. Veteran families accumulate a stable
of trusted teachers and follow them like favorite authors.

## Materials and visual design

The platform's own design is a polished consumer marketplace: photographed
class cards, teacher headshots, star ratings, age filters. It reads like
Airbnb for classes, which is the point: browsing is shopping. In-class
materials are each teacher's own slides, PDFs, and props, ranging from
professional to homemade; there is no house visual language for learning
materials. What the aesthetic signals: abundance and choice. The buyer
feeling it sells is "there is a class for exactly what my kid is into,"
which no fixed curriculum can match, and which is also why budgets leak
one enrollment at a time.

## Pricing

- Two ways to pay, as of Jul 2026 ([membership page](https://outschool.com/join-membership)):
  pay per class at the listed dollar price, or buy a credit membership
  where each credit is worth 50 cents toward enrollments.
- Membership tiers: 80 credits/$40, 144/$70, 248/$120, 500/$240, 800/$380,
  1,285/$600 per month. Higher tiers include bonus credits (up to 85/mo);
  membership adds a self-paced class library, no marketplace fee, and
  Concierge support. Unused credits roll over while the membership stays
  active. First month free on the $40 plan at signup as of Jul 2026.
- Typical class costs: small-group live sessions about $5-36 per session,
  1:1 tutoring about $24-58 per session, self-paced courses about $10-94
  per week of content ([Brighterly pricing guide](https://brighterly.com/blog/outschool-pricing/)).
- Real annual spend varies more than any competitor in this category: a
  one-class-a-week family on the $40 plan spends about $480/yr; a
  multi-kid, multi-class family can pass $3,000/yr without noticing,
  which is exactly the complaint ("subscriptions and credits can add up
  quietly in the background," [a veteran user review](https://funhomeschoolflorida.com/outschool-for-homeschooling/)).
- The catch parents cite most: cancel the membership and access to
  credits ends with the billing cycle; ongoing classes are weekly
  subscriptions that must be manually unsubscribed; refund policies vary
  by class and are strict ([Common Sense Media](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/website-reviews/outschool),
  [Screenwise review](https://screenwiseapp.com/media/outschool-app)).
- ESA/ClassWallet awkwardness is a documented friction for
  state-funds families ([05-competitor-user-voice](../../../homa-planning/icp-research/05-competitor-user-voice.md) §3).

## Why families choose it

- To outsource what they cannot teach: upper math, writing feedback,
  foreign language, anything the parent dreads.
- Social connection without school: live peers, recurring clubs, real
  teachers who know the kid's name.
- Interest-led wins: the catalog can meet any obsession, which converts
  reluctant learners.
- Neurodivergent fit: small groups, chosen topics, no forced pace.
- No commitment: try a $10 one-time class, walk away.

## Why families leave it

- The credit model. The 2025 migration from simple pay-per-class produced
  the platform's angriest sustained feedback: "I much preferred the old
  format where I could choose classes and pay," "It's hard to tell how
  much classes cost and feels restrictive," and losing access immediately
  on cancel ([Trustpilot verbatims captured in 05-competitor-user-voice](../../../homa-planning/icp-research/05-competitor-user-voice.md) §3).
- Billing chaos and weak support: double charges, hard refunds, slow
  responses; "read the refund policy twice" is standard community advice
  ([Common Sense Media](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/website-reviews/outschool),
  [Screenwise](https://screenwiseapp.com/media/outschool-app)).
- Cost creep: per-class spending compounds invisibly against a fixed
  budget; families audit and cut.
- Quality lottery: a few weak classes in a row sour the whole platform.
- Scheduling load: live classes mean calendar management; families
  simplify back to self-paced or curriculum-based work.
- Note the asymmetry: churn language is about the pricing model and
  operations, almost never about teachers. Families often quit angry at
  Outschool while still loving specific Outschool teachers.

## Competitive notes for Homa

- Not a direct competitor: Outschool sells the child's learning hour,
  Homa serves the parent running the whole operation. They co-exist in
  the same family budget, which is the risk: Outschool spend is lumpy
  and can crowd out a $29-49/mo assistant subscription in a tight month.
- The stack-assistant angle: Outschool families have the exact problem
  Homa solves, fragmentation. Classes across 4 teachers produce no
  unified record of the child. Homa should ingest Outschool activity as
  evidence (class titles, teacher feedback, completed projects) and fold
  it into the child's profile and compliance record.
- Vocabulary to know when a family says they use it: credits (50 cents
  each), memberships vs pay-per-class, ongoing classes (weekly billing),
  camps, self-paced classes, the Classroom page, following a teacher.
  Knowing that "we do Outschool for writing" usually means a specific
  beloved teacher, not the platform, earns trust.
- Pricing lesson: Outschool is the category's loudest proof that opaque
  credit systems burn trust. Parents literally write "it's hard to tell
  how much classes cost." Homa's billing must stay flat, predictable,
  and cancel-safe; never introduce an abstraction between dollars and
  value ([05-competitor-user-voice](../../../homa-planning/icp-research/05-competitor-user-voice.md) §8).
- Watch item: Outschool's stated AI-personalization direction could grow
  toward per-child learning profiles, adjacent to Homa's lane. Its data
  is class-consumption history, not observational evidence, so the moat
  differs, but monitor announcements under the incoming CEO.
