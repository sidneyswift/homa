# Fascinating Education

## Snapshot

Fascinating Education is high school science taught as narrated pictures:
online Chemistry, Biology, and Physics courses (plus Anatomy and
Physiology and a Logic of Medicine mini-course) built by Dr. Sheldon
Margulies, a retired neurologist who taught medical students at the
University of Alabama and University of Maryland and decided teenagers
deserved the same clarity. There is no textbook; each lesson is a slide
deck of simple colorful illustrations with Margulies narrating, on the
theory that engaging visual processing (his "right hemisphere" framing)
before terminology makes hard science gentle. It is one of the standing
answers when a secular homeschool family asks for high school science
that is not Apologia (`apologia.md`) and not a heavy lab program.
Mindshare is small but persistent, sustained by review-crew coverage and
budget pricing rather than marketing.

## Reputation and rankings

There is no Cathy Duffy review, which itself signals the program's
under-the-radar position. Sentiment lives in Schoolhouse Review Crew
coverage, homeschool.com's product review, and long-tail blog reviews,
and it is consistently warm with the same shape: parents praise the
plain-English explanations, the assume-nothing starting point, the
storyline structure, and how little the parent must do; the recurring
criticisms are that it is lecture-based with thin hands-on work (a
wiggly kid gets bored), tests feel genuinely high school hard, and the
platform is bare-bones (shared logins, no automated gradebook, answer
keys a determined student can find). The program's own headline evidence
is the founder's 2007 Dunbar High School (Baltimore) experiment, where
the junior-class state exam pass rate rose from 40 percent to 87 percent
the year he taught biology (self-reported on the About page). Reviewers
aimed at secular audiences explicitly flag it as evolutionary,
standard-science content. Trajectory: stable niche, with a recent site
migration that added free early lessons and free math minicourses.

## Pedagogy and method

Margulies states his method plainly: assume the student knows nothing,
slice information thin, present slowly in plain English, follow a
storyline, show how the science explains real life, and de-emphasize
terminology. Lessons are narrated illustration sequences rather than
filmed lectures or textbook chapters; even formulas are explained
visually. The sequence philosophy is chemistry first (biology is argued
to be applied chemistry), then biology, then physics. Assessment is a
test per lesson, printable or online; in Chemistry and Physics, test
questions carry help buttons that re-teach the concept a second way
before the student answers, a re-explanation mechanic reviewers single
out. There is no spiral review and no lab requirement outside
Chemistry's household-item labs; the parent decides pacing (the
publisher notes some students finish a course in a month, others take a
year) and the parent decides what goes on the transcript.

## Structure and scope

Three full-credit high school courses: Fascinating Chemistry, Fascinating
Biology, and Fascinating Physics (18, 18, and 15 lessons in the current
storefront's paid blocks, each lesson roughly 45 minutes of narrated
slides plus its test). Fascinating Medicine is a two-part elective
(Anatomy and Physiology, plus the shorter Logic of Medicine). The
current site also carries a middle school chemistry course, free
grammar school and middle school math minicourses (Margulies argues
math anxiety is the real science anxiety), and free test-and-answer
banks. Lessons 1 through 4 of the main courses are free samples on the
new storefront. Chemistry includes labs done with household or easily
purchased items; Biology and Physics are lecture-and-test. There is no
placement machinery; the publisher's guidance is course order
(chemistry, biology, physics) and a middle schooler strong in math can
start early. Credits are the family's call, and the publisher says so
directly, which makes it a clean example of the mom-issued-credit
culture covered in `../../comparisons/high-school.md`.

## A lesson up close

The student logs in, opens the next lesson, and watches a sequence of
simple, bright, purpose-drawn illustrations while Margulies talks
through them, question by question: each slide poses or answers a small
question that leads to the next, so a 45-minute lesson feels like a
guided chain of "and why would that happen?" rather than a chapter.
The student can print the lesson script to read along, take notes, or
study later, and a glossary handles unfamiliar terms. After the lesson
(or after a re-listen) the student takes the test, online or on paper;
in Chemistry and Physics, a stuck student presses the help button on a
question and gets the concept re-explained differently before
answering. The parent grades from the answer key (nothing is recorded
automatically), logs the score wherever the family keeps records, and
schedules the Chemistry lab when one applies. A completed lesson
produces test results and, in Chemistry, occasional lab work; a course
produces what the parent decides to call a credit.

## Materials and visual design

Everything lives in the browser: narrated slide lessons, printable
scripts (text plus the slide images), printable tests and answer keys.
The illustration style is deliberately simple and diagrammatic, closer
to a physician's teaching sketches than to textbook photography, with
color used for meaning rather than decoration. There is no print
product, no boxed kit, no video of a person. The aesthetic signals
gentle seriousness: no cartoon mascots, no gamification, no glossy
production, just clear pictures and a calm voice, which is exactly the
pitch to a parent whose teen found textbook science walls of text.

## Pricing

The program migrated storefronts, and pricing differs by channel. On
the current official site as of Jul 2026: lessons 1 to 4 of each main
course are free; the remaining high school lesson blocks cost $45 each
(Chemistry lessons 5-18, Biology lessons 5-18, Physics lessons 5-15,
Anatomy and Physiology lessons 5-15), Logic of Medicine is $20, middle
school chemistry is $20, and the math minicourses and test banks are
free. Legacy channels (the older fascinatingeducation.com storefront
and award-site listings) list $99 per course for a year of family
access with discounted two- and three-course bundles; treat those
figures as the old model. Either way this is among the cheapest real
high school science available, and family access covers multiple
students. Price page: https://fascinatingeducation.net/courses.

## Why families choose it

The parent cannot or will not teach chemistry, and this explains it for
them, gently, for under $50 a course. Secular families get standard
science without adapting around a worldview. Anxious or
science-scarred students get an assume-nothing on-ramp that reviewers
consistently call confidence-building. Budget families get family-wide
access at a fraction of courseware prices. And light-lab families get
high school science without equipment purchases (Chemistry's household
labs excepted).

## Why families leave it

Hands-on kids wilt in a lecture-only format, and families wanting real
lab science credit (especially for STEM-bound teens) move to lab-based
programs or co-op and dual-enrollment lab courses. Families wanting
records automation find the bare platform frustrating: no gradebook,
shared logins, discoverable answer keys. College-prep maximalists
question whether a gentle course signals enough rigor for a transcript
headed to selective admissions, and step up to AP-compatible courseware
like Thinkwell (`../online-digital/thinkwell.md`) or graded outside
providers. And Christian families wanting worldview-integrated science
were never the audience; they stay with Apologia (`apologia.md`).

## Competitive notes for Homa

Fascinating Education proves the demand for gentle, visual,
assume-nothing science explanation at high school level, the exact
register Homa's materials should hit when a parent says their teen is
lost in science. Its help-button pattern (re-explain the same concept a
different way, at the moment of being stuck, before revealing the
answer) is a materials mechanic worth copying. Its gaps are Homa's
openings: no grading or records (Homa can be the gradebook and
transcript layer around it), no lab evidence trail (Homa can log
Chemistry labs as portfolio artifacts), and no credit guidance (the
publisher explicitly leaves credits to the parent, which is precisely
the transcript anxiety Homa's high school features address). When a
family says they use it, expect a secular-leaning, budget-conscious
household with low parent confidence in science; know the course order
(chemistry, biology, physics), the lesson-test rhythm, and that pacing
is fully elastic. Vocabulary: lessons and scripts, help buttons,
Fascinating Medicine, the Dunbar story.
