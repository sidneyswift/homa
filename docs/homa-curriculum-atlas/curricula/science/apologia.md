# Apologia (Exploring Creation science)

## Snapshot

Apologia is the dominant Christian homeschool science publisher in the United States, and the default answer when a conservative Christian homeschool family asks "what do we use for science." Founded in the early 1990s (commonly dated 1994) by Dr. Jay Wile, a nuclear chemist, around his self-teaching high school courses, the company was sold to Davis Carman in June 2008 and has since grown into a K-12 publisher of science, Bible, math, and worldview materials, with science still the flagship. The "Exploring Creation" brand spans a K-6 immersion series (Young Explorer), two junior high courses, and a full college-prep high school lab sequence. Everything is written from an explicit young-earth creationist worldview: the theology is inside the science explanations, not in a removable appendix. Multiple reviewers call it the most widely used Christian homeschool science curriculum in the country, and its author-facing marketing claims over a million elementary books sold (publisher claim, unverified). Its junior high and high school courses are famous for being written directly to the student so teens can self-teach.

## Reputation and rankings

- Practical Homeschooling Reader Awards: Apologia's own awards page claims top rankings from 2011 through 2026, and it has been the perennial category winner in homeschool science for over a decade.
- Old Schoolhouse Excellence in Education awards: top ranked 2011 through 2020 per the same page. The company claims more than 100 awards overall (publisher claim).
- Cathy Duffy reviews the elementary, junior high, and high school lines at length and rates the upper-level courses "among the best options for college-prep, lab science courses" that allow independent study.
- Community sentiment is sharply split along worldview lines. Christian users are intensely loyal; a representative long-term review (Boldly Educated, 13 years of use) calls it the best Christian homeschool science available. Critical threads focus on three things: the young-earth framework being inseparable from the content (BioLogos forum threads catalog specific scientific errors and worldview-driven claims in Wile-era texts), the sheer reading load at every level, and lab-supply logistics. Secular review sites (for example Modulo) rate it not recommended for secular families, which is less a criticism than a market boundary.
- Trajectory: stable to rising inside its segment. It owns the Christian science shelf the way Saxon once owned math. One notable dissent: Exodus Books, a long-time retailer, observes that post-Wile editions are somewhat less content-dense than the originals, trading depth for user-friendliness.
- The founder split matters for reputation: Jay Wile resigned in 2010, two years after selling, citing disagreement with the new owner's direction, and now publishes competing courses at Berean Builders (see `berean-builders.md`). Old-guard families sometimes treat Wile's departure as a quality dividing line, and Wile has publicly noted he did not write the elementary series (Jeannie Fulbright did).

## Pedagogy and method

- Immersion over survey: one branch of science per year, studied deeply, rather than a topic-a-week spiral. Apologia argues depth builds love of the subject; critics call the result coverage gaps unless the family plans a multi-year rotation.
- Conversational direct-address prose is the house signature. Elementary books read like a mom chatting with her kids (Fulbright writes in first person to the child); upper books talk the student through concepts the way a friendly teacher would. This is the single most-praised trait in reviews.
- Elementary layers in Charlotte Mason-influenced practices: narration-style oral review, notebooking, and nature-adjacent activities (see the methods glossary at `docs/homa-planning/icp-research/10-homeschool-methods-glossary.md` for the CM background).
- Junior high and high school are built for student independence: the text, the "On Your Own" questions, study guides, and tests form a closed self-teaching loop. Parent grades from the solutions manual.
- Mastery cadence: a module is studied for about two weeks, closed with a study guide and a test, then left behind. Assessment is traditional (tests, lab write-ups) at upper levels; elementary assessment is oral review and notebooking, with optional tests.
- Worldview integration is a design goal, not an accident: lessons routinely attribute design in nature to God, and the biology course evaluates evolution critically from a biblical frame.

## Structure and scope

- Elementary, Young Explorer Series (K-6, multi-age by design): eight one-year courses with no required order: Astronomy, Botany, Chemistry and Physics, Zoology 1 (Flying Creatures), Zoology 2 (Swimming Creatures), Zoology 3 (Land Animals), Human Anatomy and Physiology, Earth Science. Authors: Jeannie Fulbright (seven titles), Rachael Yunis (Earth Science). Each textbook has 14 lessons; the suggested pace is about two weeks per lesson, roughly 28 weeks per course. Components per course: hardcover or softcover textbook, a spiral Notebooking Journal (roughly grades 3-6) or Junior Notebooking Journal (K-3, less writing), optional audiobook. A household lab supply list is printed in the textbook.
- Junior high: Exploring Creation with General Science (grade 7) and Physical Science (grade 8), 14-16 modules each, lab work with household items.
- High school: Biology, Chemistry, Physics (college-prep lab courses), plus Advanced Biology, Advanced Chemistry, Advanced Physics, Marine Biology, Health and Nutrition, and a no-lab High School Astronomy elective. Biology 3rd edition (author Vicki Dincher): 16 modules over about 34 weeks; the evolution module is the only one with no labs.
- Components at high school: softcover textbook (Biology runs 710+ pages), spiral student notebook (about 550 pages, with daily schedule), solutions and tests manual, student test pages. Optional: audiobook, video instruction USB (Sherri Seligson lectures plus filmed experiments), Self-Paced Online courses (Canvas platform, 15-month access, auto-graded tests), and live online classes.
- Scope and sequence PDFs are published per course (see links below).

## A lesson up close

Elementary (from the parent's seat): the notebooking journal opens with a suggested daily schedule that splits each of the 14 lessons across about two weeks at two sessions per week. A session is: parent reads several pages of conversational text aloud (the reading level is deliberately above primary grades, so K-2 kids listen rather than read), pauses at embedded "Try This" style activities that use household items, then runs the oral "What Do You Remember" review questions. The child then works in the notebooking journal: guided pages with lines for narration-style writing, drawing boxes, note-taking templates, crossword-style vocabulary work, full-color mini-book cut-and-fold templates, and experiment record pages. A completed day produces a journal spread the child can show off. Parents report 30-60 minutes per session. The junior journal swaps writing lines for coloring and simpler prompts, letting one read-aloud serve multiple ages, which is the series' quiet superpower and the reason co-ops like it.

High school (from the student's seat): the student reads a module section on their own, answers embedded "On Your Own" questions (solutions printed in the book), and performs the experiments (three types in biology: household, microscope, dissection; microscope and dissection require purchased kits). At module end they complete a two-to-four-page open-book study guide as a pretest, then take the closed-book module test from the test booklet, which the parent grades from the solutions manual. The student notebook structures daily assignments, note-taking, and lab reports so the student does not have to invent their own system. Expect 45-60 minutes daily, five days a week; reviewers consistently peg upper courses at 5-7 hours weekly.

The page itself: two-column-free, textbook-style layout with full-color photography, generous but not lavish white space, and long prose paragraphs. The notebooking journals are the interactive layer; the textbooks contain no fill-in work. This division (reusable text plus consumable journal) defines the buying pattern.

## Materials and visual design

Elementary textbooks are produced like mainstream school hardcovers: full color, glossy stock, photographic illustration, warm and reverent tone in captions. Newer editions are moving to softcover to hold prices down (noted by Exodus Books). Notebooking journals are spiral-bound, about 9 by 11 inches, printed in color, with varied page anatomies (lined narration pages, drawing frames, mini-book templates, puzzles). High school texts are dense softcovers with infographic-style full-page diagrams in the 3rd editions, spiral student notebooks, and thin stapled test booklets. The aesthetic signals trustworthy Christian traditionalism: polished, photographic, wholesome, closer to a textbook publisher than a boutique brand. The shelf presence of a multi-year Apologia stack is substantial, and the resale market (hardback texts hold value, journals are consumable) is active.

- Elementary: textbook $55 list ($41.25 sale), notebooking journal $39 ($29.25), build-your-own course set $94 ($70.50). One journal per child; the textbook is reusable across siblings, so a second student adds only $39.
- High school print (Biology 3rd edition): textbook $93 list ($69.75 sale), solutions and tests about $28, student notebook about $40-48; retailer bundle of text plus solutions runs $121 list (about $91 on sale at Rainbow Resource). Realistic all-in print cost per course: $120-160 before lab equipment.

- Self-Paced Online: $319 list per course ($239.25 sale), 15-month access, includes digital text, audio, video lessons, filmed experiments, and auto-graded tests.

## Why families choose it

- Worldview trust: the family's theology and the science curriculum agree, so mom never has to pre-screen a page.
- The teen self-teaching design: a 9th grader can run biology without a science-capable parent, which is rare and load-bearing for large families.
- Rigor reputation: college-prep credibility, transcripts co-ops and Christian colleges recognize, AP-adjacent advanced courses.
- The conversational voice: kids who hate textbooks tolerate these.
- Ecosystem depth: audiobooks, video instruction, self-paced online, live classes, co-op familiarity, ubiquitous used-market availability.
- Award halo and word of mouth: it is the default recommendation in Christian homeschool circles, so choosing it is safe.

## Why families leave it

- Worldview mismatch: old-earth, evolution-accepting, or secular families cannot filter the content out; they leave (or never arrive), typically toward Real Science-4-Kids or Elemental Science (neutral) or BFSU and Mystery Science (secular).
- Reading load: 400-plus-page read-alouds exhaust some elementary families; wiggly or visual-first kids stall. Common destinations: The Good and the Beautiful science units (shorter, family-style), Berean Builders (activity-every-lesson), or video-first options.
- One-topic-per-year immersion feels narrow to families who want annual breadth; they move to spiral or survey programs.
- Upper-level time cost (5-7 hours weekly) collides with heavy co-op or sports schedules; some switch to lighter video courses or outsource to live classes.
- Wile loyalists: families who trusted the original author follow him to Berean Builders, especially after edition changes.
- Lab logistics: gathering or buying supplies is a named frustration; families who will not do labs quietly downgrade to reading-only usage or leave for kit-inclusive programs (Noeo) or video-lab options.

## Competitive notes for Homa

- Vocabulary to know: "module" (not chapter), "Young Explorer", "notebooking journal" vs "junior notebooking journal", "On Your Own" questions, "study guide" as open-book pretest, course names like "Zoology 1" and "Swimming Creatures". A mom saying "we're in module 7 of General Science" has a grade-7 student roughly week 13-14 of the year.
- The two-week module cadence and the 14-lesson elementary year are scheduling facts Homa can use to place a family in time and generate aligned materials.
- Materials pattern worth learning: the notebooking journal. Guided pages with mixed anatomies (narration lines, drawing frames, mini-books, vocabulary puzzles, experiment records) plus a printed daily schedule is exactly the artifact shape Homa's generated materials could match for these families.
- The multi-age read-aloud plus per-child journal model is the family-style pattern Homa should respect when a family has several kids on one course.
- Gap Homa fills: per-module household supply lists surfaced a week ahead (supply logistics is a top churn factor); gentle scheduling help for the heavy upper-level courses; review questions and quizzes in Apologia's oral-review style for elementary.
- Worldview behavior: when a family stack includes Apologia, Homa must treat young-earth creationist framing as the family's chosen context. Generated science materials should stay within the family's settings, never inject evolutionary deep-time framing unrequested, and never editorialize either direction. This is exactly the per-family worldview-respect setting the atlas comparison recommends.
- Direct overlap: Apologia's Self-Paced Online line (video, auto-grading, schedule) is the closest thing this publisher has to a software product; it is course-shaped, not assistant-shaped, and has no cross-curriculum awareness. Homa complements more than competes here.
