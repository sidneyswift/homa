# Beautiful Feet Books

## Snapshot

Beautiful Feet Books (BFB) is the flagship of literature-based history: no
textbook at all, just a curated pack of living books (biographies, historical
fiction, picture books, many of them classics BFB brought back into print)
tied together by a full-color teacher guide. Founded in 1984 by Rea Berg, who
holds a degree in children's literature, and still family-owned in Atascadero,
California, BFB sells era-based study packs from kindergarten through high
school, with Early American History for primary grades as its historic core
and newer ancient, medieval, and modern world packs rounding out the map. It
is the Charlotte Mason-adjacent choice for families who believe history is
best learned through beautiful books read together on the couch.

## Reputation and rankings

BFB advertises that all its products are reviewed by Cathy Duffy, and the
Medieval History pack carries a Cathy Duffy Top 103 Pick badge; Duffy's
reviews of the Early American guides are detailed and favorable. The brand has
decades of goodwill in Charlotte Mason and literature-first circles, and its
reprints of classic children's biographies are collector items in their own
right. Community praise centers on the book selection and the aesthetic
pleasure of the materials; customer review scores on pack pages run around 4.6
of 5. Critical threads: the read-aloud and discussion load falls squarely on
the parent; assessment is light (discussion and notebooking, not tests), which
worries box-checkers; full packs are expensive up front; and some of the
vintage literature carries dated cultural portrayals that families debate in
forums. Compared with Sonlight, the other literature giant, BFB is narrower
(history and geography only) and cheaper. Trajectory is stable, with steady
new pack releases (a recent Medieval Intermediate pack) and a loyal base.

## Pedagogy and method

The method is Charlotte Mason by way of the library: living books instead of
textbooks, narration and discussion instead of quizzes, notebooking as the
record of learning, short parent-led lessons built around shared reading.
Guides schedule the reading and then prompt comprehension and character-level
discussion questions, vocabulary, mapping, timeline work, and hands-on extras
(recipes, crafts, web and video links the company calls Rabbit Trails). The
worldview posture is distinctive: written by believers, opening (in Early
American) with an imago Dei framing, deliberately presenting multiple
perspectives (Columbus and Native experience side by side), yet designed to be
usable by non-religious families, with charter-school variants that strip the
explicitly religious titles. Parent involvement is high by design: this is a
curriculum you do with your kids, not one you hand them, until the upper-grade
guides shift toward independent reading and essays.

## Structure and scope

Packs are organized by era and grade band rather than a strict cycle. Current
majors: Early American History (Primary K-3, Intermediate 4-6, and a 7-9
guide), Around the World with Picture Books I and II (K-3 geography and
cultures), Ancient History (Intermediate 5-8 and Senior High 9-12), Medieval
History (5-8, covering 400-1600 including medieval Africa and China and the
Reformation), Modern American and World History (upper grades), plus
California History, History of Science, Geography Through Literature, and
electives like History of the Horse and History of Classical Music. A pack
contains the teacher guide (print plus digital), the full book list (the
Medieval 5-8 pack ships 20 books), and often a timeline and map. Guides are
also sold alone for library users. Lesson counts vary by guide (Cathy Duffy
documents 129 lessons across six units in Early American Intermediate and 82
lessons across five units in the 7-9 guide); packs cover a school year at
three to four sessions per week. Scope and sequence PDFs are downloadable per
pack.

## A lesson up close

A day with the Medieval 5-8 pack: the parent and student read the scheduled
chapter from the current living book (say, a Joan of Arc biography), 20 to 30
minutes aloud or independently. The guide then offers a handful of discussion
questions that move from comprehension toward character and moral reasoning, a
vocabulary word or two, and a notebook prompt: the student writes or draws a
response, adds a timeline figure, or completes a map exercise locating the
events. Some lessons add a Rabbit Trail (a linked video, a recipe, a museum
page) or a hands-on project. There is no worksheet stack and no test; the
notebook the child builds across the year, full of narrations, sketches, maps,
and timeline entries, is the assessment artifact. The parent checks answers
against the guide's notes. A completed week is two to four such sessions plus
sustained progress through one or two real books.

## Materials and visual design

The teacher guides are BFB's design statement: full-color, generously
illustrated with classic art and the packs' own book covers, typeset more like
a trade book than a manual. The literature packs themselves are the product:
real hardcovers and quality paperbacks, including BFB's own reprints of
mid-century classics (the d'Aulaire-era illustrated biography tradition),
which look and feel like a home library rather than a curriculum. Timelines
and maps are poster-format extras. The aesthetic signals heritage and taste:
soft, painterly, bookshelf-proud, the anti-workbook. The box is heavy with
books and light on consumables.

## Pricing

As of Jul 2026, from bfbooks.com: teacher guides about $25-60; complete packs
range roughly $145-290 depending on book count (Medieval History 5-8 is
$289.95 against a $382.96 itemized retail; Early American Primary packs run
lower). The publisher-documented budget path: buy the guide, borrow the books
from the library, spend under $60 for the year. Books are fully reusable
across siblings and hold strong resale value; only the notebook is consumable.
Charter packs (religious titles removed) price similarly.

## Why families choose it

They want history to feel like the best part of the day: real books, together,
no textbook voice. The book curation saves them from vetting hundreds of
titles themselves. The guides supply just enough structure (schedule,
questions, maps, timeline) to make a library-based education feel legitimate.
The gentle Christian posture suits families who want faith-friendly without
doctrine-heavy. And the materials are beautiful enough to keep.

## Why families leave it

The parent is the engine; when read-aloud time collapses (new baby, work), the
program stalls. Families needing grades, tests, or transcript scaffolding move
to Notgrass or textbook programs. Strictly secular families object to the
Bible readings and providential undertones; strongly devout families find it
not Christian enough and go to MOH or TruthQuest. The upfront pack price
stings against SOTW-level alternatives. And some kids simply want more
hands-on or workbook structure than discussion-and-notebook provides.

## Competitive notes for Homa

BFB proves that curation is a product: the pack is a judgment call about which
20 books make an era come alive, and families pay a premium for that judgment.
Homa's analog is dynamic book-list curation (by library availability, reading
level, and sensitivity preferences) plus generated discussion questions and
notebook prompts keyed to whatever chapter a family read today, which is
exactly the guide's job. Vocabulary to know: pack names and grade bands (Early
American Primary versus Intermediate), Rabbit Trails, notebooking. When a
family says they use BFB, Homa should expect no tests, a notebook as the
artifact of record, heavy read-aloud rhythms, and probable gaps in assessment
and writing mechanics that Homa can quietly fill. The charter-pack pattern
(same curriculum, religious content removed) is a ready-made model for Homa's
worldview-sensitivity toggles.
