# The Story of the World

## Snapshot

The Story of the World (SOTW) is a four-volume narrative world history for
elementary students by Susan Wise Bauer, first published in 2001 and now
published by Well-Trained Mind Press. It is the category king of homeschool
history: the publisher says it has reached millions of children over 25 years,
and it is the default first recommendation in nearly every homeschool forum
thread about elementary history. Each volume is a chapter book of story-style
history meant to be read aloud, paired with an Activity Book (now split into
an Instructor Guide and Student Workbook in the new edition) that turns it
into a full history and geography year. SOTW is the practical instrument of
the four-year chronological cycle from Bauer's The Well-Trained Mind:
ancients, medieval, early modern, modern, one year each, repeated with more
depth at each stage. A 25th Anniversary Expanded Edition of Volume 1 shipped
in early 2026, the franchise's biggest refresh ever.

## Reputation and rankings

SOTW is one of Cathy Duffy's 103 Top Picks, and her review calls the four
volumes "valuable contributions" for classically oriented history study.
Retail copy describes it as an award winner across homeschool magazines and
reader polls, though specific award years are not well documented (unverified
beyond the publisher's claim). Community standing is enormous but contested
from both flanks. Secular families point out that biblical narratives get
generous story-length treatment (Abraham, Joseph, Moses, the resurrection of
Jesus), which is why r/secularhomeschool threads often route people to
Curiosity Chronicles or History Quest instead. Christian families sometimes
want more explicit providential framing and move to The Mystery of History.
Long-running community error lists exist (the Imaginative Homeschool blog
maintains a chapter-by-chapter list for Volume 1, updated through 2025), and
critics raise Eurocentrism and oversimplification concerns, especially in the
modern volume. Cathy Duffy notes Volume 3 feels "a little scattered" and that
religion nearly disappears from Volume 4. None of this has dented its
trajectory: the 25th anniversary edition, new audiobook, and continued forum
dominance signal a stable-to-rising franchise.

## Pedagogy and method

The intellectual lineage is the trivium as popularized by The Well-Trained
Mind: grammar-stage students absorb stories and facts, so history is taught as
narrative, chronologically, starting with the ancients in first grade rather
than the community-helpers spiral of public school social studies. Bauer picks
key characters and events and tells them at story depth instead of surveying
everything thinly. Retention mechanics come from the activity program, not the
text: oral review questions after each reading, a two-to-five sentence
narration (a Charlotte Mason technique; the parent scribes for young
children), map work, and optional hands-on projects. There is no formal
assessment until the optional test books (recommended for grades 4 and up).
The parent's role is reader and discussion leader; sessions run roughly 20 to
40 minutes. The same four-period cycle is designed to be repeated in grades 5
to 8 (logic stage, with outlining and primary sources) and again in high
school, which is why SOTW families rarely leave the ecosystem even after
outgrowing these books.

## Structure and scope

Four volumes, each one school year:

- Volume 1, Ancient Times: earliest nomads to the last Roman emperor. Target
  grades 1-4. The 2026 25th Anniversary Expanded Edition is 349 pages with new
  chapters on China, Korea, and the Silk Road plus ten new mini-biographies.
- Volume 2, The Middle Ages: fall of Rome to the Renaissance. Grades 2-5.
- Volume 3, Early Modern Times: Elizabeth I to the California Gold Rush. Grades 3-6.
- Volume 4, The Modern Age: 1850 to the end of the twentieth century. Grades
  4-8, written more like a survey text and more comprehensive than the earlier
  volumes.

Each classic volume has 42 chapters divided into roughly 96 sections (most
chapters have two); families fit them to a 36-week year by doubling up on
short chapters. Components per volume: the text (hardcover, paperback, PDF,
Kindle, audiobook), the Activity Book (curriculum guide plus reproducible
student pages), an optional test book with answer key, and optional extras
(Review Questions Worksheets PDF, coloring book). The 25th anniversary edition
of Volume 1 reorganizes this into an explicit lesson structure: text,
Instructor Guide (206 pages, with parent scripts for narration, review, and
mapping), Student Workbook (130 perforated pages), Tests and Answer Key, and a
Review Questions Worksheets PDF. The old activity books cross-reference the
Kingfisher and Usborne world history encyclopedias as picture-reference spines
and carry long literature and library lists per chapter. Coverage is world
history with a Western center of gravity but chapters on India, China, Africa,
and the Americas throughout.

## A lesson up close

A typical SOTW day at the classic editions: the parent reads one chapter
section aloud (5 to 15 minutes; many families play the audiobook instead and
follow along). The parent asks the Activity Book's review questions orally,
sample answers provided. The child gives a short narration, which the parent
writes down for young students or the child writes for older ones; the guide
includes sample narrations. Then one or two extras: the blackline map exercise
for the chapter (circle a city, trace a river in blue, draw a route), a
coloring page tied to the story, or one project chosen from several offered
(the famous Volume 1 examples include mummifying a chicken and building paper
models; project instructions occupy much of the guide). Review cards,
reproducible on cardstock, accumulate as a review deck. A completed week
produces a narration page, a map, maybe a coloring page or craft, which many
families bind into a history notebook. In the 25th anniversary edition the
same rhythm is packaged as numbered lessons with the student-facing pages
(puzzles, board games, craft templates, memory work, maps, coloring)
consolidated in the workbook. The tests, when used, are one page per chapter:
multiple choice, matching, fill in the blank, and true or false, with sentence
answers appearing from Volume 2 and essay questions in Volume 4.

## Materials and visual design

The texts are trade-paperback chapter books (also hardcover), black and white,
with line-art illustrations and simple maps; the aesthetic is storybook, not
textbook, and visually quiet by design. The 25th anniversary Volume 1 adds new
illustrations and maps and clearer formatting that distinguishes myths and
ancient texts from historical narrative. Activity books are thick (Volume 2's
runs 464 pages), utilitarian, black and white, with reproducible student pages
segregated at the back for easier copying. The signature material artifact of
the franchise is arguably the audiobook: the classic volumes are narrated by
Jim Weiss, the beloved storyteller whose voice is, for a generation of
homeschool kids, the sound of history class (Volume 1 runs about 8 hours;
Volume 4 nearly 13). The new Volume 1 edition is narrated by Katherine de
Leiros, 9.5 hours, distributed on Audible, Apple, and Spotify as well as
direct. Compared with full-color competitors (Mystery of History's
third-edition hardbacks, Curiosity Chronicles' color spreads), SOTW's pages
look austere; its sensory richness lives in the read-aloud voice and the
hands-on projects.

## Pricing

As of Jul 2026, from the publisher and major retailers (welltrainedmind.com
lists all components):

- Minimum viable: the text alone, about $12-17 paperback per volume ($16.95
  for the 25th anniversary Volume 1 paperback).
- Typical: text plus activity book (classic volumes about $20-40 by retailer;
  Volume 4's is the priciest) plus optional tests (about $9-13). Roughly
  $50-75 per year.
- Everything: add the audiobook ($27.95 list, $23.76 direct for the new Volume
  1; Jim Weiss editions about $22-45 by retailer) and coloring book. Under
  $120 per year.

Texts and activity books are reusable across siblings (student pages are
family-reproducible or purchasable separately), so multi-kid economics are
excellent. Resale is famously liquid: used SOTW is everywhere, and older
editions retain value. The 2006 edition remains on sale while remaining
volumes await their anniversary revisions; the new Volume 1 components are not
compatible with old editions.

## Why families choose it

It makes history the favorite subject: kids ask for the next chapter. It is
open-and-go for a tired parent (read, ask, narrate, map). The audiobook
converts car time into school time. It fits the classical culture many
families already bought into via The Well-Trained Mind, and everyone in the
co-op is using it, so tribal knowledge and used copies are abundant. It reads
aloud well to mixed ages, and the activity guides explicitly serve a grade
spread. It is cheap relative to boxed history programs.

## Why families leave it

Secular families leave over the weight of biblical narrative and the storybook
blending of scripture, myth, and history (destinations: Curiosity Chronicles,
History Quest, Build Your Library). Devout families leave for explicitly
Christ-centered chronology (destination: The Mystery of History, BiblioPlan,
TruthQuest). Some families find the pace slow and story-heavy relative to
facts covered, or tire of activity-book prep. Accuracy-minded parents cite the
community error lists. And everyone eventually ages out: Volume 4 tops out
around grade 8, pushing families to Notgrass, History Odyssey, or high school
texts (Bauer's own The History of the Ancient World series is the adult
continuation).

## Competitive notes for Homa

SOTW defines the vocabulary Homa must speak: "we're in SOTW 2", "we do the AG
questions and map work", "we listen to Jim Weiss in the car" are complete
descriptions of a family's history program, and Homa should resolve volume
numbers to eras and grade bands automatically. Materials lessons: narration as
the default comprehension check (not worksheets), review questions with sample
answers for the parent, map work as a first-class weekly artifact, and project
options at three effort levels. The gaps Homa can fill: the
42-chapters-into-36-weeks scheduling problem; auto-generating the
age-differentiated outputs the guides only gesture at (a narration prompt for
the 6-year-old, an outline for the 11-year-old, from the same chapter);
supplement matching from its long library lists by what a family can actually
get; and a secular-sensitivity toggle, since the single most common SOTW
complaint is worldview balance in either direction. Homa-generated history
materials will be compared to the SOTW activity guide by every classical
family; match its narration-review-map spine before innovating past it.
