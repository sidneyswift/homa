# Scratch (and ScratchJr)

## Snapshot

Scratch is the default answer to "how do we start coding" in homeschooling,
and it is not a curriculum at all: it is a free, browser-based block-coding
platform and online community where kids ages 8 to 16 build games,
animations, and interactive stories by snapping code blocks together.
Mitchel Resnick's Lifelong Kindergarten group at the MIT Media Lab launched
it in 2007; since 2019 the independent nonprofit Scratch Foundation runs
it, funded by donations, with a stated reach of 200 million kids and over
one billion projects created. ScratchJr, a simpler tablet app for ages 5
to 7 co-developed with the DevTech Research Group, needs no reading and
has logged over 60 million users of its own. Because Scratch ships no
lessons, no sequence, and no assessment, the homeschool market has grown a
whole ecosystem of free and cheap wrapper curricula around it, and "we do
Scratch" can mean anything from a structured weekly course to a kid alone
in the sandbox.

## Reputation and rankings

- Scratch holds default status the way Khan Academy does in math practice:
  it wins no homeschool award circuits and needs none, because every
  coding roundup, co-op class, and library program starts there. A 2026
  homeschool guide rates it 5/5 and calls it the answer "for most students
  ages 8-14."
- The consistent praise: genuinely free, real programming concepts under
  the colorful surface, tight feedback loop, and intrinsic motivation
  (kids come back because they want to finish their own game).
- The consistent complaints, in forums and reviews: no structure, so
  self-direction quietly becomes playing other people's games instead of
  making them; the open community (comments, shared projects) needs the
  same supervision judgment as any social platform, though it is
  moderated; and a child can plateau at copy-and-tweak without a parent
  ever knowing.
- Institutional trust is unusually deep: schools adopted it wholesale, and
  when Google retired its free Scratch-based CS First curriculum in June
  2025 it pointed everyone at a Raspberry Pi Foundation replacement built
  on Scratch again (Experience CS), which reads as the whole field
  agreeing Scratch is infrastructure.
- Trajectory: rising. The foundation is building Scratch 4.0 (with AI
  features) and has a stated goal of reaching 100 million more children
  by 2027.

## Pedagogy and method

The intellectual lineage is constructionism: Resnick was a student of
Seymour Papert (Logo), and Scratch is the Papert tradition's mainstream
product. The Lifelong Kindergarten framing is the four P's (projects,
passion, peers, play) and "low floor, high ceiling, wide walls": easy to
start, room to grow, many kinds of projects. There is deliberately no
lesson sequence. Kids learn by making something they care about, hitting a
wall, and finding the block or technique that gets through it; remixing
other people's shared projects is a designed-in learning mechanic, not
cheating. The blocks themselves teach real concepts: sequences, loops,
conditionals, variables, events, message broadcasting between sprites,
parallelism, and custom blocks (functions). Because blocks snap rather
than parse, syntax errors do not exist, which is exactly the training
wheel that later makes the move to typed languages feel like a cliff (see
the coding comparison doc). ScratchJr strips the model down further for
pre-readers: icon blocks, no variables, animation-first.

## Structure and scope

Scratch has no levels, so structure comes from what a family wraps around
it. The wrappers that matter, all free unless noted:

- Built-in tutorials and coding cards: guided first projects inside the
  editor; the standard first two weeks.
- Creative Computing Curriculum (Harvard Graduate School of Education):
  the closest thing to an official course, seven units from "getting
  started" through games, with teacher guides and student workbooks;
  widely used by homeschoolers and co-ops.
- Experience CS (Raspberry Pi Foundation, backed by Google): a free,
  classroom-managed Scratch with 18 project units for ages 8 to 14,
  launched for the 2025-26 school year as the successor to Google's
  retired CS First.
- Books: Super Scratch Programming Adventure and DK's Scratch project
  books (about $20) give a project-per-chapter spine many families prefer
  to screens-only instruction.
- YouTube: the Griffpatch channel is the community's acknowledged
  advanced track (multi-part builds of real games).
- Paid wrappers: Outschool classes, co-op coding clubs, and course
  platforms (CodaKid and Tynker both include Scratch or Scratch-like
  tracks) sell structure and accountability on top of the free tool.

A commonly recommended homeschool cadence is two to three sessions a week
of 30 to 45 minutes, one session learning a technique and the rest
building, with project ambition ramping from an animated card to a
chase game to a multi-level platformer over a year.

## A lesson up close

There is no teacher script; a Scratch "lesson" is a project session. The
child opens the editor: stage top left showing the running project, sprite
list below it, block palette down the left edge color-coded by category
(motion blue, looks purple, control orange, and so on), and the script
area in the middle where blocks snap into stacks. A beginner following a
tutorial card builds a stack under "when green flag clicked," runs it
instantly, and adjusts numbers to see what changes. A more experienced
kid opens their saved game, reproduces a bug, and hunts the logic error
across two sprites' scripts, which is debugging in the real sense. A
finished session produces a project that runs, saved to the account and
optionally shared to the community, where comments and remixes arrive
from other kids. What it does not produce is any record a parent can
file: no score, no completed-lesson list, no artifact for a portfolio
unless the family invents one. Parents who want evidence resort to
screenshots, project links, and narration ("tell me how it works").

## Materials and visual design

Scratch is a screen product with a strong visual identity: the orange cat
mascot, chunky rounded blocks in saturated category colors, a big
friendly stage. It reads as playful without being babyish, which is why
it holds kids into middle school. ScratchJr is visibly younger: bigger
blocks, icon-only, pastel scenes. The optional physical layer is thin but
real: printable or purchasable coding cards, the project books' comic
styling, and unofficial printable planning sheets families use to make
project work feel like school. Everything runs in a browser or the free
offline editor, on cheap hardware, which quietly matters for large
families.

## Pricing

Free, structurally: the Scratch Foundation is a donation-funded nonprofit
and states its commitment to keeping the platform free, as of Jul 2026.
There are no accounts to buy, no premium tier, and no ads. ScratchJr is a
free tablet app. Real family spending attaches to the wrappers: a $20
project book, an Outschool class, a co-op club fee, or a paid platform
subscription when parents decide they need structure and progress
reporting (see the coding comparison doc for that market's economics).

## Why families choose it

- Free forever, from a nonprofit, with no upsell pressure: the rare
  elective with zero purchase anxiety.
- The kid asks to do it. Coding via making games is the elective that
  self-motivates, which matters in the guilt-subjects dynamic
  (`../../comparisons/languages-electives.md`).
- No parent expertise needed: tutorials are self-serve and the parent
  role is encouragement plus time-boxing.
- ScratchJr-to-Scratch is a natural K-8 arc a family can start at age 5.
- It is the lingua franca: every library club, co-op class, and coding
  book assumes it.

## Why families leave it

- Structure hunger: parents who cannot tell whether play is progress buy
  a sequenced platform (Tynker, CodeCombat, CodaKid) mostly to get
  lessons, dashboards, and the feeling of a course.
- The text-coding cliff: around ages 11 to 13, kids who have mastered
  blocks either move to Python or JavaScript or stall; Scratch itself
  offers no bridge, and the move is the category's classic churn moment.
- Drift: the community is fun, and some kids slide into playing and
  chatting rather than building; parents respond by supervising, turning
  off sharing, or switching to a closed platform.
- Ceiling cases: a minority of ambitious kids hit real platform limits
  (performance, 3D, code export) and leave for Godot, Roblox Studio, or
  plain Python early.

## Competitive notes for Homa

- Vocabulary to know: sprites, the stage, blocks and scripts, remixing,
  studios (project collections), the green flag, ScratchJr vs Scratch.
  "She is really into Scratch" means self-directed project work with no
  external record; ask what she is building, not what lesson she is on.
- The structure gap is Homa's entire lane here: Scratch deliberately
  ships no sequence, no records, and no assessment. A family assistant
  that turns a child's actual projects into a plan (one technique per
  week), a log (projects, hours, concepts demonstrated), and eventually a
  transcript line does what the free tool never will, without competing
  with it.
- Watch the cliff: a 12-year-old deep in Scratch is a predictable
  transition moment. Homa can see it coming from age plus stack and can
  suggest the bridge (a first Python project tied to what they already
  built) before frustration or boredom decides instead.
- The interest signal is gold: what a kid builds in Scratch (games,
  animation, music tools) is a direct read on interests Homa can use
  across every other subject's materials.
- Learn from the four P's: project, passion, peers, play is a proven
  engagement engine for self-directed learning, and it is the same logic
  as Homa generating practice around a child's obsessions.
