# ARTistic Pursuits

## Snapshot

ARTistic Pursuits is the structured art curriculum of homeschooling: a
PreK-12 series of hardcover art courses by Brenda Ellis (honors graduate
of the Kansas City Art Institute, 25+ years teaching K-12 art), built on
the conviction that children should make original art from real
observation rather than copy crafts. The first book (a high school
drawing text co-created with her husband Daniel) shipped in 1999; the
current editions pair each hardcover with professional video instruction
on DVD, Blu-ray, and lifetime streaming. Each course interleaves art
history and appreciation (masterwork plates with discussion questions)
with technique instruction in professional-grade media (charcoal,
pastels, gouache, clay, watercolor). It is the perennial "real art, not
crafts" answer: Cathy Duffy Top Picks (101 and 102 editions), and first
places in Practical Homeschooling's fine arts categories plus the
Excellence in Education art award across 2006-2020. The publisher
self-reports over one hundred thousand users.

## Reputation and rankings

- Award shelf, per the publisher's awards page and third-party review
  crews: repeated Practical Homeschooling Reader Award first places in
  elementary, middle school, and high school art; Excellence in
  Education art-category winner from 2006 to 2020; Cathy Duffy 101 and
  102 Top Picks (her 103 edition dropped electives categories entirely
  for space, which is itself a telling fact about how the market ranks
  electives).
- Cathy Duffy's verdict: real synergy across the series, professional
  media choices over school-grade tempera and crayons, and enough scope
  per lesson that one book can serve several children at different
  depths.
- Community sentiment (Reddit, Well-Trained Mind, review blogs):
  praised as a genuine art education for non-artist parents; the two
  standing complaints are cost (books plus artist-grade supplies) and
  K-3 setup and cleanup labor, since young kids cannot run gouache and
  clay days alone.
- Video editions receive high marks for clarity; Cathy Duffy separately
  reviewed the text-and-video series as a "see and do" solution for
  parents who do not want to figure out how to teach art.
- Trajectory: stable category leader for structured art, sitting
  opposite [You ARE an Artist](you-are-an-artist.md)'s follow-along
  warmth; families sort themselves by whether they want art taught as a
  discipline or enjoyed as a family activity.

## Pedagogy and method

The method teaches children to see like artists, then make original
compositions, explicitly not step-by-step copying: lessons introduce a
concept (line, shadow, balance, depth), show it working in a masterwork,
demonstrate the technique, then send the child to compose their own
piece from observation of real objects, places, and people. That
original-composition stance is the program's core differentiator against
follow-along products (the publisher and reviewers both frame it this
way against Creating a Masterpiece and Home Art Studio). Art history is
integrated rather than separate: K-3 volumes walk chronologically from
cave painting toward modern art; the older-edition levels focus on
American art (grades 4-5), world art (middle school), and European
masters (high school). Media mastery is deliberate: early levels sample
widely, upper levels narrow to fewer media and build real expertise.
Parent role is facilitator: read or play the lesson, stage supplies,
supervise; the teaching is in the book and video.

## Structure and scope

- Preschool: The Way They See It (ages 3-5), a parent-education book as
  much as a child's course.
- K-3: eight slim hardcover volumes (about 64 pages, 18 lessons each);
  two volumes (36 lessons) make one school year. The sequence runs
  chronological art history: Vol. 2 Art of the Ancients, Vol. 6 Art of
  the Impressionists, Vol. 8 Art in America, and so on.
- Grades 4-5, middle school, and high school: paired volumes per band
  (The Elements of Art and Composition, then Color and Composition),
  each a year's course; older editions carried 68 projects per book.
  The same titles repeat across bands at rising sophistication, so
  "Elements of Art, Middle School" is a real course name a mom will
  say.
- Components per volume: hardcover text with masterwork plates, DVD and
  Blu-ray discs, optional lifetime streaming, and an optional
  per-volume art supply pack (curated artist-grade materials: 5 lb
  air-dry clay, Blick pastels, gouache sets, Prismacolor pencils, in a
  branded tote).
- Lesson pattern (K-3, per reviewer walkthroughs): page one teaches the
  concept, page two presents a masterwork with discussion prompts, page
  three is the hands-on project applying the concept.
- Pace: one lesson per week is the designed default.

## A lesson up close

A K-3 session starts on the couch: the parent reads the concept page
aloud (what artists see in shapes, say), then the child studies the
masterwork reproduction while the parent asks the printed discussion
questions, a picture-study rhythm CM families will recognize. Then the
table gets messy: the project page (or the video demonstration) shows
the technique, and the child makes their own picture, not a copy of an
exemplar, from observation or memory. Twenty to forty-five minutes,
most of it child work time. An upper-level session is more studio-like:
the student reads the element being taught, watches the video
demonstration, and executes a composition assignment in the target
medium, building toward a portfolio across the year. What a completed
week produces is one finished original piece per child plus absorbed
art history, and, cumulatively, a body of work that high schoolers can
legitimately call a fine arts credit.

## Materials and visual design

Hardcover books with full-color masterwork reproductions on heavy
stock, clean traditional layout, serious but not austere: closer to a
real art text than a workbook. Video production is straightforward
demonstration filming. The supply packs are the tell: real artist
materials (Amaco clay, Blick pastels, Pelikan gouache, Prismacolor)
rather than craft-aisle substitutes, signaling to the buyer that this
is art as a discipline. The aesthetic message is museum-and-studio
seriousness, the opposite pole from chalk-dust playfulness, and
families read the difference accurately when choosing between the two
art defaults.

## Pricing

As of Jul 2026, from publisher product pages (linked; bundles shift):

- Per volume: $59 (hardcover plus DVD and Blu-ray), $79 with lifetime
  online access added, or $59 online-only.
- Two-volume year bundles (K-3): about $99-139 depending on format
  (publisher advertises $19 savings per bundle).
- Art supply packs: about $86-104 per volume, the real budget line;
  reviewer guidance ranges $47-275 across books and bundles.
- Full K-3 eight-volume bundle: about $632.
- Economics: books and videos are non-consumable and famously reusable
  across siblings (lifetime streaming helps); supplies are the
  recurring cost. Resale on the hardcovers is healthy, standard 40-60%
  patterns.

## Why families choose it

- "Real art, not crafts": parents who want drawing, composition, and
  media technique taught as a subject buy the category's most
  credentialed option.
- Open-and-go for the art-intimidated parent: the book and video carry
  the instruction; no Pinterest prep.
- Integrated art history: one purchase covers appreciation and studio
  skills, which reads as efficient to box-checking planners.
- Award and Top Picks pedigree: the safest structured-art pick under
  social proof.
- High school credibility: upper courses produce a defensible fine
  arts credit with portfolio evidence.

## Why families leave it

- Cost stack: $59-79 per volume plus $90-100 supply packs, times
  siblings' consumable materials, gets cut in tight years; families
  drift to free YouTube art, library books, or a co-op class.
- Parent labor at K-3: staging clay and gouache for a five-year-old is
  real work; tired parents switch to lower-mess video art
  (chalk pastels, drawing apps).
- Kids who want follow-along fun: children who just want a pretty
  finished picture find observation-and-composition assignments
  frustrating; those families land on You ARE an Artist or Creating a
  Masterpiece.
- Elective triage: art is a guilt subject, first skipped in busy
  seasons regardless of curriculum quality (the pattern named in
  `../../comparisons/languages-electives.md`).

## Competitive notes for Homa

- Vocabulary to know: the level bands (K-3 Vols. 1-8, Elements of Art
  and Composition, Color and Composition at 4-5, MS, HS), supply
  packs, the video editions, Brenda Ellis. "We're in Elements, middle
  school" identifies both course and band.
- The three-page lesson grammar (concept, masterwork discussion,
  original project) is a strong pattern for Homa-generated art
  materials: it cleanly separates knowledge, appreciation, and
  creation, and it never asks the child to copy.
- Respect the original-work ethic: Homa should never generate
  "draw-this" exemplars for AP families; discussion prompts, supply
  staging checklists, and portfolio logging are the aligned
  supplements.
- Gap Homa fills: supply logistics and session staging (the number one
  K-3 friction), plus turning weekly pieces into a dated portfolio
  and, at high school, a credit record with hours. The publisher sells
  supply packs but nothing manages the week-to-week execution.
- The awards page is a case study in elective award economics
  (Excellence in Education wins 2006-2020, PHS firsts, Top Picks
  badges compounding for a decade); see `market-overview.md` on why
  award shelf-life is so long in this market.
