# Getting Started with Latin, Spanish, and French (series)

## Snapshot

The Getting Started with... series is a family of single-volume,
self-teaching language books by William E. Linney (Armfield Academic
Press, first title 2007), each subtitled "for Homeschoolers and
Self-Taught Students of Any Age." The method is radical minimalism: one
new word or one new concept per lesson, a handful of translation
sentences to apply it, an answer key in the back, and free MP3 recordings
(native-speaker pronunciation plus the author talking through every
lesson) on each book's website. The Latin volume is the famous one, 134
lessons built on only about 90 vocabulary words, and it is the standing
community answer to "we want to try Latin without buying a program."
The series now spans Latin (plus the sequel Keep Going with Latin),
Spanish, French, Ancient Greek, German, and Russian, with Spanish
carrying a Well-Trained Mind top recommendation. Mindshare is large
relative to its footprint: it is the budget on-ramp the big language
curricula get compared against.

## Reputation and rankings

- Cathy Duffy reviews the Latin, Spanish, and French volumes together
  and favorably: gentle, genuinely self-instructional, best for middle
  school and up (younger kids need a parent alongside), "a substantial
  course for an amazingly low price" when paired with the free audio.
- The Well-Trained Mind selected Getting Started with Spanish as a top
  recommendation (noted on the author's own site), and WTM forum threads
  routinely name the Latin book as the lowest-friction Latin start.
- Long-form reviewer verdicts match: a Latin-teaching blogger calls it
  "a perfect guide to the very basic first steps of Latin, particularly
  for the fearful beginner," praising the one-concept pacing, the white
  space, and the derivative and expression sidebars, while noting the
  limits: repetitive practice sentences and no English-to-Latin
  composition exercises.
- Honest ceiling, stated by every reviewer: these are not full
  first-year high school courses. The Latin book covers only the first
  two declensions and two conjugations; vocabulary counts are small by
  design. It is an on-ramp, not a credit.
- Trajectory: stable classic. The books rarely change editions, the
  price stays low, and each new language volume (Russian, German,
  Ancient Greek) extends the same formula.

## Pedagogy and method

One concept per day is the whole philosophy. Linney's argument is that
language programs fail self-taught students by stacking new grammar, new
vocabulary, and new endings simultaneously; his books hold vocabulary
nearly constant so working memory goes to grammar. Lessons are numbered,
a page or less, and always end in immediate application: translate up to
ten short sentences, check the key. Grammar is taught explicitly and in
plain English (articles, declensions, conjugations named and explained),
which places the series in the grammar-translation lineage, softened to
micro-doses. The free audio does two jobs: native pronunciation of every
word and exercise, and separate "author's commentary" tracks where
Linney teaches each lesson aloud, which converts the paperback into
something close to a recorded course. There is no writing curriculum, no
games, no pictures, and no assessment beyond the exercises; mastery is
self-evident (you translated the sentences or you did not). A child of
about 9-10 with solid reading can self-run it; many families do it aloud
together at breakfast.

## Structure and scope

- One paperback per language, no levels, no teacher edition, no
  consumables. Latin: 134 lessons, about 90 words, first and second
  declensions, first and second conjugations. Spanish: 177 lessons.
  French: 172 lessons plus an upfront pronunciation section (French
  orthography demands it). Answer key, glossary, pronunciation guide,
  and index in the back of each.
- Sequels and siblings: Keep Going with Latin continues the Latin
  grammar and pushes spoken use; Getting Started with Ancient Greek,
  German, and Russian follow the same formula. Linney also runs
  "Linney's Latin Class," a free online continuation course with
  lectures and homework built around a public-domain textbook.
- Pace is unscheduled by design: a lesson a day finishes the Latin book
  in a school year; ambitious families double up. There is no placement
  test and none is needed; everyone starts at lesson 1.

## A lesson up close

A lesson is one small idea rendered in large type on an uncluttered
page. Early Latin lessons literally introduce one word (nauta, sailor),
explain one fact (Latin has no articles), then offer sentences like
"Sum nauta" to translate. The parent's role, if any, is to sit nearby,
play the pronunciation MP3, and check the child's oral or written
translations against the key. A day takes five to fifteen minutes. Every
few lessons a boxed feature explains a Latin expression still alive in
English (ex libris, B.C./A.D.), which is most of the cultural content.
Declension and conjugation charts appear as they are earned, a few cells
at a time. What a completed day produces is a handful of translated
sentences in a notebook, and, cumulatively, a child who has met real
grammar without tears. The page anatomy is the anti-workbook: no art, no
color, wide margins, nothing to fill in on the page itself.

## Materials and visual design

Plain self-published paperbacks, roughly 8.5 by 11 inches, black and
white throughout, generous white space, simple serif text and clean
tables. No illustrations beyond the occasional chart. The aesthetic
signals "just the language, calmly," and reviewers explicitly praise the
restfulness of the layout. The books are non-consumable (exercises are
translated aloud or in a notebook), so one copy serves every child in
the family and survives resale. Audio is downloadable MP3 from each
book's plain-HTML website, no app, no account, no subscription.

## Pricing

- About $15-24 per book street price as of Jul 2026: Cathy Duffy's
  pricing block lists Getting Started with Latin at $15.41 on Amazon and
  $22.50 at Rainbow Resource, the French and Spanish volumes around
  $22.95 (link: https://cathyduffyreviews.com/homeschool-reviews-core-curricula/foreign-language/latin/getting-started-with-latin-with-french-or-with-spanish).
- All audio (pronunciation and lesson commentary) is free on the book
  sites. There is nothing else to buy: no teacher edition, tests, or
  subscription.
- Non-consumable, so the effective multi-kid and resale economics are
  about as good as curriculum gets. This is the cheapest serious
  foreign-language start in the market.

## Why families choose it

- Price and simplicity: one cheap book, zero setup, zero subscription,
  and the audio is free.
- Fear removal: parents who never studied a language can learn one page
  ahead of the child, or just play the author's commentary.
- The "try before committing" move: families test whether Latin or
  Spanish will stick before spending $100+ on a full program (often
  before Latin for Children or Memoria's ladder; see
  `../all-in-one/classical-academic-press.md` and
  `../all-in-one/memoria-press.md`).
- Independence: an older child can genuinely self-run it, which is rare
  in foreign language below the app tier.
- Any-age design: the same book works for a 9-year-old, a teen, and the
  mom herself, so some families run it as a family subject.

## Why families leave it

- They finish it: the books are designed to be outgrown, and graduates
  move to Latin for Children, Latin Alive, Henle, or a live class for
  Latin, and to Breaking the Barrier, Spanish for Children, tutors, or
  apps for modern languages. Leaving is the success case.
- Proficiency expectations: families wanting conversation and listening
  skill in Spanish or French find grammar-translation micro-lessons too
  slow and too text-bound; they move to immersion apps, TalkBox.mom, or
  co-op classes.
- Credit needs: a high schooler needing a transcriptable "Spanish I"
  needs more scope than these books cover, so families needing credit
  start elsewhere or supplement heavily.
- Kids who need color and game mechanics: the plain pages that adults
  find restful read as boring to some children, who do better with Song
  School-style materials or Duolingo's reward loop (see
  [duolingo](duolingo.md)).

## Competitive notes for Homa

- Vocabulary to know: "GSWL" (the community abbreviation for the Latin
  book), Keep Going with Latin, Linney's Latin Class, "one lesson a
  day." A mom saying "we're doing GSWL at breakfast" means a low-key,
  5-15 minute family Latin habit, not a formal program with levels.
- The one-concept-per-day lesson grain is a materials pattern worth
  stealing: it is the most parent-trusted pacing model in homeschool
  foreign language, and Homa-generated practice for language learners
  should default to the same micro-dose shape rather than worksheet
  volume.
- Gap Homa fills: the series produces no records and no assessment.
  Turning "we finished lesson 60" into a progress record, and bridging
  graduates to a next program by mapping what GSWL actually covered
  (two declensions, two conjugations) onto the destination program's
  entry point, is exactly the clerical layer nobody owns.
- These books prove the demand for language-curious families who are
  not yet committed. Homa should treat "trying Latin" as a real,
  common stack state, distinct from "doing a Latin program."
- No competitive overlap: this is a $20 book with free audio. Homa
  complements it (scheduling, records, next-step advice) rather than
  competing.
