# You ARE an Artist (Chalk Pastels with Nana)

## Snapshot

You ARE an Artist is the Charlotte Mason circle's favorite art program: a
family video-art membership at chalkpastel.com taught by Lucia "Nana"
Hames, a grandmother who started by teaching her five grandchildren at
her daughter Tricia Hodges's kitchen table, blogged since 2010, and grew
into a library of 800+ video lessons (275-300 hours). The whole product
is built on one disarming constraint: a starter set of chalk pastels and
construction paper are the only supplies, ever. Lessons are short,
multi-age, and deliberately cross-curricular (the "I Drew It Then I Knew
It" series pairs art with history, geography, literature, science, and
Bible topics), which is exactly how CM and unit-study families want art
to behave. The company self-reports over 21,000 member families. Nana
herself, warm and endlessly encouraging ("you ARE an artist" is the
catchphrase and thesis), is the brand.

## Reputation and rankings

- Reviewer sentiment across homeschool blogs is uniformly affectionate:
  the recurring themes are "art finally happens at our house," genuine
  multi-age usability (preschool through teens at one table), and the
  supply-list relief ([Proverbial Homemaker](https://www.proverbialhomemaker.com/you-are-an-artist.html),
  [Humility and Doxology](https://www.humilityanddoxology.com/homeschool-art-chalk-pastels/),
  [The Curriculum Choice](https://thecurriculumchoice.com/you-are-an-artist-clubhouse-membership-review/)).
- Community-native touches drive loyalty: members who post finished
  pieces and tag the company report personal encouragement notes from
  Nana; live member events and the online Summer Art Camp function as
  video events the kids anticipate.
- Standing critiques are mild: the library is almost exclusively chalk
  pastel (medium monotony for kids who want to progress into drawing,
  watercolor, or acrylics), advanced teens can outgrow it, and the
  library's size overwhelms newcomers until they find the monthly
  calendars.
- No award-circuit presence to speak of; its mindshare is grown through
  the CM blog and podcast ecosystem, Instagram-friendly results, and
  word of mouth, the TGTB-style social path rather than the reader-award
  path (see `market-overview.md`).
- Trajectory: stable and beloved in its niche, with the Fine Arts tier
  (added for high schoolers, marketed as transcript-credit-worthy)
  extending the ceiling.

## Pedagogy and method

The method is follow-along video demonstration, Bob Ross grammar tuned
for children: Nana draws, narrates each stroke, and the child draws
along in real time, with technique (blending, layering, smudging light
into skies) absorbed by imitation rather than taught as theory. There is
no grading, no critique, and deliberately no prerequisite skill; the
pedagogy's core claim is confidence-first, that every child (and mom)
already is an artist. Lessons run roughly 10-20 minutes and are designed
to attach to whatever the family is studying: paint the Mayflower during
colonial history week, a hummingbird during nature study. That
integration posture (art as a way of knowing the topic, not a separate
skill silo) is why CM families, who already do picture study and nature
journals, adopt it so readily. Parent involvement is near zero: queue
the video, distribute paper, vacuum chalk dust later.

## Structure and scope

- Library: 800+ video lessons, 275-300 hours, organized by subject
  (history, geography and maps, literature, poetry, science, nature,
  seasonal, Bible, STEAM) and by series, with an index page and monthly
  art calendars curating a default path.
- Membership tiers as of Jul 2026: Clubhouse Complete ($40/mo, all
  lessons plus seasonal lessons, master classes, parent lessons, Summer
  Camp, members' community, I Drew It Then I Knew It companion guides);
  plus Fine Arts ($50/mo) adding the high-school-directed art and music
  appreciation curriculum; an everything tier ($60/mo) folding in the
  nature study membership and hour challenges. A cheaper partial tier
  (A Simple Start) exists for starters.
- Fine Arts strand: grades 9-12 material (famous artists and composers,
  art appreciation) that the publisher markets as qualifying for high
  school fine-arts transcript credit, with a Visual Art Credit Tracker
  printable in the member dashboard.
- One membership covers one family; co-ops buy separate co-op pricing.
- Supplies: one starter chalk pastel set (a few dollars per child) and
  construction paper. Some acrylic lessons exist as outliers.

## A lesson up close

The family pulls out pastels and paper, plays a video, and draws along:
Nana greets the kids, names the two or three colors needed, then builds
the picture stroke by stroke ("now pull that blue across your sky...
see? You ARE an artist") while everyone works on their own sheet. A
five-year-old's hummingbird and a thirteen-year-old's hummingbird come
out recognizably different in control but equally finished, which is the
multi-age trick. Ten to twenty minutes later every child holds a
complete, colorful piece, hands are chalky, and the picture usually gets
photographed for the fridge or the family's Instagram. Companion guides
(for the I Drew It Then I Knew It series) add a page of related facts,
book links, and narration-friendly questions. What a completed lesson
produces is the elective ideal: a tangible artifact per child per
session with zero parent prep.

## Materials and visual design

The product is video plus printables: home-video-warm production
(kitchen-table energy, not studio slickness), Nana's hands and voice
carrying the instruction. Printable PDFs (calendars, companion guides,
planners, credit trackers) are simple, cheerful, and home-printer
friendly. The site and brand lean bright and homespun, with the
ARTiST capitalization and chalk-dust aesthetic. The signal to the buyer
is "low-pressure, grandmother-warm, zero-intimidation," the exact
opposite register from atelier-serious art programs like
[Artistic Pursuits](artistic-pursuits.md).

## Pricing

As of Jul 2026, from the publisher's membership pages (linked; tiers
have shifted over the years, so older reviews cite $20/mo and $200/yr
figures that no longer match):

- Clubhouse Complete: from $40/mo (about $480/yr per family).
- Clubhouse plus Fine Arts: $50/mo. Everything (adds nature study):
  $60/mo.
- A Simple Start partial membership: entry tier for new families.
- Supplies stay under about $20 per child per year (pastels and paper).
- The membership covers unlimited kids in one family, so per-student
  cost drops fast in large families, the buyer profile the marketing
  targets.

## Why families choose it

- Art stops being the dropped subject: 10-15 minute video lessons with
  two supplies remove every excuse, which is precisely the guilt-relief
  pitch (the testimonial language on the site is explicitly "one of the
  biggest holes in our homeschool").
- Whole-family at one table: preschooler through teen do the same
  lesson, the rarest property in electives.
- Curriculum integration: art lessons matched to history, science, and
  literature let art count double in a CM or unit-study week.
- Nana: the encouragement culture converts art-anxious kids (and moms).
- Cheap supplies and no studio: fits small homes and small budgets.

## Why families leave it

- Medium fatigue: after a year or two of chalk pastels, kids who want
  drawing fundamentals, watercolor, or "real art class" progression
  move to Artistic Pursuits, Masterpiece Society, local classes, or
  YouTube tutorials.
- Skill ceiling: talented teens outgrow follow-along demonstration and
  need technique instruction and critique.
- Subscription math: $480+/yr for one elective gets cut in budget
  seasons, especially once the library's favorites feel exhausted.
- The chalk dust: a real, frequently-joked-about churn factor for
  mess-averse households.
- Co-op outsourcing: when the family joins a co-op with an art class,
  the membership becomes redundant (the co-op-first pattern for art;
  see `../../comparisons/languages-electives.md`).

## Competitive notes for Homa

- Vocabulary to know: Nana, the Clubhouse, I Drew It Then I Knew It,
  A Simple Start, chalk pastels as the medium, monthly art calendars,
  Summer Art Camp, the Fine Arts high school tier. "We do chalk
  pastels with Nana" is a complete description of a family's art
  program.
- The integration move is the lesson: this program wins by attaching
  the elective to the core stack (paint what you are studying), which
  is exactly how Homa should schedule and suggest electives, as
  attachments to existing units rather than a parallel track.
- The artifact-per-session property is worth copying: every lesson
  ends in a fridge-worthy object with the child's name on it. Homa
  materials for young kids should aim for the same finished-thing
  density; it is also ready-made portfolio evidence.
- Gap Homa fills: turning a pile of chalk drawings into records. The
  publisher already senses this (Visual Art Credit Tracker, Fine Arts
  transcript claims); a family assistant that logs each lesson done,
  ties it to the topic studied, and accumulates an art credit or
  portfolio automatically completes the loop the PDFs gesture at.
- Tone benchmark: Nana's encouragement register (never critique,
  always "you ARE an artist") is the emotional bar for how Homa should
  talk to kids about creative work.
