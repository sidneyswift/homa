# Foundations in Personal Finance (Homeschool Edition)

## Snapshot

Foundations in Personal Finance is Dave Ramsey's high school money
course packaged for homeschools: a one-semester, video-taught personal
finance elective for grades 9 to 12, teaching budgeting, saving, debt
avoidance, investing, insurance, taxes, college and career planning,
real estate, and retirement in 13 stand-alone chapters. The school
version sits in thousands of classrooms; the homeschool edition adds a
consumable student text with scripture references, optional devotionals,
a digital teacher section, and streaming video lessons taught by Ramsey
and his team of personalities. In Christian homeschool culture it is
close to a default: when a family decides the teen needs a personal
finance credit (increasingly a state graduation requirement), this is
the first product they hear named. Its worldview is the Ramsey worldview:
all debt is to be avoided, credit cards included, taught with revival
energy.

## Reputation and rankings

Aggregated reviewer sentiment calls it the gold standard for homeschool
financial literacy, with Cathy Duffy and The Homeschool Mom among the
reviewers praising the production value, the entertainment factor, and
the zero-prep open-and-go design. Publisher claims include meeting
standards in all 50 states and alignment to Jump$tart national
financial literacy standards (publisher claims, widely repeated by
reviewers). The honest critique, consistent across parent reviews and
forum threads, is dogma rigidity: the program does not teach nuanced
credit use, it teaches total credit avoidance, and families who want
their teen to understand credit scores and responsible card use as
functioning tools must supplement or push back at the dinner table. A
second critique is that the math is light; this is a behavior course,
not a consumer math course. Trajectory: stable and strong, buoyed by
the wave of state personal-finance graduation mandates and the Ramsey
media machine.

## Pedagogy and method

Video-first behavioral teaching. Each chapter is carried by
professionally produced video lessons in which Ramsey and his team of
presenters teach money principles with stories, humor, and conviction;
the student text is a fill-in-as-you-watch consumable workbook, and
assessments are auto-graded on the digital platform. The pedagogy is
persuasion toward habits (the famous baby steps, sinking funds,
emergency funds, cash flow planning) rather than computation, and the
course is explicit about its behavioral thesis: personal finance is
mostly behavior, not math. The homeschool edition supports two modes,
parent-led or fully independent study, and the 13 chapters are modular
and teachable in any order, with 45-day, 90-day, and 180-day pacing
options. The faith layer (scripture reference guide, topical
devotionals, family discussion ideas) is included but flagged optional
at the parent's discretion.

## Structure and scope

One semester, 13 stand-alone chapters, typically transcripted as a half
credit in personal finance (some families stretch it to a full credit
with the included activities and the middle school edition exists
separately for grades 6 to 8). Components: the 288-page consumable
student workbook (print edition) with a voucher code for one year of
digital access, streaming video lessons, auto-graded chapter reviews
and tests, 35-plus student activities, and a teacher section with
pacing guide, answer keys, glossary, case studies, family discussion
prompts, printable certificate of completion, and a bonus 90-minute
documentary on the student loan industry (Borrowed Future). Content
strands: saving and budgeting, debt and credit (avoidance framing),
financial planning, insurance, investing and retirement, taxes, real
estate and mortgages, college funding without loans, careers and
income. Each student needs their own workbook and platform seat;
sibling workbook-plus-streaming packs are sold for this.

## A lesson up close

A chapter runs one to two weeks at typical pacing. The student logs
into the platform (or the parent projects it at the kitchen table),
plays the chapter's video segments, and fills in the guided-notes
blanks in the workbook as the presenters teach; segments are broken
into short sections with activities between them, such as budgeting
exercises, case studies, or the documentary discussion. After the video
content, the student completes the chapter review and the auto-graded
test on the platform; the parent's dashboard shows scores, and the
teacher section supplies answer keys for the paper activities. A
family using the faith layer adds the chapter's devotional and a
discussion prompt. A completed chapter produces a filled workbook
section, an auto-graded test score, and, per the reviews, the actual
product: dinner-table money conversations the household was not having
before. A completed semester produces a certificate and a transcript
line.

## Materials and visual design

The videos are the asset: studio-produced, fast-cut, personality-driven
teaching that reads as media, not as filmed lecture, which is why teens
tolerate and often like it. The workbook is a full-color softcover
consumable with guided notes, infographics, and activity pages in
contemporary corporate-clean design. The digital platform splits
teacher and student sections and includes calculators and tools. The
aesthetic signal is professional Christian-adjacent media polish:
nothing homespun, nothing academic, closer to a conference stage than
a classroom, which is precisely the Ramsey brand promise.

## Why families choose it

A state or family graduation requirement needs filling, and this is
the turnkey answer: zero prep, auto-graded, one semester, half credit.
The values match: for debt-averse Christian families, Ramsey says at
scale what the parents already believe. Teens do not hate it, which
for an elective is half the battle. And the brand carries trust built
by two decades of radio, Financial Peace University in their church
basement, and often the parents' own debt-payoff story.

## Why families leave it

Fewer leave than never arrive: secular families and families who teach
responsible credit use often pick a different program or assemble
their own from free resources (Next Gen Personal Finance is the common
secular alternative named in forums). Completion-level complaints are
mild: the one-year access window pinches families who stall, the
anti-credit absolutism requires supplementing for credit scores and
card mechanics, and a teen who already runs their own budget finds it
repetitive. Some families use the course but quietly skip the
devotional layer, which the product design explicitly permits.

## Competitive notes for Homa

This product proves that electives with a values match, a credit line,
and zero parent prep sell at $85-90 in exactly Homa's demographic. Three
lessons. First, requirement-driven purchases: personal finance is
increasingly a graduation requirement, so Homa's credit-planning surface
(see `../../comparisons/high-school.md`) should know which states
require it and suggest when to schedule it; the regulatory context lives
in `docs/homa-planning/icp-research/06-regulatory-esa-landscape.md`.
Second, transcript mechanics: families need to know this is customarily
a half credit and how to title it; Homa should generate that line from
logged completion. Third, the optional-faith-layer pattern (scripture
present but parent-switchable) is a design Homa should recognize when
families describe their stack's worldview intensity. When a family says
"we're doing Ramsey," expect the whole-household money culture, know
the vocabulary (baby steps, emergency fund, sinking fund, debt
snowball, Borrowed Future), and never frame credit-building content as
a correction to their curriculum.
