# TouchMath

## Snapshot

TouchMath is the multisensory counting-points method: every numeral from 1
to 9 carries TouchPoints, dots placed on the numeral itself (patterns
echoing dice and dominoes), which a student touches and counts to add,
counts backward to subtract, and skip-counts to multiply and divide.
Colorado Springs sixth-grade teacher Janet Bullock developed it in 1975
with special education colleague Lyn Strand for students who could not
connect quantities to written symbols, and it grew into the special
education world's best-known math intervention, marketed today as "the
only explicit, multisensory math program that uses the numeral itself as
a manipulative." Its heartland is public school special education
(self-contained, resource, and inclusion rooms, Tier 2 and Tier 3
intervention) from preschool through transition-age students, and it now
leads with dyscalculia positioning. For homeschoolers the story has a
twist covered below: the dedicated homeschool editions are gone, and the
method now reaches homes mostly through legacy materials, a la carte
manipulatives, and an enormous informal worksheet economy.

## Reputation and rankings

- In special education, TouchMath is a household name with fifty years of
  classroom presence; a 2017 Educational Research Quarterly review of the
  program's studies concluded it "should be considered an evidence-based
  practice" for teaching computation to students with and at risk for
  disabilities, and a 2018 meta-analysis reached similar conclusions for
  addition skills. The publisher's research page catalogs supportive
  single-case and small-group studies.
- The critical view, argued publicly by math educators such as The
  Recovering Traditionalist: TouchPoints deliver accuracy without
  fluency. The method can park students permanently in the counting
  stage, inhibiting derived-fact strategies, estimation, and number
  sense, so mainstream math educators treat it as a scaffold that must
  be faded, not a math program. The company's own goal statement agrees
  in principle (students should eventually internalize the points).
- Homeschool mindshare is modest and mostly historical: Schoolhouse
  Review Crew and similar outlets reviewed the PreK-2 homeschool
  editions warmly a decade ago, and "touch point math" remains a
  recognized rescue technique in homeschool forums for kids who cannot
  retain facts.
- Trajectory: commercially rising in institutions (private-equity-backed
  growth, direct-to-district sales, dyscalculia branding), receding as a
  consumer product.

## Pedagogy and method

Explicit, systematic, procedural instruction wrapped around one
representation. The TouchPoint system assigns each numeral 1 through 9
its dots (numerals 6 through 9 use ringed double-tap points), and
computation becomes structured counting: touch and count forward for
addition, count backward from the larger number for subtraction, skip
count for multiplication and division. Instruction follows a
Model-Lead-Test (I do, we do, you do) cycle and the
concrete-representational-abstract continuum: real objects, then
TouchPoints on numerals, then bare numerals as points fade. The design
target is the student for whom memory retrieval fails: TouchPoints let a
working-memory-impaired child compute reliably without fact recall or
finger counting (the latter matters socially; fingers stigmatize, dots
on a worksheet do not). Scope covers counting and cardinality through
operations, base ten, measurement, data, and geometry, aligned to state
and extended standards. The honest boundary, per both critics and the
publisher's fade-the-points intent: it teaches computation procedure,
not conceptual number sense, and a student who never fades the points
has a ceiling.

## Structure and scope

Current product structure (as of Jul 2026) is institutional: per-grade
or grade-band Classroom Solutions in Print (teacher guides, student
activity sheets, manipulatives) or Blended form (web platform with
adaptive digital intervention, progress monitoring dashboards, plus
manipulatives), spanning PreK, K, grades 1 and 2, grades 3 to 5, and an
Upper Grades band for older students working on foundational skills.
Manipulatives sell separately: TouchPoint posters, TouchNumerals sets,
textured TouchCards, Desktop TouchLines, TouchShapes, and counters. The
discontinued homeschool line (PreK through 2nd grade downloads, six
modules of about 90 student pages per unit) is what most homeschool
reviews describe; secondhand copies still circulate. There is no
placement test culture; institutional screeners and the platform's
assessments position students instead.

## A lesson up close

Drawn from the publisher's program materials and the era of homeschool
reviews: a lesson opens with the teacher modeling the skill aloud while
touching the points ("touch and count"), the student echoing in unison,
then working alone as the teacher checks (Model-Lead-Test). The student
page is distinctive at a glance: large numerals wearing their dots,
heavy visual scaffolding, one skill per page, generous spacing, and
minimal reading load, built for students who shut down at dense
worksheets. Early sequences literally practice touching: correct point
placement and counting order on each numeral is itself a taught skill,
drilled until automatic, because every later operation rides on it. A
subtraction page cues counting backward from the bigger number; a
multiplication page cues skip counting while touching the points of one
factor. Sessions are short and daily. What a day produces is a completed
sheet with correct answers a child could not produce yesterday, which is
exactly why parents of despairing kids forgive the method its ceiling.
This describes the page anatomy only; the publisher posts sample pages
and demo videos on its product pages.

## Materials and visual design

Institutional-friendly: bright primary palette, chunky sans-serif
numerals big enough to receive fingers, laminated posters, textured
cards, and molded plastic TouchNumerals with tactile points. Student
sheets are deliberately sparse, low-text, and high-white-space, closer
to occupational therapy materials than to a commercial workbook's
decoration. The aesthetic signals accessibility and procedure, not
wonder: the visual opposite of Beast Academy, and plainer even than
Math-U-See's calm pages. The physical kit footprint in classrooms is
large; the home-scale version was a binder of printouts plus a poster.

## Why families choose it

- The child who "cannot do math" computes correctly on day one; for
  dyscalculia, ID, autism, and working-memory profiles that win rebuilds
  a family's hope.
- No fact-memorization prerequisite and no finger-counting stigma.
- The method is learnable in an afternoon and consistent across all four
  operations.
- School familiarity: kids exiting special ed classrooms often arrive
  home already trained on the points, and continuing beats switching.

## Why families leave it

- The ceiling: parents notice accuracy without understanding, points
  that will not fade, or a wall at multi-digit and mental math; the
  common destinations are Math-U-See (concrete but conceptual, see the
  [profile](math-u-see.md)), RightStart, or Ronit Bird's games-based
  number-sense work (see the [profile](ronit-bird.md)).
- There is nothing consumer-priced to buy anymore, so continuing means
  improvising materials.
- Kids without genuine retrieval deficits pick up the dots as a crutch
  and skip fact learning, which teachers and parents both flag.

## Competitive notes for Homa

- Vocabulary to know: TouchPoints, double-tap points, touch and count,
  skip counting the points, fading the points. A family saying "we do
  touch math" usually means the technique, not a purchased curriculum,
  and often means a special-needs math history worth handling gently.
- The institutional pivot left a genuine market hole: families want the
  technique in printable, home-scale, child-personalized form, and
  currently assemble it from teacher marketplaces. Homa-generated
  practice can serve the underlying need (dot-scaffolded computation
  that fades support progressively) as original design, without copying
  TouchMath's trademarked point arrangements.
- The fade-the-scaffold arc is the design lesson: pages that
  progressively remove support (full dots, partial dots, bare numerals)
  are a pattern Homa's materials studio should own for any skill, with
  the fade visible to the parent as progress evidence.
- Honest-broker duty: when a family asks about touch points, the
  balanced answer includes the fluency critique and the exit ramp
  toward number-sense work, matching the atlas's
  complements-vs-substitutes ethic.
