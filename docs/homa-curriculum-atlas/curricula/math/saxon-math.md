# Saxon Math

## Snapshot

Saxon Math is the grand old workhorse of homeschool math: a K to 12 spiral
program created by John Saxon, a former Air Force officer turned math
teacher, in 1981, and published today by Houghton Mifflin Harcourt. Concepts
arrive in tiny increments, and every day's problem set reviews everything
taught so far, which produces famously strong retention and famously bored
children. The homeschool line splits in two: a scripted, manipulative-heavy
K to 3 program, and the textbook series from Math 5/4 up through Calculus
that students largely run themselves. Reviewers estimate over a million
students have used it, and it remains a default recommendation in
traditional and classical circles. Its dual-grade level names (5/4, 6/5,
8/7) are a vocabulary all their own.

## Reputation and rankings

Cathy Duffy includes Saxon Math 5/4 through Calculus in her 103 Top Picks
and calls the series "hugely popular among homeschoolers," crediting the
independence it gives students. Community sentiment is unusually split
and unusually stable: Saxon is simultaneously the program families
describe as "thorough, solid, outstanding" and the one they describe as
dry, tedious, and the cause of daily tears; one long-running blog review
compresses it to "I hate it. But I also love it." Forum threads document
both directions: children who cried through Saxon and thrived after
switching to Teaching Textbooks, and children whose standardized scores
dropped after leaving and who came back. Trajectory: stable but aging.
It keeps its installed base and results reputation while newer
conceptual programs take the younger families.

## Pedagogy and method

Saxon's method is incremental development plus distributed practice.
A concept is introduced in a small slice, practiced lightly, then folded
into the daily mixed review for the rest of the year, with later lessons
extending it. There are no chapters in the usual sense and no topic units;
the sequence deliberately interleaves strands. The orientation is
procedural: rules, facts, and algorithms first, with conceptual explanation
kept brief (Cathy Duffy notes it "leans more toward rules" than programs
like Math-U-See or RightStart). Assessment is continuous: cumulative tests
on a fixed schedule, with an 80 percent mastery bar recommended before
moving up a level. The K to 3 program is different in kind: scripted
teacher dialogue, a daily calendar "Meeting," and manipulatives, closer to
a conventional early-elementary program than to the upper books.

## Structure and scope

The homeschool ladder is Math K, 1, 2, 3, then Math 5/4, 6/5, 7/6, 8/7,
then Algebra 1/2 (optional pre-algebra), Algebra 1, Geometry (optional),
Algebra 2, Advanced Mathematics, Calculus. The 5/4 through 8/7 texts have
120 lessons plus 12 investigations each. The two-digit names are the
famous trap: the publisher's own placement guide treats the second digit
as the default grade (Math 5/4 for fourth grade), while the community
reads them as "grade 5 average or grade 4 accelerated." A Saxon level name
alone under-determines a child's grade by a full year. Free placement
tests exist for K to 3, middle grades, and upper grades; the middle-grades
test is 100 questions scored in bands of 20. The repo's math crosswalk
maps every Saxon level to grade anchors and Homa bank items with sourced
placement semantics: see
`docs/homa-benchmarks/13-curriculum-crosswalk-math.md` section 1. A
parallel school-market line (Intermediate 3 to 5, Courses 1 to 3) exists;
homeschoolers overwhelmingly use the ladder above.

## A lesson up close

A Math 5/4 through 8/7 day has four fixed parts, identical all year.
First, Warm Up: math-fact practice, mental math prompts the parent reads
aloud, and a word problem, about 5 to 10 minutes. Second, the increment:
the student reads the day's new concept directly from the textbook, one to
two pages of explanation with worked examples; there is no teacher edition
because the text speaks to the student. Third, Lesson Practice: a handful
of problems on just the new concept. Fourth, Mixed Practice: 25 to 30
numbered problems, of which only a few touch today's topic; the rest cycle
through everything previously taught. Newer editions print a small
reference number beside each problem pointing to the lesson where that
concept was taught, which is the program's one concession to a struggling
student. A cumulative test lands roughly every five lessons (23 tests per
level). A completed day produces a full page or two of worked problems and
takes 45 to 60 minutes, longer for slow workers; splitting the lesson
across two sittings is a common family adaptation. In K to 3 the day looks
different: the parent runs a scripted lesson and calendar Meeting, and the
child completes a front-and-back worksheet.

## Materials and visual design

Softcover textbooks, dense black-and-white pages, small print, minimal
illustration; newer editions add a single accent color. Cathy Duffy's
verdict is that the books "still lack visual pizzazz," and the plainness
is part of the brand: no distractions, nothing to date itself, nothing to
entertain. The homeschool kit per level contains the student text
(reusable), a consumable tests-and-worksheets booklet, and a solutions
manual. K to 3 kits instead include a scripted teacher's manual, a meeting
book, and student workbooks, plus a manipulative kit sold separately. The
used market is deep and liquid because the textbooks do not wear out and
editions change rarely; third editions from 2004 remain the standard
homeschool stock.

## Pricing

As of Jul 2026, a Math 5/4 homeschool kit lists at $323.13 but streets at
$117.95 (Rainbow Resource), about $120 to $152 (Christianbook), and
$201.45 (Sonlight and BookShark). Upper-grade kits run $103 to $161 street
depending on level and edition. The recurring consumable is only the
tests-and-worksheets booklet, so a second child costs a fraction of the
first. Video instruction is a common add-on from third parties (DIVE,
Nicole the Math Lady, Saxon Math Teacher), roughly $50 to $75 per course
per year. Used kits commonly cut costs 40 to 60 percent. Price pages:
https://www.christianbook.com/page/homeschool/math/saxon-math and
https://www.rainbowresource.com/024422.html.

## Why families choose it

It works without the parent teaching: the text explains, the student
works, the parent checks. Retention is the selling point families repeat
to each other, along with standardized test results and a complete,
predictable path from kindergarten to calculus. Prep time is effectively
zero. It is also cheap after year one and nearly indestructible on the
used market. For parents who distrust newer conceptual approaches, Saxon
reads as the serious, proven option.

## Why families leave it

The same review volume that builds retention drives the churn. The classic
failure mode is a capable child ground down by 30-problem mixed sets that
feel like punishment for already knowing the material; forum threads
describe daily tears and two-hour lessons. Families leaving for
independence with less pain go to Teaching Textbooks. Families leaving for
conceptual understanding or hands-on work go to Math-U-See, RightStart, or
Singapore-family programs. A second churn point is the jump into the upper
books (Algebra 1/2 and beyond), where explanation gets terser and some
families switch to video-taught or more scaffolded high school options.
Kids who need one topic at a time, or who are visual learners, are the
consistent poor fits, a caveat even the friendly reviews repeat.

## Competitive notes for Homa

Saxon teaches Homa three things. First, level-name semantics: when a mom
says "she's in 6/5," Homa must not infer grade; the crosswalk carries the
translation, and the placement-test result always outranks the level name.
Second, the mixed-practice pattern (small new increment, broad daily
review, reference numbers back to the teaching lesson) is a materials
design worth studying for Homa's own practice generation, minus the
volume. Third, the gap Homa can fill for Saxon families is precisely the
pain: intelligent review trimming (which of the 30 problems this child
actually needs), conceptual "why" explanations on demand, and morale
support for long lessons. Vocabulary to know: increments, investigations,
Warm Up, Mixed Practice, the Meeting (K to 3), DIVE and Nicole the Math
Lady (video companions). Saxon families measure legitimacy in review
volume, so Homa supplements should be framed as reinforcement, never as
replacement.
