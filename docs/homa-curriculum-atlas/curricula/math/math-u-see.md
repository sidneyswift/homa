# Math-U-See

## Snapshot

Math-U-See is a mastery-based K to 12 program from Demme Learning, created
by former teacher and homeschool dad Steve Demme in the early 1990s. Its
signature is the Integer Block Kit: color-coded, snap-together blocks that
model everything from counting to long division, paired with short video
lessons taught by Demme himself. Levels carry Greek letters instead of
grades (Primer, Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta, Epsilon, Zeta, then Pre-Algebra
through Calculus), and each elementary level masters essentially one topic
for the whole year. It is the market's default answer for a child who is
struggling, math-anxious, or hands-on, and one of the few programs that
keeps manipulatives in play up through pre-algebra. Reviewers report
hundreds of thousands of families using it, and its blue blocks are
instantly recognizable at any curriculum fair.

## Reputation and rankings

Math-U-See is a fixture of homeschool award lists and is reported as a
Cathy Duffy Top Pick by multiple review aggregators; Duffy's own review
praises it as "one of the rare multi-sensory math programs" that carries
manipulatives through pre-algebra. Community sentiment splits by child.
For strugglers, it has near-rescue status: parents describe kids who
finally "get" math and enjoy it. The critical threads are equally
consistent: math educator Kate Snow warns it "may not provide enough
challenge" for average-to-strong students and calls it "especially weak in
developing kids' critical thinking," and Well-Trained Mind threads report
children bored by a year of one topic or under-prepared for the
mixed-topic reality of standardized tests. Trajectory: stable. The brand
is mature, the fan base is loyal, and its niche (anxious and tactile
learners) renews itself every year.

## Pedagogy and method

The model is mastery sequencing plus a single concrete representation.
Each level owns one core skill (Gamma is multiplication, Delta division,
Epsilon fractions, Zeta decimals), and the student does not move on until
mastery, demonstrated by the program's signature move: the child teaches
the concept back to the parent. The four-step lesson cycle is: parent
prepares (video plus instruction manual), parent presents with the blocks,
child practices to mastery, child reviews cumulatively. The blocks encode
number as length and color (units green, tens blue, hundreds red), and the
same physical system stretches from place value to polynomial models, with
fraction overlays at Epsilon and algebra-decimal inserts at Zeta. Levels
deliberately carry no grade labels; placement is by skill readiness via an
interactive online tool rather than a grade-normed test. The trade-off is
built in: one deep model, low variety, and light problem-solving demand.

## Structure and scope

Thirteen levels: Primer (a gentle, no-mastery-required kindergarten year),
Alpha through Zeta for elementary, then Pre-Algebra, Algebra 1, Geometry,
Algebra 2, PreCalculus, and Calculus. Each level has about 30 lessons,
typically one per week. Components per level: instruction manual, video
instruction (DVD or streaming digital pack), student workbook, and a tests
booklet, plus the shared manipulative kits. Critically for placement,
Math-U-See's ladder diverges from grade-labeled programs in ORDER, not
just pace: multi-digit multiplication arrives a year early (Gamma), while
decimals arrive a year late (Zeta) because Epsilon is fractions only. The
repo's math crosswalk maps each level to Homa bank items and documents why
MUS levels need item-level mapping, never grade translation: see
`docs/homa-benchmarks/13-curriculum-crosswalk-math.md` section 3. The
common community grade mapping (Primer = K through Zeta = grade 6) comes
from Kate Snow's buying guide and is approximate by design.

## A lesson up close

A week runs like this. The parent (or, in many real households, the child)
watches Demme's lesson video, typically 5 to 15 minutes of him teaching
the concept at a whiteboard with blocks. The parent then rebuilds the idea
at the table: the child builds the problem in blocks, writes it, and says
it (the program's build-write-say routine). The workbook supplies six
worksheets per lesson: the first three practice only the new concept, the
last three are systematic review mixing in prior skills, and the family
uses as many as mastery requires before the lesson test. Pages are black
and white, uncluttered, and low-count by design, roughly a dozen to twenty
problems with generous space, plus an enrichment item at the end. The
mastery check is verbal: the child explains the concept back, teaching the
parent. A normal day runs 30 to 45 minutes; a lesson stretches or
compresses freely because the schedule has no dates. What a completed week
produces: a few worksheet pages, a passed test, and a child who can narrate
the concept with blocks.

## Materials and visual design

The aesthetic is deliberately plain paper plus toy-bright plastic. Student
workbooks are softcover, black and white, with large type and simple
layouts that read as calm rather than austere; all the color lives in the
manipulatives. The Integer Block Kit (133 pieces in a storage box) is the
durable center of the system, built to survive a decade of siblings;
fraction overlays are transparent color film in a folder. Videos are
unpretentious: Demme, a whiteboard, blocks, and dad jokes. The whole
package signals "we will not overwhelm your child," the visual opposite of
Horizons' dense color or Beast Academy's comic energy.

## Pricing

As of Jul 2026: Universal Sets (instruction manual, video access, student
workbook, tests, needed manipulatives) run about $130 to $190 per level
depending on level. Level Up sets for continuing families (no
manipulatives) run about $65 to $122. The Integer Block Kit alone is about
$80 and serves Primer through Pre-Algebra; Epsilon adds roughly $50 of
fraction overlays and Zeta about $25 of algebra-decimal inserts. A
sibling reusing manuals and blocks needs only a new workbook and tests,
roughly $40 to $50. First-year cost lands near $215; subsequent years and
subsequent kids cost much less, and blocks resell well. Current set
prices are on the per-level pages at https://mathusee.com.

## Why families choose it

A struggling or anxious child starts succeeding, which buys enormous
loyalty. The video does the teaching, the blocks make abstractions
touchable, mastery pacing removes the treadmill feeling, and the
skill-based levels remove grade-level shame (a fourth grader in Beta is
just "in Beta"). Multi-kid economics are excellent, and the method stays
consistent from kindergarten to calculus.

## Why families leave it

Three exits recur. First, the challenge ceiling: strong students coast,
parents notice missing problem-solving depth, and they move to Beast
Academy, Singapore-family programs, or Saxon for more demand. Second,
strand anxiety: because each level is one topic deep, children can be
mid-Gamma with multiplication mastery but light on measurement, time, and
data compared to grade-labeled peers, which surfaces at standardized
testing or a return to school; the publisher's own sequencing (no decimals
until Zeta) is a feature families misread as a gap. Third, the upper-level
transition: reviews describe the Pre-Algebra to Algebra 1 stretch as
thinner than the elementary years, and some families switch to
video-taught or more rigorous high school options. Block fatigue is the
minor fourth: kids who crave variety tire of one manipulative.

## Competitive notes for Homa

When a family says "we use Math-U-See," Homa must think in Greek letters
and skills, never grades: "finished Epsilon" seeds fraction mastery and
explicitly does NOT seed decimals (that is Zeta's whole job), per the
crosswalk. Reassurance copy writes itself here, because the sequence
difference is by design and parents worry it is a gap. Materials lessons
worth stealing: the build-write-say loop, the teach-it-back mastery check
(a beautiful low-tech assessment Homa can prompt parents to run), and the
six-worksheet ramp from focused practice to systematic review. The gap
Homa fills: cross-strand supplements (measurement, data, word problems)
that respect MUS vocabulary, and translation for testing season.
Vocabulary to know: Universal Set, Level Up set, Integer Blocks, Decimal
Street, build-write-say, the level names themselves.
