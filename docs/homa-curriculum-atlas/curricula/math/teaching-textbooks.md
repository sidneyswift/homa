# Teaching Textbooks

## Snapshot

Teaching Textbooks is the homeschool market's independence machine: a
fully self-teaching math curriculum for grades 3 through Pre-Calculus,
built by brothers Greg and Shawn Sabouri in 2004 specifically to take the
parent out of daily math. Every lesson is delivered by the app (version
4.0 as of 2026): a friendly narrated lecture, auto-graded problems with
hints and second chances, a solution video for every single problem, and a
gradebook that runs itself. It began as CD-ROMs plus spiral-bound
textbooks and is now subscription software. Its mindshare is enormous,
especially among large families and math-averse parents, and its
reputation comes as a pair: beloved for ending math battles, and dogged by
a persistent community claim that it runs about a grade level behind.

## Reputation and rankings

Cathy Duffy's review is warm, calling the courses great for independent
study and noting they require no work from parents; she classifies the
series as college-prep "even though it is not as rigorous as some other
courses." Community sentiment is dominated by two threads. The
gratitude thread: parents report reclaiming hours per week and children
who stopped crying over math. The rigor thread: forum posts and comparison
sites repeat that levels run behind ("TT5 would really be 4th grade" is a
representative forum formulation), a claim the publisher never makes but
families act on by placing children one level up. Duffy herself notes many
students, especially advanced ones, need one level higher than their grade.
Trajectory: stable-to-strong; the subscription pivot and the $216.95
large-family cap keep it structurally attractive, and its placement tests
and 15-lesson free trial lower switching friction to near zero.

## Pedagogy and method

The design goal is completion without a teacher. Instruction is spiral:
concepts arrive gently, recur throughout the year, and daily problem sets
mix new material with review. The tone is deliberately low-pressure,
concrete before abstract, with animations and a patient narrator voice;
every problem is read aloud on demand, which makes it a quiet favorite for
dyslexic students. Feedback is immediate: wrong answers trigger hints,
most problems allow a second attempt, and a worked solution video exists
for every problem, so the feedback loop never requires a parent. The
orientation is procedural competence and confidence rather than depth;
there are few multi-step or novel problems, which is precisely the
trade-off critics name. Assessment is continuous auto-grading plus
quizzes, all recorded in the parent gradebook, where parents can tighten
settings (disable hints or second chances) if they want more
accountability.

## Structure and scope

Ten levels: Math 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, Pre-Algebra, Algebra 1, Geometry, Algebra
2, Pre-Calculus; there is nothing below grade 3 and no Calculus. Each
level is a school year of lessons (roughly 110 to 130 plus quizzes,
varying by level) delivered in the app, with offline capability limited to
six downloaded lessons at a time. Placement tests are free printable PDFs
with a uniform structure across levels: 30 problems in two 15-problem
sections with a published pass rule, and the publisher tells unsure
families to try the higher level first. The repo's math crosswalk records
the placement mechanics, the runs-behind evidence trail, and the practical
translation (seed a TT level about one bank item lower than its name
suggests, and trust the placement test over the level name): see
`docs/homa-benchmarks/13-curriculum-crosswalk-math.md` section 6. Version
4.0 is app-only; legacy physical books and CD sets circulate on the used
market but are no longer the product.

## A lesson up close

The child logs in and the app resumes where they left off. A lesson opens
with a narrated, animated lecture of about 5 to 15 minutes; the child can
pause and rewatch freely. Then 20 to 25 problems, answered directly in the
app with a built-in scratchpad; each is graded instantly. A miss offers a
hint and usually a second try; a second miss offers the solution video,
which walks the full worked solution step by step. Every fifth-or-so
lesson is a quiz. The parent's entire involvement can be a glance at the
gradebook, which records scores, time on task, and which problems were
missed, and can flag grade drift. Sessions run about 30 to 60 minutes,
shorter in the elementary levels. There is no physical page anatomy to
describe anymore: the "worksheet" is a problem screen with the character
mascots, large type, and one problem at a time, which is exactly why
screen-averse families pass. What a completed day produces: a logged,
graded lesson and, in most households, no parental interaction at all.

## Materials and visual design

Fully digital, cartoon-styled, and intentionally unintimidating: rounded
characters, celebratory feedback, a buddy-not-teacher voice. The visual
language reads younger than its grade levels, which soothes anxious
students and mildly annoys some teens. The app runs on computers, tablets,
and phones; the digital gradebook produces professional-looking records
for portfolio and umbrella-school reporting. Nothing ships in a box, there
is nothing to resell, and nothing to store, which large families count as
a feature and curriculum-fair traditionalists count as a loss.

## Pricing

As of Jul 2026, per the publisher's FAQ: Math 3 to 5 cost $48.95 per year
each; Math 6 and 7 cost $63.95; Pre-Algebra, Algebra 1, and Geometry cost
$79.95; Algebra 2 and Pre-Calculus cost $83.95. The Large Family Discount
caps 4 to 8 children at $216.95 per year across any levels. Subscriptions
run 12 months from first lesson, and a finished level can roll into the
next within the window. The first 15 lessons of any level are a free
trial with no payment information collected. Free tutoring help is
included. Price page: https://www.teachingtextbooks.com/faqs.

## Why families choose it

Peace. It ends the daily math fight, frees the parent (decisive in
four-plus-kid households), grades everything, and keeps records. The
audio support serves dyslexic and reluctant readers. The price is among
the lowest of the name-brand programs, and the family cap makes it the
cheapest serious option per child for large families. Children who have
failed elsewhere often rebuild confidence here, which parents describe in
before-and-after terms.

## Why families leave it

Rigor doubt is the big one: the runs-behind reputation makes
college-ambitious families nervous around middle school, and the standard
exits are Saxon (for volume and rigor), Singapore-family or Math Mammoth
(for depth on paper), or AoPS (for strong students who were coasting).
Some families run TT one level up as a fix and stay. Screen fatigue is
the second exit, especially for families limiting device time or with
younger children who fidget through videos. Third, the format ceiling:
no Calculus, so STEM-track students transition out by senior year
regardless. Finally, students who need hands-on representation go to
Math-U-See; TT has no physical component at all.

## Competitive notes for Homa

Interpretation rules: a reported TT level seeds about one item lower on
the Homa bank than the name implies, per the crosswalk, and a TT
placement-test result outranks the level name. Never echo "behind"
framing to the parent; the honest reassurance is that TT sequences
differently and the placement test is the signal. Product lessons:
TT's second-chance-plus-solution-video loop is the best-in-class model of
never letting a child end a problem in failure, and its parent gradebook
is the bar for effortless progress visibility; Homa's progress surfaces
compete with this directly. The gap Homa fills: paper practice and
hands-on extension for a screen-only program, multi-step and word-problem
depth for families worried about rigor, and bridge planning into Calculus
or outside programs after Pre-Calculus. Vocabulary: levels are "Math 6"
style names, "4.0" is the platform generation, "the app," "second
chances," the free trial, the Large Family Discount.
