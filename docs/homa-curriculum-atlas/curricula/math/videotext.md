# VideoText Interactive

## Snapshot

VideoText Interactive sells exactly two products, and each claims to be
several courses at once: Algebra: A Complete Course (176 video lessons
covering pre-algebra, Algebra 1, and Algebra 2 as one integrated program)
and Geometry: A Complete Course (geometry plus trigonometry, with formal
proof as a cornerstone). Author Tom Clark, a veteran math teacher, built
the program around short 5-to-10-minute video lessons that develop every
concept analytically, with the house slogan that there are no shortcuts,
tricks, rules, or formulas, only understanding. It is a fixture of the
classical and conceptual-math corners of the homeschool world, now owned
and distributed by Memoria Press (profiled at
`../all-in-one/memoria-press.md`). Its mindshare is modest but durable:
the program dates to the VHS era and its fans treat the dated production
as a badge of substance.

## Reputation and rankings

Cathy Duffy reviews it as an unusual, logically sequenced course that
combines pre-algebra through Algebra 2, teaching equations and
inequalities together and organizing concepts by degree rather than by
the conventional chapter order. A 2026 homeschoolpicks review rates it
4.6/5 as one of the strongest visual-learner math options and a budget
standout, with the honest caveats the community repeats everywhere: the
videos look decades old, there is no live teacher or built-in human
support, practice volume is light compared to Saxon, and the line stops
after geometry, forcing a provider switch for precalculus. Classical
forums recommend it steadily. Trajectory: stable to fading; the Memoria
Press acquisition gives it distribution, but the format ages and the
self-grading online competition (Shormann, Teaching Textbooks, CTC Math)
keeps growing.

## Pedagogy and method

Mastery and conceptual, in deliberate opposition to both spiral review
and procedural shortcut-teaching. Each brief video develops one concept
through inquiry: the narrator poses questions, and the intended use is
aggressive pause-button dialogue, with the publisher suggesting a pause
every 15 to 20 seconds so the student answers before the video does. The
publisher explicitly discourages note-taking during the video so
attention stays on the concept; printed Course Notes reproduce every
screen afterward. Practice is intentionally lean (the WorkText restates
objectives and walks more examples before exercises), and the assessment
philosophy is distinctive: check work against step-by-step solutions and
perform error analysis, grade initial work on completion rather than
accuracy, test only the next day to dodge short-term memory, and use the
second version of every quiz and test for retakes until mastery. The
intellectual position, argued in the publisher's scope and sequence
rationale, is that algebra is one subject artificially split into two
school years, and geometry is one subject artificially chopped into
chapters.

## Structure and scope

Two programs. Algebra: A Complete Course is 176 lessons across 10 units
sold as six modules (A through F), with 360 pages of Course Notes, a
590-page WorkText, solutions manuals for every problem, and two versions
of every progress test; completing it lets the family claim three
transcript credits (Pre-Algebra, Algebra 1, Algebra 2). Cathy Duffy pegs
the whole program at roughly two to two and a half years of work, and
notes its pre-algebra strand covers number foundations but not the
geometry and measurement strands most modern pre-algebra courses include.
Geometry: A Complete Course is five modules (A through E) across six
units, from the structure of geometry through triangles, polygons, and
circles, plus an online-only trigonometry unit; it claims one Geometry
credit, and the publisher documents how Algebra plus Geometry together
support claiming Algebra 1, Algebra 2, Geometry, and (per its FAQ
materials) trigonometry coverage toward a precalculus foundation. Both
programs publish scope and sequence rationale PDFs and program
schematics on their course pages. Placement counseling and a free trial
login (guest access at videotextonline.com) stand in for a formal
placement test.

## A lesson up close

A day is one lesson, and a lesson is a conversation with a pause button.
The student (ideally with the parent for the dialogue, though many teens
run it alone) watches a 5-to-10-minute video that develops a single
concept with computer graphics and animation, pausing at each posed
question to answer before the narrator does; a paused-and-discussed
lesson takes 10 to 15 minutes. No notes are taken during the video.
Afterward, the student reviews the Course Notes page that replicates the
lesson's screens, then opens the WorkText: restated objectives, key
terms, several fully worked additional examples, then an exercise bank.
The student checks their own work against the step-by-step solutions
manual and is expected to explain any error found (the program's error
analysis ritual). Quizzes and unit tests come in two forms each, often
with open-response questions asking the student to state the concept in
writing, and the publisher coaches testing a day after the work, never
the same day. A completed day produces a worked exercise page, a
self-checked error analysis, and, at unit boundaries, a test the parent
grades with the instructor's guide.

## Materials and visual design

The videos are the brand's paradox: computer-generated graphics,
animation, and color-sequencing that were state of the art when produced
and now read as distinctly retro (reviewers say 1990s and they are not
wrong). Production values aside, the visual argument is real: concepts
are shown in motion, with color used to track terms through
transformations. Print components are workmanlike softcovers: Course
Notes that mirror video screens, a plain WorkText, solutions manuals,
and test booklets; the online edition replaces all of it with PDFs and
streaming behind a login. Nothing about the aesthetic is childlike, which
suits its teen audience; the signal is serious, plain, a little
old-fashioned, the video equivalent of a chalkboard proof.

## Pricing

As of Jul 2026, from the official shop pages (checkout runs through
Memoria Press): Algebra online $299, covering two students in the
household with a three-year license each (activations need not be
consecutive), additional students $49; half-course bundles (modules ABC
or DEF) $175 each. Online plus printed books $399; online plus books and
DVDs $450 (three-module bundles $260). Geometry pricing mirrors Algebra:
$299 online, $399 with books, $450 with books and DVDs. There is a
60-day money-back guarantee on online programs, and expired instructor
accounts can be reactivated briefly for SAT and CLEP review. The
per-student economics are the quiet selling point: $299 buys two to
three high school credits for two students, which beats nearly every
video competitor. Price pages: https://videotext.com/shop-videotext/ and
https://videotext.com/shop/.

## Why families choose it

Visual and conceptual learners who need to see why algebra works, after
procedural programs produced tears or memorized fog. The one-time price
covering multiple years and two students. The integrated multi-credit
structure, which compresses the sequence and appeals to families who
find the standard algebra-geometry-algebra sandwich incoherent. The
mastery testing culture (retakes, error analysis, next-day tests) suits
families who care more about understanding than grades. And classical
homeschoolers meet it through Memoria Press distribution.

## Why families leave it

The dated production is the first filter; some teens refuse it. Kids who
need heavy repetition find the practice volume thin and drift to Saxon
or supplement with worksheets. There is no human in the loop: families
needing accountability or live help move to Mr. D Math
(`../online-digital/mr-d-math.md`) or Derek Owens (`derek-owens.md`).
The line ending at geometry forces every family to choose a new provider
for precalculus anyway, so some switch earlier to a program that goes
the distance (Shormann at `shormann-math.md`, Thinkwell at
`../online-digital/thinkwell.md`). And the dialogic method quietly
depends on a parent willing to sit and pause; used as a pure solo video
course by an unmotivated teen, it degrades into watching math television.

## Competitive notes for Homa

VideoText is the market's proof that multi-credit integrated courses
create transcript anxiety that a product can solve: its own site
dedicates pages to explaining what credits a family may claim, because
"we finished VideoText Algebra" maps to three transcript lines. Homa
should encode those claims (Algebra completion = Pre-Algebra, Algebra 1,
Algebra 2; Geometry completion = Geometry plus trig exposure) so a
family's logged work translates to correct credit lines automatically,
the exact pattern the high school comparison doc
(`../../comparisons/high-school.md`) develops. The pedagogy also carries
a materials lesson: pause-prompts that force the learner to answer
before the explanation continues are a cheap, powerful interaction Homa
can borrow in generated materials. When a family says they use
VideoText, expect a conceptual-leaning, budget-aware household, know the
module letters (A through F) rather than grade levels, and expect a
provider decision looming at precalculus, which is a natural moment for
Homa to help compare next steps. Vocabulary: modules, Course Notes,
WorkText, error analysis, two-version tests, claimed credits.
