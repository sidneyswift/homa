# CTCMath

## Snapshot

CTCMath is an Australian online K to 12 math program built around one
teacher: Pat Murray, whose short recorded lessons (typically 4 to 10
minutes) anchor more than 1,400 tutorials from kindergarten through
senior high school topics. One subscription unlocks every grade level,
and the family plan covers all children in the house, making it one of
the cheapest per-child complete programs on the market. Many families
run it as the full elementary and middle school curriculum; others use
it for catch-up or review. Its pitch is friction removal: tiny lessons,
automatic grading, per-strand diagnostics, and a patient teaching voice.

## Reputation and rankings

CTCMath advertises widely in homeschool media (including on Cathy
Duffy's site) and collects consistently warm blog reviews: the recurring
praise is short lessons, independence, and price; the recurring
criticisms are dryness, limited word-problem depth, and a lighter high
school offering many families treat as supplemental. Trajectory: stable,
holding the value end of the online category between Teaching Textbooks
(more handholding, higher per-child price) and free-but-unstructured
Khan Academy.

## Pedagogy and method

Direct instruction in minimal doses: a short video teaching one concept
plainly, then adaptive interactive questions, with optional printable
worksheets and worked solutions. Organization is topical (strand and
topic trees per grade) rather than spiral; an optional weekly revision
feature adds mixed review. Students self-run with parent-configurable
passing standards, and everything is auto-marked into detailed reports.
There is no narrative, no characters, and no game layer, which suits
easily distracted kids and bores others.

## Structure and scope

Grades K through 12 in one platform: elementary and middle grades are
complete strand-organized curricula (number, algebra, measurement,
geometry, statistics), and high school runs from Basic Math and
Pre-Algebra through Algebra, Geometry, Trigonometry, Pre-Calculus, and
Calculus streams. Topics end with diagnostic tests, reports track pass
rates and time per child, and parents assign lessons or let students
roam levels freely. There is no placement gate; the per-strand
diagnostics serve that role.

## A lesson up close

The child opens the assigned topic and watches a 4 to 10 minute video
of Murray working examples with clear spoken explanation and on-screen
working. A bank of interactive questions follows, marked instantly; a
one-page printable summary and optional worksheets add paper practice.
Results post automatically to the parent report with scores and
attempts. A daily session is commonly two or three lessons, 20 to 40
minutes, and produces logged scores with no parent grading.

## Materials and visual design

All digital and deliberately plain: whiteboard-style videos, simple
question screens, unadorned parent-first reports, functional
black-and-white printables. The absence of gamification is a design
position; the product sells calm efficiency.

## Pricing

As of Jul 2026, the publisher's FAQ lists $39.90 monthly or $257 yearly
for one student and $59.90 monthly or $397 yearly for a family. In
practice CTCMath has run a homeschool half-price offer continuously for
years, landing families at roughly $148.50 to $198.50 per year for all
grades and children (blogs cite both figures across seasons; the
checkout page governs). A no-card free trial exists. Price pages:
https://ctcmath.com/faq and https://ctcmath.com/purchase/homeschool50.

## Why families choose it

Per-child price is unbeatable for large families, and all-grades access
lets one subscription serve a 1st grader and a 9th grader while letting
anyone drop back a level for review without shame or cost. Short
lessons fit attention-challenged kids, auto-grading satisfies
record-keeping states, and the teacher never gets annoyed.

## Why families leave it

Depth and dryness: families wanting rigorous problem solving move to
Math Mammoth, Dimensions Math, or AoPS-family programs, and STEM-track
high schoolers commonly switch by Algebra 2. Kids needing narrative or
hands-on work drift to Teaching Textbooks or Math-U-See. Screen-wary
families use it only as a supplement.

## Competitive notes for Homa

The 4-to-10-minute lesson atom is this category's strongest proof that
instruction length, not total workload, drives completion for
distractible kids; Homa's lesson briefs should respect that atom size.
Per-strand diagnostics with parent-set pass marks are a clean seed-data
model: when a family says "we use CTC," Homa can ask for strand results
rather than a grade label. The gaps Homa fills are the ones reviewers
name: word problems, applied projects, paper practice to balance screen
time, and bridge planning when high schoolers outgrow it.
