# Beast Academy

## Snapshot

Beast Academy is the elementary arm of Art of Problem Solving (AoPS), the
company that has dominated advanced and competition math education since
Richard Rusczyk founded its online community in 2003. Launched in 2012
with lead author Jason Batterson, it teaches math for ages 6 to 13 through
full-color comic Guide books starring monster students at a math school,
paired with black-and-white Practice books and a parallel online platform,
Beast Academy Online. It covers levels 1 through 5, four units per level,
going deeper than grade-level standards and leaning hard on puzzles and
problems designed to make kids struggle productively. The publisher itself
recommends starting "about a level 'behind'" the child's grade. It is the
consensus pick for mathy kids, the secular-homeschool darling, and the
on-ramp to the AoPS Prealgebra-through-Calculus pipeline.

## Reputation and rankings

Beast Academy's reputation is elite and specific: reviewers call it the
antidote to drill-and-kill, one parent-review site describing it as "the
gold standard" for moving beyond standard worksheets, and
gifted-education communities treat it as the default elementary
recommendation. Its Guides earn a rare compliment: kids read them
voluntarily, in bed, like comics. The critical threads are honest about
the cost: steep difficulty curves, initial tears from perfectionists,
and a poor fit for children still building basic fluency; Reddit parent
reviews warn strugglers cannot keep up even in early levels, and the
publisher leans in with productive-struggle guidance rather than
softening. Trajectory: rising. Beast Academy Online broadened it from a
books-for-gifted-kids product into a mainstream subscription, and level
1 (completed 2022) extended it downward.

## Pedagogy and method

The lineage is AoPS competition-math culture translated for eight year
olds: the belief that math is learned by wrestling with problems slightly
too hard for you, not by repeating problems you can already do. Guides
teach through story: the monster students get things wrong, argue, and
discover methods, modeling productive struggle. Practice books ramp from
straightforward exercises to starred and double-starred problems that are
expected to be missed on the first try; hints live in the back of the
book, framed as guiding questions rather than answers. Content is "loosely
based on the Common Core standards" but covers "grade-level standards in
greater depth than other elementary math curricula" (publisher's FAQ),
with levels 4 and 5 reaching into middle-grades material. Organization is
topical and mastery-oriented (chapters own their topics), with puzzle
variety doing the engagement work that spiral review does elsewhere.
Parent role is coach and morale officer, not lecturer.

## Structure and scope

Five levels, each in four units (A to D), each unit containing three
chapters: 12 chapters per level, and the publisher's pacing of 12 to 15
days per chapter fills a 180-day school year per level. Per unit there
is a full-color Guide (about 96 to 112 pages) and a companion Practice
book (about 128 to 176 pages); Beast Academy Online covers the same
chapters in the same order with entirely different problems, so books
and online work as complements or alternatives. Placement tests exist
per level, and the start-a-level-behind rule means level names
understate the typical user's grade by about a year: a Beast 3 student
is typically a grade 4 child working grade 3 topics at depth. Completing
level 5 qualifies a student for AoPS Prealgebra, the publisher's grade 6
course. Chapter topic lists per unit are public on the store pages (for
example, 3A is shape classification, skip-counting, perimeter and area),
and supplementary Puzzles books (one per level, 400 to 500 puzzles)
extend practice without new content. The repo's math crosswalk maps
levels to Homa bank items and encodes the level-behind semantics: see
`docs/homa-benchmarks/13-curriculum-crosswalk-math.md` section 7.

## A lesson up close

A book-based day starts with the Guide: the child reads a comic section
where Grogg, Winnie, Alex, and Lizzie work through the new idea, usually
with a wrong turn on the way. Then the matching Practice section: a
boxed recap of key examples at the top of the first page, then problems
that begin at confidence level and climb fast; a page might hold six to
twelve problems, generously spaced, with puzzle formats (grids, paths,
cross-number puzzles) replacing repetitive drill. Starred problems
signal challenge, double stars signal "expected to be missed"; the child
is taught to try, fail, re-read, and only then take a hint from the
guiding questions in the back. A day runs 30 to 60 minutes but produces
fewer completed problems than a traditional program, by design. Online,
the same chapter becomes short animated lessons, adaptive problem sets,
and games, with a parent dashboard; the Guides are readable digitally
inside the subscription. A completed day produces a handful of genuinely
earned solutions, and families of perfectionists report sitting close
for the first weeks to reframe "I can't" into "not yet."

## Materials and visual design

The Guides are real graphic novels: full-color, professionally drawn
(Erich Owen's art), with continuing characters and jokes, printed at comic
trim size. Practice books are matte black and white, clean and spacious,
with the beast characters appearing at section heads; the restraint keeps
the puzzle content readable. The Puzzles books mirror the practice
aesthetic. Beast Academy Online carries the same art into animation. The
whole identity signals "math is a game worth playing," and it is the
category's strongest proof that rigorous and delightful can coexist: the
atlas README uses "Beast Academy's comic energy" as a design reference
point.

## Pricing

As of Jul 2026: printed Guide plus Practice sets run about $27 to $30 per
unit, so a full level of books is about $100 to $120. Beast Academy
Online costs $15.99 per month or $99.99 per year for the first student,
with siblings at $10.99 per month or $64.99 per year (up to six). The
bundle (one year online plus a full level of books) is $160 and is the
publisher's headline offer; bundles and yearly plans are purchasable
through ClassWallet for ESA families. Puzzles books are about $10 to $13.
Practice books are consumable; Guides and Puzzles resell. Price pages:
https://beastacademy.com/online/enroll and
https://help.beastacademy.com/a/1792549-ways-to-save-on-subscriptions-and-books.

## Why families choose it

Their kid is bored, mathy, or both, and Beast Academy treats that child as
a problem solver rather than a worksheet machine. The comics create pull
(kids ask for math), the depth creates trust, and the AoPS pipeline gives
ambitious families a visible path from level 1 through olympiad-track high
school math. Secular families also choose it as the strongest fully
secular elementary program.

## Why families leave it

The difficulty is the exit as well as the entrance. Children still
consolidating basic fluency drown in it, and perfectionists can burn out
on double-starred problems. The most common adaptation short of leaving is
pairing it with an easier fluency program (community threads describe
alternating "hard" Beast with "easy" Math Mammoth for arithmetic
practice). Families who leave outright typically go to Singapore-family
programs (still conceptual, gentler ramp) or Math Mammoth; families who
needed independence more than depth drift to online options. The other
structural exit is completion: after level 5 the path is AoPS Prealgebra,
which is a textbook-and-online world with a distinctly older-student feel,
and some families detour into a conventional pre-algebra year instead.

## Competitive notes for Homa

Placement semantics first: "she's in Beast 3" typically means a grade 4
student, and finishing a level means the named grade's skills at unusual
depth; the crosswalk encodes this, and Homa should never read Beast level
names as grade names. Beast families' recurring need is exactly what the
community already improvises: parallel fluency practice that respects the
child's identity as a strong math kid, which is a materials-generation
opportunity with careful framing (practice as training, not remediation).
Design lessons worth studying: hint architecture (guiding questions before
answers), star-rated difficulty as an honest contract with the child, and
narrative characters who model failure. When a Beast parent reports tears,
the crosswalk-aware reassurance is that the program is calibrated for
productive struggle, and flat progress during a hard chapter is its shape,
not a stall. Vocabulary: Guides, Practice books, starred and double-starred
problems, BA Online, AoPS Prealgebra, MATHCOUNTS (the ambition horizon for
many of these families).
