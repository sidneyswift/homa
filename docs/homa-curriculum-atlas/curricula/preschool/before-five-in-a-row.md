# Before Five in a Row

## Snapshot

Before Five in a Row (BFIAR) is the preschool entry of the Five in a
Row family, the picture-book unit study franchise profiled at
[../all-in-one/five-in-a-row.md](../all-in-one/five-in-a-row.md). One
softcover or digital guide by Jane Claire Lambert builds gentle
mini-units around 24 classic picture books (Goodnight Moon and The ABC
Bunny among them) for ages 2 to 4, followed by More Before
Five in a Row (MBFIAR), a newer companion using 14 slightly harder
books for ages 3 to 5. Where the flagship volumes prescribe reading one
book five days in a row with a rotating subject lens, BFIAR is looser
by design: an array of story-connected activities to sprinkle through
ordinary days, plus a 40-page "Parent's Treasury of Creative Ideas for
Learning Readiness" covering play, motor skills, songs, and teachable
moments at bath time and the grocery store. It has been the sentimental
first curriculum of literature-loving families for three decades and
remains a fixture of gentle-preschool recommendation threads.

## Reputation and rankings

BFIAR inherits the Five in a Row halo: the series is a Cathy Duffy Top
Pick, and the Before volumes are the standard answer to "something
gentle for my 3-year-old" in both Christian and secular communities.
Reviewers consistently praise the book selections, the near-zero prep,
and the cuddle-on-the-couch texture; the standing caveats are that the
activity instructions can be vague ("it's often up to you to determine
exactly what to do," per Cathy Duffy), that Goodnight Moon-tier titles
skew young for a verbal 3.5-year-old while other titles run long for a
2-year-old, and that four of the 24 books are out of print (The Red
Carpet, The Little Rabbit, If Jesus Came to My House, Jenny's Surprise
Summer), which turns book-gathering into a library-and-thrift hunt.
The 2020 second editions refreshed the design, and MBFIAR's release
extended the preschool runway. Trajectory: stable classic, carried by
word of mouth and multi-generational nostalgia rather than marketing.

## Pedagogy and method

Relationship-first literature immersion. The theory, consistent with
the whole FIAR line, is that a great picture book rewards repeated
visits and can carry all early learning: vocabulary and narration from
the story, art from the illustrations, counting and classifying from
the pictures, science and social studies from the setting. Unlike the
elementary volumes there is no five-day rotation or weekly grid; the
publisher explicitly frames BFIAR as readiness and relationship, not
school. Activities are menus, discussion is the medium, and nothing is
assessed. The second part of the guide (the Parent's Treasury) is
closer to a parenting handbook than a curriculum: ideas for building
readiness through daily life, motor play, music, and art. MBFIAR steps
slightly toward structure with longer, more detailed lessons (six or
more pages per book versus about four) and a simple Bible lesson
opening each unit.

## Structure and scope

- Before Five in a Row: 24 picture-book units for ages 2 to 4, each
  with story-connected activities across Bible, science, math,
  language, art, motor skills, and relationships, plus the Parent's
  Treasury section (about four guide pages per book).
- More Before Five in a Row: 14 picture-book units for ages 3 to 5
  with more detailed activities, expanded Bible content, story disks,
  a storybook map, and an animal classification game.
- Between the two guides, 38 week-sized lessons: a little more than a
  year at one per week, or a relaxed two-year span, which is how the
  publisher positions them (use two or three times weekly, or at
  bedtime).
- Each guide includes story disks and a fanciful storybook-map spread
  at the back (color laminated disks ship with publisher-direct
  copies): after finishing a book, the child places its disk on the
  map, the junior version of the flagship program's world-map ritual.
- No phonics, no math sequence, no handwriting: families wanting
  skills add them separately, and the publisher's own next step is
  Five in a Row Volume 1 around age 4 or 5.

## A lesson up close

There is no prescribed day. A typical use, echoed across reviews: the
parent skims the unit's four-ish pages once, puts the book on hold at
the library, and then over a week reads the book aloud several times,
picking one or two activities per sitting. For Goodnight Moon (the
publisher's free sample lesson) that might be noticing the painting
style, hunting for the mouse in each spread, talking about the moon,
a shape game, or a related Bible verse. Activities need almost nothing
beyond the book and household items. The output is conversation,
play, and the story disk on the map; there is nothing to file, grade,
or check off. Parents who want a plan impose one (bloggers share DIY
planning pages); parents who resist structure cite exactly this
looseness as why it worked.

## Materials and visual design

A single perfect-bound softcover (8.5 by 11) or PDF per guide,
text-forward with modest black-and-white layout, warmly written to the
parent; the 2020 second editions modernized covers and typography. The
real materials are the picture books themselves, mid-century American
illustration at its most beloved, and that is the aesthetic the buyer
is purchasing: the curriculum object is a manual, but the product on
the shelf is a basket of classic books. Story disks and the storybook
map add the one bit of printed color and ceremony.

## Pricing

As of Jul 2026: $49 per guide, print or digital, from fiveinarow.com
(also $49 at Amazon, Christianbook, and Rainbow Resource). The books
are extra: most families library-source them or buy used; Rainbow
Resource sells a BFIAR guide-plus-literature package around $198-265
depending on promotions, minus the out-of-print titles. Guides are
non-consumable, famously resellable, and circulate constantly in
buy-sell-trade groups, so real cost for a patient buyer is low. A
realistic all-in year (guide plus partial book purchases) lands well
under $150.

## Why families choose it

- The gentlest structured thing that exists: reading aloud they were
  already doing, with a light plan attached.
- Multi-decade trust and nostalgia; many buyers were rowed through it
  themselves as kids.
- Library-powered and reusable: one $49 guide covers every sibling.
- A ramp, not a program: it leads naturally into Five in a Row proper
  as the child hits 4 or 5, a built-in multi-year path.

## Why families leave it

- Book-hunting friction, sharpened by the out-of-print titles.
- Vague activity instructions frustrate parents who want told-what-to-
  say plans; those families move to The Peaceful Preschool or Playing
  Preschool, which give day-by-day structure at the same price point.
- No skills spine: families anxious about letters and counting add
  ABCJesusLovesMe, TGTB Preschool, or letter-of-the-week printables,
  and the supplement sometimes becomes the program.
- Kids on the young end wiggle out of the longer books; kids on the
  old end outgrow the titles before the parent finishes the guide.

## Competitive notes for Homa

- Vocabulary to know: rowing, BFIAR and MBFIAR (families use the
  acronyms), story disks, the Treasury. "We're rowing Blueberries for
  Sal" from a family with a 3-year-old means BFIAR culture: gentle,
  book-first, assessment-averse.
- The story disk map is ambient progress visualization for toddlers,
  the same lesson the flagship profile draws: evidence of learning
  that feels like a keepsake. Homa's earliest progress surfaces should
  look like this, not like a checklist.
- Gap Homa fills: everything around the book. Theme-matched activity
  ideas when the guide runs thin, printable story props, a reading log
  that becomes a keepsake, and substitution suggestions when a title
  is out of print at the library. That last one is a real, recurring,
  unserved pain for this exact program.
- BFIAR families are pre-loaded for Homa's read-aloud-centric habits:
  they already organize the week around one book. Homa's planner
  should natively support a one-book-many-lenses rhythm at preschool
  scale.
