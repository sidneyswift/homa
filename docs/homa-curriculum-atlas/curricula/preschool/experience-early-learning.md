# Experience Early Learning (Mother Goose Time)

## Snapshot

Experience Early Learning, still widely known by its pre-2021 name
Mother Goose Time, is the subscription-box preschool: a monthly themed
kit containing a full month of daily lesson plans and the physical
materials to run them, prepped into day-by-day bags. The company dates
itself to the mid-1980s (its own materials cite both 1984 and 1987),
sells primarily to childcare centers and home daycares, and maintains a
homeschool program marketed as "the most homeschool-friendly option
available," promising about 80 learning experiences per month across 33
skills and 8 domains. Its flagship Experience Preschool line covers
ages roughly 2.5 or 3 to 5, with Experience Toddler (18 months to 3)
and Experience Baby (birth to 18 months) below it. It is now "a
brightwheel company," folded into the childcare-software platform, and
it is by a wide margin the most expensive way to homeschool a
preschooler, priced against daycare tuition rather than against $40
PDFs.

## Reputation and rankings

No Cathy Duffy review exists. Reputation lives in two adjacent worlds:
among home daycare and small preschool providers it is a beloved
institution (testimonials emphasize professional image, embedded
assessment, and hours of prep saved), and among homeschoolers it is
the "everything in the box, zero prep" option recommended for working
parents, grandparents, and burned-out planners. Long-form homeschool
reviews praise the daily pre-packed bags, the professionally produced
music, and the craft quality; the recurring complaints are cost, the
volume of consumable craft output, and a classroom shape (circle time,
calendar displays, name tags) that can feel institutional for one
child at a kitchen table. The 2021 rename and the later brightwheel
integration moved the brand visibly toward the B2B childcare market:
public price charts disappeared behind quote forms, and assessment
tooling now routes through brightwheel. Trajectory: stable-to-growing
institutionally, fading as a homeschool-culture name; homeschool
roundups increasingly file it as the expensive boxed option.

## Pedagogy and method

Theme-based, whole-child, standards-mapped play. Each month is a
thematic study broken into weekly topics and 20 daily lessons; skills
(social-emotional, physical, language, cognitive) are woven into
games, art, music, and movement rather than taught as subjects. The
company publishes a skills framework (33 skills across 8 domains, 35
in some of its own materials) aligned to NAEYC, Head Start, Common
Core, and all 50 states' early learning standards, and its Experience
Assessment observation system carries a third-party psychometric
validation claim. The model is teacher-led circle time plus activity
rotations, scaled down for home use; parents are told to follow the
teacher guide day by day, and everything needed is in the day's bag.
It is the most schoolish pedagogy in the preschool category: not in
worksheet terms (activities are hands-on and playful) but in its
classroom rhythms, documentation habits, and standards vocabulary.

## Structure and scope

- Monthly kit anatomy: a Teacher Tool Bag (four-week teacher guide
  set, skills chart, theme web, activity calendar, supply gathering
  list), a Circle Time bag (monthly display pieces, concept cards for
  the featured shape, color, letters, and numbers), and 20 Daily
  Discovery Bags with the art materials, games, photos, and science
  supplies for each day, pre-sorted per child.
- Monthly extras build a library over the year: storybooks, a Forest
  Friends character book (character education), music, games, and
  manipulatives; a Getting Started kit ships with the first box.
- Suggested book list of about 20 titles per month, two included.
- Add-ons: More Math and More Literacy packs, Experience God
  (Christian supplement), STEAM packs, Dance 'N Beats, and toy
  collections; Experience Toddler and Experience Baby run parallel
  for younger siblings.
- Digital member resources mirror the print planning materials, and
  assessment records live in brightwheel.
- No multi-year scope and sequence in the homeschool sense: themes
  rotate monthly and the skills framework repeats with rising
  difficulty, so a family subscribes for a season rather than
  completing a course.

## A lesson up close

The parent opens the day's numbered Discovery Bag, skims the teacher
guide's page for the day (an opening circle-time routine with the
calendar and concept cards, a story or discussion, then two or three
activities: a craft project, a game, a movement or music block), and
runs it with materials already cut, counted, and bagged. A day
produces visible output, usually a craft the child made and a game
played, and the guide prompts quick observational assessment notes.
Providers report roughly 1-2 hours of planning per week instead of
many; homeschool parents report the opposite friction, more structure
and more paper crafts than one child needs. Time per day flexes from
30 minutes to a full preschool morning depending on how much of the
guide the family runs.

## Materials and visual design

The strongest physical production in the category: full-color printed
guides, die-cut game pieces, posters, concept cards, quality craft
inputs, and professionally recorded music, all branded in a bright,
rounded, classroom-poster aesthetic. It signals licensed-preschool
polish, which is exactly what home daycare buyers want their parent
clients to see, and the opposite of the household-items minimalism of
Playing Preschool. The kits accumulate: a year of subscriptions
fills real shelf space with games, books, and displays.

## Pricing

As of Jul 2026 the company quotes pricing per program through a form
rather than publishing a chart. The last public chart (2023-24 school
year) listed the preschool curriculum kit for 1-4 children at $109.99
per month plus $19.99 shipping, with prepay discounts of 10% (3-5
months) to 15% (9-12 months); supplements like Experience God ran
about $20 per month for the teacher pack. Homeschool.com's directory
listing shows $70.99 per month per child, which likely reflects an
older or single-child configuration; treat both figures as
directional. Any way you compute it, a school year lands near or
above $1,000, ten to twenty times the category's PDF norm. Materials
are largely consumable per child (craft supplies per student), so
multi-kid reuse is limited to guides and games.

## Why families choose it

- True open-and-go: the only preschool option where every material
  for every day arrives prepped, which working and burned-out parents
  buy as time, not curriculum.
- Provider crossover: home daycares can serve mixed groups and show
  parents a professional, standards-aligned program.
- Assessment and standards language: the rare early-years product
  that documents development, which matters to providers and to
  parents facing preschool-equivalency expectations.
- Kids like the boxes: monthly delivery is an event, and the craft
  and music production values hold toddler attention.

## Why families leave it

- Price: the single dominant churn factor; families discover the $40
  PDF universe or realize a library card does most of this.
- Consumable clutter and craft fatigue: a craft per day is a lot of
  glitter for one child, and the accumulated kit pieces overwhelm
  small homes.
- Classroom shape: circle time for an audience of one feels
  performative; homeschool families wanting relationship-first
  mornings drift to literature-based options.
- Subscription friction: monthly billing plus shipping makes pausing
  and quitting easy, and the quote-form pricing adds distrust.

## Competitive notes for Homa

- Vocabulary to know: Mother Goose Time (families still say it),
  Experience Preschool / Toddler / Baby, Discovery Bags, Circle Time
  bag, Experience God, More Math and More Literacy, brightwheel. A
  family using it is either an ex-provider, a working homeschool
  parent buying time, or a family with daycare-level budget.
- The lesson in the model: prep is the product. Its buyers pay
  $100+ per month mostly to not gather, cut, and plan. Homa's
  activity generation should treat prep elimination as a first-class
  feature: printable-ready, household-supply-listed, no assembly.
- The assessment integration is the category's only real
  documentation story, and it is provider-grade, not parent-grade.
  Homa can give ordinary families the same "here is her development,
  observed through play" narrative without the classroom apparatus.
- The monthly-theme-box rhythm (theme, daily bags, a music moment, a
  craft) is a scheduling primitive some families will ask Homa to
  reproduce cheaply: "make me a month of ocean weeks like Mother
  Goose Time used to."
