# Tapestry of Grace

## Snapshot

Tapestry of Grace (TOG), from Lampstand Press and author Marcia Somerville,
is the maximalist whole-family classical curriculum: one history-centered
plan that teaches every child in the house the same era at the same time,
each at their own level. Four year-plans cycle through all of recorded
history (ancients; medieval through the American Revolution; the 1800s;
1900 to the present), so a K-12 student surveys history three times at
rising depth, the trivium mapped onto repetition. Around the history spine
it weaves literature, writing, worldview and church history, geography,
government, philosophy, and fine arts. TOG is famous for two things: the
depth families call transformational, and "the Fog," the community's own
name for the multi-week overwhelm new users experience facing its 40-50
page weekly plans. It is a buffet by design, not an open-and-go program.

## Reputation and rankings

- Cathy Duffy features TOG among her Top Picks for classical Christian
  families, praising the chronological connections and flexibility while
  warning plainly that it is intellectually rigorous, demands heavy teacher
  preparation, and should have "large blocks of time... dedicated over the
  summer" to planning.
- The community coined "Tapestry Fog" for the onboarding overwhelm; the
  term is old (blog posts from 2010), affectionate, and still current in
  forum advice. The standing counsel: it is a buffet, choose a plate, do
  not eat everything.
- One reviewer's blunt line, often echoed: "the most user un-friendly
  manual I think I have ever come across," paired in the same breath with
  praise for the literature quality.
- Trajectory: stable niche. TOG owns the "whole family, one era, maximum
  depth" position; families comparing it shop Sonlight (easier, boxed) and
  My Father's World (lighter family-cycle) rather than CC or Memoria.

## Pedagogy and method

Classical unit study: history is the integrating spine, the trivium is
implemented as four learning levels applied to the same weekly topic:
Lower Grammar (K-3, stories, coloring maps, narration, simple projects),
Upper Grammar (4-6, detailed reading, written narration, geography),
Dialectic (7-9, analytical reading, essays, logic, worldview discussion),
and Rhetoric (10-12, primary sources, research papers, Socratic
discussion led by the parent). Students move levels by readiness, not
grade. Reading assignments are marked Core vs In-Depth so parents can
scale; the author explicitly coaches assigning only what fits each child.
Assessment is discussion, writing, and optional purchased Evaluations;
the parent is discussion leader and must pre-read the background
material, which Cathy Duffy frames as the parent acquiring "a great deal
of education of their own."

## Structure and scope

- Four year-plans, each 36 weeks in four 9-week units. Year 1: creation
  through the fall of Rome. Year 2: medieval world through the American
  Revolution. Year 3: the 19th century. Year 4: 1900 to current events.
- Each week-plan (40-50 pages) contains, for all four levels at once:
  reading assignments (spine and literature), discussion outlines and
  worldview threads, writing assignments, geography and map work,
  timeline entries, hands-on projects, and fine arts.
- The Loom: the digital companion holding year-wide resources and forms.
  Optional add-ons sold separately: Map Aids, Evaluations (weekly tests),
  Pop Quiz (audio review), Writing Aids (the writing program's toolkit).
- Books are not included: TOG is a plan plus book lists, with Bookshelf
  Central as the publisher's bookstore and explicit support for library
  substitution (Story of the World appears as an alternate spine in Years
  3-4).
- One purchase covers the whole family with no per-student fees, and the
  plan is reusable on the next cycle pass.

## A lesson up close

TOG's unit of work is the week, not the day. The parent's weekend ritual:
read the teacher's notes for the week, pick each child's plate from the
buffet (Core readings first), reserve library books, and print the maps
and student activity pages for the levels in play. Across the week, each
child reads their own level's assignments on the same topic; the
6-year-old colors a map and narrates, the 11-year-old writes a summary
and fills a timeline, the 15-year-old reads a primary source. The week
converges in discussion: the parent uses the provided Socratic outline
with the Dialectic/Rhetoric students while younger kids do the project
(building, baking, drawing the era). What a completed week produces:
narrations, maps, timeline entries, written work, and a family that
argued about the same century at dinner. The failure mode is equally
concrete: an unpruned week is more than any family can do, which is the
Fog.

## Materials and visual design

TOG ships as thick loose-leaf or digital PDF year-plans meant for 3-inch
binders (the binder-assembly ritual is part of community lore), dense
two-column teacher pages, functional student activity pages, and no
decorative design language of its own; the beauty is outsourced to the
trade books on the reading list. The aesthetic signal is graduate
seminar, not gift shop: buyers are told up front this is a professional
tool for a parent-educator, and the packaging matches.

## Pricing

As of Jul 2026, snapshots disagree enough to warrant care: digital units
run about $45-85 and full-year digital access roughly $120-250 depending
on format and sale (one 2026 check reports $120-185; an early-2025 review
reports $200-400 per year-plan; verify at tapestryofgrace.com before
quoting). Books are the real budget: $500-700 per year is realistic with
Dialectic/Rhetoric students buying new, and Lampstand's own
everything-new ceiling estimate is about $2,000, rarely reached because
of library use, substitutions, and reuse. The multi-kid economics are
the best in paid classical education: one plan, all children, reusable
next cycle.

## Why families choose it

- One preparation serves every child: the multi-age design is the whole
  point, and for large families it beats per-grade programs on both money
  and sanity.
- Depth: worldview, church history, and Socratic discussion integrated,
  not bolted on; HSLDA-style college-prep credibility at the top end.
- Flexibility with permission: Core vs In-Depth marking and
  substitute-friendly book lists make trimming legitimate.
- The family intellectual culture it creates: everyone in the same
  century, arguing at dinner.

## Why families leave it

- The Fog never lifts for some: parents who need open-and-go leave for
  Sonlight, My Father's World, or BookShark, which schedule days instead
  of offering buffets.
- Prep load collides with life: a new baby or a job ends the weekly
  pre-reading, and the family downgrades to a lighter spine (Story of the
  World alone).
- Only-child families: the multi-age superpower is wasted, and
  per-student programs fit better.
- Worldview mismatch: the Reformed thread is pervasive; secular and
  differently-convicted families use the book lists but not the plan.

## Competitive notes for Homa

- Vocabulary: year-plan (1-4), unit (1-4), week-plan, learning levels
  (Lower Grammar, Upper Grammar, Dialectic, Rhetoric), the Loom, Core vs
  In-Depth, Map Aids, Writing Aids, Pop Quiz, the Fog. "We're in Year 2
  Unit 3, one Dialectic and two Lower Grammar" fully specifies a family's
  week; Homa should parse that sentence.
- TOG is the strongest existing proof that one-topic-many-levels is a
  product shape families pay for, and its failure mode (the Fog) is
  precisely a planning-load problem. An assistant that does the weekly
  plate-picking (choose Core readings per child, print the right level's
  pages, prep the discussion outline) attacks TOG's number one churn
  cause without touching its content.
- Homa's multi-kid materials generation should learn TOG's level grammar:
  same topic, four output registers (color-and-narrate, write-and-map,
  analyze-and-essay, source-and-debate).
- Copyright care: TOG's plans are the product; never reproduce week-plan
  content. Homa-generated era-aligned materials must be original and can
  cite the family's own books.
