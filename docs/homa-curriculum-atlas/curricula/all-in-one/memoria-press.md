# Memoria Press Classical Core Curriculum

## Snapshot

Memoria Press is the boxed-curriculum answer to classical education: complete
Junior K through 12th grade packages a parent teaches at home, no co-op
required. Founded in 1994 in Louisville, Kentucky by Cheryl Lowe, initially
to publish her Latina Christiana Latin course, it grew into a full publisher
whose materials are developed and continuously tested in its own
brick-and-mortar school, Highlands Latin School. The identity is
Latin-forward, book-driven, and unapologetically rigorous: Latin begins as
early as grade 1-2 and runs to AP, literature is taught through whole
classic novels with study guides, and composition follows the ancient
progymnasmata sequence. Where Classical Conversations sells community,
Memoria Press sells a finished plan: curriculum manuals with every day of 34
weeks laid out in checkboxes. The publisher self-reports serving families in
all 50 states and over 50 countries; it is the standing "structured
classical without CC" recommendation across reviewer sites and forums.

## Reputation and rankings

- Cathy Duffy Top Picks: both the Classical Core packages ("Memoria Press
  Complete Programs for PreK-10") and Simply Classical, its special-needs
  adaptation, appear in her 103 Top Picks list. Her review notes the
  original third grade moved too fast for many students, which is why most
  levels now ship in two speeds (standard Classical Core and Accelerated).
- Its Latin sequence is widely described by reviewers as the best available
  to homeschoolers; HomeschoolPicks calls it "arguably the best K-12 Latin
  curriculum available" (citing Cathy Duffy's praise for its clarity and
  gentle progression).
- Community sentiment is warm but consistent about the load: "truly
  classical. Thorough. Beautiful... And very rigorous" is a representative
  blogger verdict. Because materials are also used in real classrooms, the
  default schedule is heavy; the publisher's own forum normalizes trimming
  ("treat the manual as a guide, not law"). Veteran users tell newcomers
  not to do every workbook.
- The plain black-and-white student pages are polarizing in a mild way:
  some parents call the look dry; others, including parents of children
  with ADHD, praise the distraction-free layout on purpose.
- Trajectory: stable to rising with the broader classical surge. It is the
  most common landing spot named by families leaving Classical Conversations
  who still want classical structure, and its Simply Classical line has no
  real competitor in the classical special-needs niche.

## Pedagogy and method

Memoria Press implements the trivium as curriculum design rather than as a
weekly event. Its house philosophy (stated on the publisher's own curriculum
page) is top-down: define the educated graduate first, then lay the steps
backward to Junior K. Distinctives:

- Latin as the spine. The publisher's founding argument is that Latin trains
  the mind, orders English grammar, and carries the vocabulary of the
  sciences. The sequence: Prima Latina (grades 1-2 intro), Latina
  Christiana (3-4), then the Forms series (First through Fourth Form,
  grades 5-8) into Henle and Wheelock for high school, with AP Latin
  possible.
- Mastery through repetition and recitation. Daily opening recitation
  (catechism-style question and answer, poetry, math facts), copybook work,
  and cumulative review are built into every level. Assessment is
  traditional: workbook exercises, quizzes, and tests with teacher keys.
- Whole books, not excerpts. Literature is a set of real novels per grade
  (for example grade 3 pairs D'Aulaires' Greek Myths with four classic
  novels) studied through Memoria's own student study guides.
- Composition via progymnasmata: the Classical Composition series walks the
  ancient rhetoric exercises (fable, narrative, chreia, and so on) across
  the upper grades.
- Outsourced math: Memoria Press deliberately does not write its own math;
  packages schedule Rod and Staff in the elementary years (Saxon also
  recommended) and VideoText or standard texts later. The curriculum manual
  paces the third-party math day by day.
- Parent role: teacher, from a script-adjacent manual. Lessons are
  teacher-led at the table; instructional DVDs and streaming video exist
  for Latin and composition so older students can partially self-run.

## Structure and scope

- Levels: Junior K through 12, one package per grade. Grades 3 through 9
  ship in standard and Accelerated variants (Accelerated encounters some
  resources a year earlier). Eleventh and twelfth grades are more a la
  carte (Cathy Duffy notes no fully fixed packages historically; a twelfth
  grade set with curriculum manual now exists, anchored by Vergil and Pliny
  in Latin).
- Subject strands per grade, per the publisher's package builder:
  recitation, phonics/spelling, grammar, core language arts skills,
  literature and poetry, classical studies (Greek myths, then formal Greek
  and Roman history via the Famous Men series), Christian studies, math
  (scheduled third-party), science (nature study early, traditional texts
  later), writing/composition, American and modern studies in upper
  elementary, and Latin throughout.
- Components per package: a spiral curriculum manual (the daily plan), all
  student books and consumable workbooks, teacher manuals with answer keys,
  study guides for the literature, and wall charts or flashcards where the
  subject uses them. A New User add-on covers first-time extras.
- Pace: 34 weeks of five-day plans; the publisher explicitly blesses
  adjusting to four days.
- Parallel lines: Simply Classical (ages 2 through adult, special needs,
  with readiness assessments per level) and Memoria Academy (accredited
  online classes, $400-700 per course, for outsourcing single subjects).

## A lesson up close

The parent's day starts in the curriculum manual, a spiral book laid out as
a week-per-spread grid: columns for Monday through Thursday/Friday, one row
per subject, every cell a checkbox with a terse instruction (the publisher's
public first grade sample shows the pattern). A first grade day opens with
recitation from the appendix schedule: prayers, the week's poem, letter
sounds, addition families, reviewed cumulatively so weeks of material stay
warm. Then short blocks: phonics in First Start Reading, spelling, a math
workbook lesson, copybook (careful transcription of scripture or poetry in
the child's best hand), and cursive. The literature and enrichment block
assigns one read-aloud picture book per week, re-read across days, plus one
painting (a Cassatt, say) and one piece of music (a Faure movement) held for
the whole week and reviewed by looking and listening again.

The child's paper reality is workbook pages and copybook pages: numbered
exercises, handwriting rules, no cartoon border art. In grade 5, the same
grid runs First Form Latin (video lesson optional, then workbook exercises
and drill), a literature guide chapter with comprehension and vocabulary
questions on a novel chapter, composition, math, and recitation. Reported
daily time: 1-2 hours in K-2, about 3 hours by grade 3, up to 5-6 hours in
high school; forum veterans coach overwhelmed parents to cut workbooks
before cutting books. What a completed day produces is a visible paper
trail: checked boxes in the manual, filled workbook pages, a copybook page,
which is exactly what portfolio-state parents want.

## Materials and visual design

The aesthetic is deliberate restraint: clean serif typography, generous
margins, black-and-white interiors with fine-art plates where art belongs
(study guide covers use classical paintings). Student guides are consumable
workbooks, roughly letter-size, saddle or perfect bound; curriculum manuals
are spiral so they lie flat next to the teacher. There is no mascot, no
comic art, and very little color; the publisher's fans read this as
seriousness and focus (several explicitly credit the plain pages for
helping distractible kids), critics read it as drab. The box itself is
heavy: a full grade package is a stack of 15-25 books. The signal to the
buyer is private-school gravitas at mail-order prices, reinforced by the
Highlands Latin School provenance the marketing leads with.

## Pricing

As of Jul 2026, per independent 2026-catalog summaries (link the package
pages for the live truth):

- Junior K core package: about $250-350.
- Grades 1-3 core packages: about $400-500.
- Grades 4-8 core packages: about $500-700 (repo segment research recorded
  K-6 packages at $513-625 in Jul 2026, consistent).
- High school core packages: about $600-900.
- Memoria Academy online classes: $400-700 per course per year, optional.
- Customizable package builders let families swap or drop strands (for
  example dropping math if they already own one), which meaningfully lowers
  the real spend.

Economics for multi-kid families are favorable relative to CC: teacher
manuals, curriculum manuals, novels, and charts are reusable; only student
workbooks are consumable re-buys. Resale is active and clean sets hold
value (standard homeschool 40-60% of retail patterns apply). No tuition
layer exists unless the family adds Memoria Academy.

## Why families choose it

- Open-and-go classical: the curriculum manual removes planning entirely
  while keeping the parent as teacher.
- The Latin: families who decided Latin matters buy the acknowledged
  category leader and stay for everything else.
- Rigor with a finish line: a coherent JrK-12 plan from one publisher, with
  the credibility of a real school using the same materials.
- Half the cost of CC with no co-op day, no tuition, no volunteer
  obligations, and no community-quality lottery.
- Simply Classical: parents of special-needs kids who still want classical
  education have essentially one serious option, and this is it.

## Why families leave it

- Load: the classroom-derived schedule overwhelms; families burn out doing
  every workbook. Some trim and stay; others leave for gentler structures
  (Charlotte Mason curricula, notably Simply Charlotte Mason or A Gentle
  Feast) citing the same rigor they came for.
- Dryness for a particular child: workbook-and-recitation format loses
  kids who need color, hands-on work, or discussion; leavers head to
  literature-rich or unit-study programs (Tapestry of Grace, Gather Round).
- Latin fatigue: the spine subject is also the first subject families quit;
  once Latin goes, the package logic weakens and families drift eclectic.
- Teacher-intensity: a parent who needs low-involvement options moves to
  video or online courseware (Veritas self-paced, Memoria's own Academy, or
  Teaching Textbooks style per-subject swaps).
- Worldview calibration: secular classical families use pieces (Latin,
  literature guides) but not the Christian Studies strand, so they
  assemble rather than buy the box.

## Competitive notes for Homa

- Vocabulary to know: Classical Core vs Accelerated, curriculum manual,
  recitation, copybook, the Latin ladder (Prima Latina, Latina Christiana,
  First through Fourth Form, Henle), Famous Men books, progymnasmata and
  Classical Composition, Simply Classical (special needs), Memoria Academy.
  A mom saying "we're in Third Form" has stated her child's grade band
  (roughly 7th) and daily structure.
- The manual is the product lesson: Memoria proves parents will pay for a
  finished daily plan with checkboxes. Homa's planner surfaces compete
  directly with this artifact; matching its scannability (week grid, one
  cell per subject per day, cumulative review made visible) is the bar.
- Gap Homa fills: the manual is static. When a family falls two weeks
  behind in one strand, rebalancing 34 weeks by hand is exactly the painful
  clerical work an assistant can do without touching pedagogy.
- Materials fit: this family accepts worksheets happily (unlike CM
  families), but expects them plain, serious, and cumulative. Practice
  generated to accompany a Memoria strand should look like Memoria: clean,
  black and white, no cartoon energy, review woven in.
- Never generate Latin translations or answers for the student, and never
  reproduce Memoria's copyrighted exercises; drill scaffolds and parent
  answer-checking aids are the safe lane, consistent with the classical
  segment's "AI must not do the child's thinking" landmine (see
  `docs/homa-planning/icp-research/08-segments-curricula.md` §7 card 1).
