# Timberdoodle

## Snapshot

Timberdoodle is not a curriculum author at all: it is the market's
best-known curator, a family company serving homeschool families since
1985 (publisher-reported) by assembling grade-level kits from other
publishers' best products, with a strong bias toward hands-on materials,
STEM, puzzles, and thinking skills. Every grade from preschool ("Tiny
Tots") through 12th ships in three tiers (Basic, Complete, Elite) and,
for most grades, two worldview editions: Classic (includes some
Christian components) and Nonreligious (secular swaps, marketed as
charter-school-friendly). A custom kit builder lets buyers swap any
component. Timberdoodle matters to this category because it proves the
curation business model: the value sold is research time and confidence,
not authored content, and it deliberately straddles the secular and
Christian markets with parallel SKUs.

## Reputation and rankings

A 2026 roundup review (synthesizing publisher data, Cathy Duffy notes,
and forum feedback) calls it one of the most distinctive boxed providers
because of the eclectic curation, notes typical complete kits at $500 to
$1,500, cites 30+ components in a typical elementary kit, and reports
most surveyed parents kept the full kit without swaps. Community
sentiment: loved for unboxing joy, quality of the chosen products
(thinking-skills workbooks, building sets, quality manipulatives), and
for saving the research spiral; criticized for cost, for uneven cohesion
(components from different publishers do not reference each other), and
for the parent still needing to orchestrate many small products.
Secular-community caution: the Nonreligious kits are widely used and
charter-accepted, but strict secular reviewers note the company itself is
Christian-owned and vet component lists item by item. Trajectory: stable,
with the kit-tier structure and the annual kit refresh (kits are dated,
like model years) keeping the catalog current.

## Pedagogy and method

There is no single pedagogy; the curation has a recognizable taste
instead: hands-on over lecture, thinking skills as a first-class subject,
STEM and building toys as school, workbook-based skills practice chosen
for independence, and a preference for products a child can largely
self-run. The company publishes a grade handbook and an online scheduler
that turns the kit into daily and weekly checklists between chosen start
and end dates, which is the glue holding the disparate components
together. Parent involvement is medium and orchestration-shaped: less
teaching, more managing many parallel products.

## Structure and scope

- Grades: Tiny Tots/preschool through 12th, one kit per grade year,
  refreshed annually (2026-2027 kits current as of this writing).
- Tiers: Basic (math, language arts, thinking skills), Complete (adds
  science, history, geography, more hands-on), Elite (adds art, extra
  STEM, enrichment; at 12th grade the publisher's example includes
  calculus, world history, economics, formal logic, game programming,
  and an engine build).
- Editions: Classic vs Nonreligious at most grades; the publisher's
  scope-and-sequence page documents per-grade subject coverage for both.
  Differences concentrate in history, science, economics, and thinking
  skills components.
- Components come from many publishers; each kit includes the grade
  handbook and scheduler access. The custom kit builder swaps components
  in and out with price adjustments; additional-student kits cover
  consumables and per-child items.

## A lesson up close

There is no Timberdoodle lesson; there is a Timberdoodle day. The parent
(or the student, from about mid-elementary) works from the scheduler
checklist: a math lesson from the kit's math program, a language arts
workbook session, a thinking-skills puzzle page, a chapter or activity
from the science or history component, and time with a hands-on or STEM
product. Each component follows its own publisher's format, so the day
is a relay across different page designs, pacing models, and assessment
styles; the checklist is what makes it feel like one program. Completed
work varies by component: workbook pages, builds, experiments. Assessment
is whatever each product ships; the kit adds none.

## Materials and visual design

The box is the product: a large, heavy, varied unboxing of workbooks,
manipulatives, building sets, art supplies, and games from many brands,
which makes Timberdoodle box-day content a genre of its own on social
media. Visual language is therefore plural by design. The unifying
artifacts are the grade handbook and the scheduler printouts. The signal
to the buyer is abundance and curation: "we already found the good
stuff."

## Pricing

As of Jul 2026: 2026 twelfth-grade kit $1,959 as configured on the
product page (Elite tier; Complete and Basic tiers price lower), with a
$1,154.86 additional-student kit; 2026 reviews place typical complete
kits at $500-1,500 with an average around $485 for lower tiers and
grades (homeschoolpicks figures; The Curriculum Compass records $200-600
for kits at the value end). Every component is also sold individually,
and the kit builder reprices customizations. Consumable workbooks
repurchase per child; manipulatives, games, and building sets reuse
well and resell well.

## Why families choose it

- Curation as a service: it collapses the months-long research spiral
  into one purchase, the strongest version of "just tell me what to buy"
  that still respects eclectic taste.
- Hands-on and thinking-skills identity: kits feel like enrichment, not
  school-at-home, and suit kids who hate textbooks.
- The two-edition, swap-anything model lets one vendor serve secular
  charter families and Christian families at once.
- Charter and ESA spendability: the Nonreligious kits are positioned
  explicitly for charter approval.

## Why families leave it

- Cost versus coherence: paying box prices for components that do not
  talk to each other wears thin; families who want integration move to
  authored programs (BookShark, MBTP, Sonlight).
- Orchestration fatigue: many small products means many small decisions;
  some families keep two favorite components and drop the kit.
- Component misfits: one wrong math or LA choice inside an otherwise
  good kit pushes families to assemble their own stack next year, which
  is the natural exit ramp into full eclecticism.

## Competitive notes for Homa

- Vocabulary to know: kit year (2026-2027), tiers (Basic, Complete,
  Elite), Classic vs Nonreligious, custom kit builder, handbook,
  scheduler. "We use the Timberdoodle 3rd grade Complete" means a
  multi-publisher stack under one checklist; ask which components
  survived, because swaps are normal.
- Timberdoodle is the closest existing business to Homa's stack-assembly
  beachhead behavior: it monetizes curation and scheduling of other
  people's curricula. Its scheduler (kit to daily checklists between two
  dates) is exactly the planning primitive Homa's planner must beat, and
  Homa can do it across any stack, not just one vendor's kit.
- The gap it leaves: no adaptation after purchase. The kit cannot notice
  a component failing, differentiate for a second child, or carry
  progress across a swap. Homa can.
- Materials lesson: thinking-skills pages and hands-on kits earn a place
  in this audience's day; Homa-generated materials can include puzzle
  and logic shapes, not just academic drill.
