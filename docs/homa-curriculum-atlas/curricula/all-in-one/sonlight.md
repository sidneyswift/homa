# Sonlight

## Snapshot

Sonlight is the original literature-based box: a complete Christian
curriculum built from stacks of real books instead of textbooks, founded in
1990 by John and Sarita Holzmann to serve overseas missionary families who
needed a full school year shipped in one box. Each program centers on an
Instructor's Guide (IG) that schedules 30-40 real books (history,
biographies, novels, read-alouds) into a 36-week plan integrating history,
Bible, geography, and literature, with language arts, science, and math
added around it. The worldview is evangelical with a distinctive global
missions heart (missionary biographies, unreached-peoples emphasis, world
cultures focus). Sonlight owns the "we read great books together on the
couch" identity in the Christian market, at box-market prices: it is one
of the most expensive mainstream packages. Its secular twin BookShark
(spun off in 2014) sells nearly the same structure without the Bible.

## Reputation and rankings

- Cathy Duffy's review is warm: she credits the Ruth Beechick and
  Charlotte Mason influences, and singles out the "open and teach"
  Instructor's Guides as "one of the best and most important features."
- Community sentiment is devotion-heavy: Sonlight has one of the market's
  most loyal alumni cultures, with families who credit it for kids who
  love books. Praise clusters on book selection, the IG's daily notes and
  discussion questions, and no-busywork days.
- The critical threads are equally consistent: the read-aloud load is
  heavy and some parents grow to dread it; the week-by-week schedule makes
  families feel perpetually behind; juggling several concurrent chapter
  books is administratively taxing; each child eventually needs their own
  level, which breaks the budget for large families; and some Christian
  families find the Bible thread separate rather than integrated, or
  object to specific book choices (other religions introduced early,
  magic in some titles, mature themes in upper levels).
- Trajectory: stable with an aging-classic feel. It defined
  literature-based homeschooling, but cheaper PDF-based literature
  programs (BookShark for seculars, Gather Round, TGTB, BiblioPlan) now
  compete for the same shelf, and its price point pushes newcomers away.

## Pedagogy and method

- Literature as the delivery mechanism: history, culture, and character
  are learned through narrative books (living books in the CM sense),
  read aloud by the parent and independently by the child, then talked
  about. Influences the founders name: Ruth Beechick's structured-but-
  real-life philosophy and Charlotte Mason's living books.
- The IG is the method: a week-per-spread schedule grid assigning exact
  pages per book per day, with teaching notes, background research,
  vocabulary, maps, timeline entries, and discussion questions supplied.
  Sarita Holzmann describes reading every candidate book multiple times
  and providing counter-balancing commentary where a book has a bias.
- Discussion replaces testing in the humanities: comprehension and
  meaning-making happen orally. There are no history worksheets or
  quizzes; assessment apparatus exists mainly in the separately scheduled
  language arts, science, and math.
- Two literature streams per level: Read-Alouds (parent reads, above the
  child's own reading level) and Readers (child reads independently at
  skill level).
- Deliberate exposure: Sonlight includes non-Christian perspectives and
  hard history on purpose, with IG notes to frame discussion. This is a
  feature for its fans and a filter for stricter worldview families.
- Parent role is high and relational: hours of reading aloud and
  discussing daily, but near-zero prep and no lesson writing.

## Structure and scope

- Levels are letters, not grades, keyed to history topics and age bands:
  T and P (preschool), K, then A through J covering American and world
  history in three cycles from kindergarten to graduation (for example A
  Exploring American History ages 6-8, F Eastern Hemisphere, G and H
  World History, J History of Science ages 13-15), then high school
  levels 100 (American History), 200 (Church History), 300 (20th Century
  World), 400 (American Government/Economics), 500, and 600. One HBL
  level can serve multiple close-in-age children.
- A complete year is assembled as History/Bible/Literature (HBL) plus
  Language Arts (writing assignments keyed to the books, plus spelling
  and handwriting in early grades), Science (scheduled books, activity
  sheets, and kits), and math from partner publishers (Sonlight sells
  Math-U-See, Saxon, Horizons, and others rather than its own math).
- All-Subjects Packages (ASP) bundle everything for one student and one
  year at the maximum discount, in 4-day or 5-day week variants.
- IGs come printed, with an online IG option (12-month access) that adds
  digital scheduling, record-keeping, and student assignment views.
- Guarantees and perks are part of the offer: a money-back "Love to
  Learn, Love to Teach" guarantee, free shipping thresholds, and payment
  plans.

## A lesson up close

A day in HBL B (roughly ages 7-9):

- The parent opens the IG to this week's spread: five columns (or four in
  the 4-day plan), one row per book or subject. Each cell says exactly
  what to read: specific pages of a history spine, a chapter of a
  read-aloud novel, a Bible passage plus memory work, a poem.
- School happens mostly on the couch: the parent reads the history pages
  and the read-aloud chapter aloud, the child follows the atlas map or
  places a timeline figure (pre-gummed stickers in levels K-J), and the
  parent asks the IG's discussion questions and listens.
- The child then does their own Reader chapter and the scheduled language
  arts: a copywork or dictation passage drawn from the week's books and,
  a few times weekly, a creative writing assignment; activity sheets
  cover comprehension and skills.
- Science is a separate short block from the science IG: a reading, an
  activity sheet, sometimes an experiment from the supplied kit.
- Nothing is turned in: a completed day produces read chapters, a filled
  activity sheet or two, a timeline entry, and conversation. Parents log
  progress by checking off the IG grid, which doubles as the homeschool
  record.

The IG page anatomy is the product: schedule grid up front, then per-week
notes sections (background, vocabulary, discussion answers), laminated
maps, and appendices. Three-week samples of every IG are free downloads,
which is the fastest way to see the layout.

## Materials and visual design

- The box is the brand moment: a year arrives as a heavy carton of 30-40
  trade books plus IG binders, famously photographed on unboxing day.
  Book covers do the visual work; Sonlight's own materials are utilitarian.
- IGs are thick, tabbed, three-hole-punched binder stock, black and white,
  grid-heavy: built for daily use, not beauty. Laminated full-color maps
  and timeline figure sheets ride along.
- Activity sheets and LA worksheets are functional black-and-white pages;
  the aesthetic pleasure lives in the literature, which is the point. The
  overall signal: serious, bookish, missions-minded family culture.

## Pricing

All prices as of Jul 2026 from the publisher's pages.

- All-Subjects Packages list from $1,055.99 (Level A) up to roughly
  $1,410-1,575 for upper-elementary 5-day configurations with required
  resources; the standing 20% ASP discount brings most orders to about
  $845-1,260 ([ASP collection](https://www.sonlight.com/collections/all-subjects-package),
  [Level D example](https://www.sonlight.com/products/fourth-grade-all-subjects-package)).
  The repo's Jul 2026 market table records list $1,056-1,501 with sale
  prices $845-1,200 ([segments research](../../../homa-planning/icp-research/08-segments-curricula.md) §4).
- Buying by subject costs more per piece; HBL alone is the core purchase
  and LA, science, and math add per-student costs.
- Consumables economics: the books are non-consumable and famously
  resellable (dedicated Sonlight buy-sell-trade groups; complete sets
  hold value well), while IG activity sheets are consumable per child.
  The catch for large families is that levels are per-student stages:
  siblings can share one HBL if close in age, but a spread-out family
  buys multiple levels a year, which is the documented cost breaker.
- The one-year money-back guarantee materially lowers trial risk and is
  marketed hard.

## Why families choose it

- The dream it sells: children who love books, learning together on the
  couch, no worksheets, no lesson prep. For book-culture families it is
  identity-affirming.
- The IG does the planning: a year of daily plans, discussion questions,
  and answers, assembled by someone the community trusts.
- Missions and world-culture depth unmatched in the category; missionary
  kids and globally minded families are the heritage audience.
- Multi-age flexibility in the middle grades: one HBL can carry two or
  three kids.
- Strong resale value takes the sting out of the price.

## Why families leave it

- Read-aloud fatigue: the parent is the delivery mechanism, and when her
  voice or time gives out the program stalls. This is the top documented
  quit reason.
- Schedule guilt: the week-grid makes drift visible, and families
  describe spiraling once they fall behind across several concurrent
  books.
- Cost at scale: multiple levels for multiple kids at four figures each
  is where big families tap out and move to family-style programs
  ([My Father's World](my-fathers-world.md), Gather Round) or cheaper
  spines (Story of the World, BiblioPlan).
- Worldview mismatches in both directions: too worldly for stricter
  families (book choices, separate-feeling Bible), too Christian for
  secular ones (who go to BookShark, its intentional secular twin).
- Kids who do not process well by listening (strong kinesthetic
  learners) and parents who want measurable checkpoints drift to worktext
  programs.

## Competitive notes for Homa

- Vocabulary to know: Instructor's Guide (IG), HBL (History/Bible/
  Literature), Level letters A-J and 100s (not grades), Read-Alouds vs
  Readers, 4-day vs 5-day, All-Subjects Package, Cores (the legacy name
  many veterans still use), BookShark (the secular twin). A mom saying
  "we're in Core D week 14" has told you her whole year; Homa should
  parse it.
- The IG is the best-in-market model of a paper planning surface: exact
  daily assignments plus just-in-time teaching notes. Homa's planner and
  materials should learn from its grid density and its
  discussion-question quality.
- The wedge is behind-ness: Sonlight's own users describe schedule guilt
  as the emotional failure mode. Homa can absorb the IG's rigidity
  (reflow the week, carry books forward, reassure) without replacing the
  curriculum.
- Book-tracking is a real unmet need: several concurrent titles per child
  with page-level positions is exactly the state Homa can hold better
  than a paper grid.
- Homa's materials rule for this family: never generate busywork. A
  Sonlight mom chose no-worksheets on purpose; offer narration prompts,
  discussion questions, and map or timeline artifacts instead.
- Record-keeping: checked-off IG grids are the family's records; when
  they leave Sonlight the history goes dark. Homa keeping a durable,
  curriculum-neutral record is the retention story.
