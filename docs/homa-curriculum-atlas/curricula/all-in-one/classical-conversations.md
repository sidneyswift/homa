# Classical Conversations

## Snapshot

Classical Conversations (CC) is the largest classical Christian homeschool
program in the US, and it is a community, not a box. Founded by Leigh Bortins
in 1997 in her North Carolina basement, it organizes weekly in-person
"community days" run by licensed local directors, wrapped around a
memorization-heavy classical curriculum that parents execute at home the other
four days. The company self-reports 45,000+ enrolled families across all 50
states and roughly 50 countries, with home-office figures citing 117,000 to
125,000 student seats (self-reported, treat as directional). Its three
programs ladder from Foundations (ages 4-12, memory work in seven subjects) up
through Essentials (ages 9-12, grammar and writing) to Challenge A-IV (ages
12-18, a full college-prep classical sequence). CC is simultaneously the
segment's strongest brand and its most controversial: families praise the
accountability and belonging, while whistleblowers and tax professionals have
publicly challenged its for-profit licensing model. Segment context:
`docs/homa-planning/icp-research/08-segments-curricula.md` §7 card 1.

## Reputation and rankings

- Cathy Duffy reviews CC materials (for example the Classical Acts and Facts
  history cards, praised as "beautiful, laminated cards") and treats CC as the
  reference point for community-based classical education. The program's
  distinctive value in reviewer verdicts is always the live community, not the
  materials themselves.
- Community sentiment is sharply bimodal. Advocates describe accountability,
  lifelong friendships, and children who can speak confidently in public.
  Critical threads (Reddit, ex-member blogs) recur on four themes: cost creep
  beyond the "stick in the sand" marketing, the expectation that paying
  parents also volunteer, memory work presented without explanation or
  context, and the burden of the Challenge workload (one detailed exit story
  describes teens working from 8 a.m. to midnight most days).
- The business-model controversy is real and documented. A 2021-2023 series
  of investigations by The Roys Report, with HomeschoolCPA (Carol Topp) and
  consultant Jamie Buckland, reported that local directors are licensees
  running for-profit businesses, that CC takes 100% of registration fees plus
  a percentage of Challenge tuition, that directors carry payroll, insurance,
  and lease liability, and that for-profit CC communities meeting in churches
  can jeopardize the church's property tax exemption (restrictions exist in
  most states; two churches were documented as assessed). Whistleblowers
  called the structure MLM-like. CC's home office publishes a Business
  Practice FAQ denying wrongdoing and framing directors as independent small
  business owners.
- Trajectory: still growing by its own counts and buoyed by the broader
  classical surge (see the Arcadia/Discovery Institute analysis cited in the
  repo's segment research), but the controversies plus post-2020 alternatives
  (classical co-ops, hybrid academies, Scholé Groups) have created a visible
  ex-CC diaspora.

## Pedagogy and method

CC operationalizes the trivium as three program stages: grammar (memorize),
dialectic (analyze), rhetoric (express). Its house doctrine, laid out in
Bortins' book The Core, is that rote memorization of a shared body of facts
is the foundation all later reasoning builds on. The distinctive mechanics:

- Memory work as the spine. Foundations students memorize weekly items in
  seven subjects (history, science, math, Latin, English grammar, geography,
  timeline), largely through songs, chants, and hand motions. Understanding
  is explicitly deferred; the grammar stage is for storage, not analysis.
  This is the single most polarizing design choice: fans call it pegs for
  later learning, leavers call it facts without context.
- Community as the accountability engine. The weekly meeting is the product.
  Tutors (trained parent volunteers, modestly paid by the director) model
  memory techniques for parents; the parent remains the teacher at home.
  CC's own framing: the program "enhances" the family's homeschool as a
  complementary service, not a school.
- Parents stay on site in Foundations. This is not drop-off enrichment; the
  parent watches the tutor teach, then repeats the material at home all week.
- At Challenge level the method shifts to Socratic seminar: six "strands"
  (logic, grammar, research, exposition, debate, reasoning) practiced through
  one full community day of discussion plus four independent home-study days.
- Assessment is informal until Challenge; the flagship motivator in
  Foundations is Memory Master, an honor for reciting the entire year's
  memory work, proofed four times (parent, second adult, tutor, director).

## Structure and scope

- Foundations (ages 4-12): 24 community-day weeks per year. Content rotates
  on a three-year cycle. Cycle 1 covers ancient world history and geography,
  biology and earth science, and Latin noun endings; Cycle 2 covers medieval
  to modern world history, ecology, astronomy, and physics, and Latin verb
  conjugations; Cycle 3 covers US history and geography, anatomy and
  chemistry, and Latin vocabulary with John 1:1-7 in Latin. Math facts and
  the 161-event timeline repeat every year. A child who starts young sees
  each cycle at least twice.
- Essentials (ages 9-12): an afternoon add-on to Foundations community day.
  English grammar taught through charts and sentence analysis, writing
  through the Institute for Excellence in Writing (IEW) method, plus math
  games. Runs on the same 24-week calendar.
- Challenge A through Challenge IV (ages 12-18): six one-year levels, each
  structured as six seminar strands on community day (6-7 hours) plus home
  study. Fixed anchor materials include Henle Latin (First Year Latin spans
  Challenge A through I) and Saxon math; literature moves from Newbery-level
  books (Challenge B, which also runs a mock trial) to American and British
  literature, formal debate, research papers, and a senior thesis. CC Plus
  offers college-credit pathways in the upper levels.
- Components: the Foundations Curriculum guide (one spiral-bound parent
  reference reused across all three cycles), cycle-specific audio, Classical
  Acts and Facts card sets, Trivium Tables, optional CC Connected
  subscription and CC Lite app for memory-work practice, and per-level
  Challenge guides plus a booklist of standard texts.

## A lesson up close

The unit of CC is the community day, not the lesson. A Foundations morning
runs roughly 9 a.m. to noon: an opening assembly (prayer, announcements, one
family presentation), then class groups of about eight children with their
tutor. The tutor runs five segments: new grammar (the week's memory items in
all seven subjects, introduced with songs and motions), a hands-on science
activity, a fine arts block (drawing, tin whistle, or master-artist study
depending on the six-week unit), student presentations (every child gives a
2-3 minute talk, 24 opportunities per year), and grammar review (games over
up to six prior weeks of material). Families lunch together; Essentials
students stay for the afternoon.

The at-home reality for the other four days: the parent drills the week's
memory work from the Foundations guide (a weekly grid of items per subject),
plays the cycle audio in the car, and layers her own math, phonics, and
reading curriculum on top, because Foundations is not a complete curriculum
and CC says so. What a completed week produces is recitation fluency, not a
paper trail: there are no worksheets to file, which some parents love and
compliance-state parents must work around. At Challenge level the home days
invert: the student works through Saxon lessons, Henle exercises, literature
reading, and writing assignments independently, and community day is where
the week's work gets discussed, debated, and presented.

## Materials and visual design

CC's materials are reference-and-recitation artifacts, not consumable student
workbooks. The Foundations guide is a parent manual: dense weekly grids, no
child-facing pages. The consumer-facing surfaces are laminated: timeline
cards and Acts and Facts card decks with fine-art reproductions on the front
and dense fact text on the back, tri-fold Trivium Tables for grammar and
Latin reference. Audio (songs for every memory item) is arguably the real
medium, since most drilling happens by singing. The aesthetic signals
seriousness and permanence: classical art, muted navy and parchment tones, no
cartoon mascots. Challenge materials look like a college syllabus: a thick
per-level guide plus a stack of third-party textbooks. The absence of a
child-facing workbook layer is a defining product fact: the family supplies
its own math and language arts, and the child's daily output is oral.

## Pricing

CC does not publish one national price; each licensed community sets tuition,
so every number is a range. Independent 2026 breakdowns converge on, as of
Jul 2026:

- Foundations: tuition roughly $400-750 per child, plus the Foundations
  guide (about $75-100, reusable three years), cycle audio ($30-60), tin
  whistle, and optional timeline cards (about $100) and CC Connected
  ($120/year). Realistic all-in: $700-1,100 per child per year.
- Essentials: additional tuition roughly $385-800, an Essentials guide
  (about $95), and IEW materials ($150-300).
- Challenge: tuition roughly $800-1,400 (some communities report totals to
  $2,400), plus $400-700 in books and supplies. Realistic all-in: $2,000-
  2,500 per student per year.
- Registration fees ($30-145 per student, non-refundable, paid to CC) sit on
  top; multi-child families commonly report $2,500-8,000 total per year.
  Tuition is typically non-refundable after the year begins.

Consumables economics are mild (the guides are reusable, cards resell well);
the resale market for CC materials is active, and one ex-member documented
recouping about $1,500 selling out. The dominant cost is tuition, which has
no resale.

## Why families choose it

- The community: weekly accountability, friendships for both mom and kids,
  and a peer group that shares the family's convictions.
- Someone else planned it: a fixed 24-week rhythm and a defined body of
  memory work removes planning anxiety without removing the parent's role.
- Public speaking: 24 presentations a year from age 4 is a visible,
  demonstrable outcome parents cite constantly.
- The classical brand promise: rigor, Latin, and a college-prep high school
  track (Challenge) with built-in Socratic discussion that is nearly
  impossible to replicate alone.
- One system for all ages: a family can run K through 12 inside one
  ecosystem, one day, one location.

## Why families leave it

- Cost realism: the "stick in the sand" simplicity message collides with
  tuition plus materials plus volunteer expectations, and families do the
  math (especially with three or more children).
- Memory work fatigue: children reciting facts they do not understand wears
  on parents who want depth; leavers often move to Memoria Press, a Charlotte
  Mason approach, or self-assembled classical stacks using The Well-Trained
  Mind.
- Challenge workload: the upper levels are heavy enough that families report
  losing hobbies, jobs, and margin; some shift to a la carte online providers
  (Veritas, Memoria Academy, Wilson Hill) for high school.
- Community variance: the director makes or breaks the experience, and a weak
  community year is a churn event.
- Business-model conscience: the for-profit licensing structure, church tax
  exposure, and director liability stories caused a visible, values-driven
  exodus around 2019-2023.
- Foundations is not complete: parents must still buy and run math and
  language arts, which surprises newcomers who thought they bought a
  curriculum.

## Competitive notes for Homa

- Vocabulary to know cold: cycle (1, 2, 3), community day, tutor vs director,
  Memory Master, strands, Challenge A/B/I-IV, Essentials charts, IEW, Henle,
  Foundations guide. A mom who says "we're in Cycle 2, week 14" has told Homa
  her entire history and science scope for the year; Homa should resolve that
  reference without asking follow-ups.
- The structural gap Homa fills: CC deliberately provides no child-facing
  practice layer and no records. Per-child practice materials that align with
  the family's memory-work week (same topics, no reproduced CC text), plus a
  paper trail for portfolio states, complement CC instead of competing with
  its community.
- Respect the at-home stack: a CC family always runs separate math and
  phonics; Homa should ask what those are rather than assume CC covers them.
- The landmine, per the segment card: never appear to do the child's
  thinking. Materials framed as recitation aids, drill helps, and parent
  tools are safe; anything smelling of AI-generated answers for the student
  is not. Do not generate or reproduce CC memory work text; point to CC's own
  audio and app for that.
- What CC proves about the market: classical families will pay four figures
  per child per year for accountability and belonging. The product lesson is
  that structure plus community is worth more than content; Homa's version of
  accountability (weekly rhythm, gentle check-ins) borrows the value without
  the tuition.
