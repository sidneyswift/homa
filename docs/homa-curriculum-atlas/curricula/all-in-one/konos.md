# KONOS Character Curriculum

## Snapshot

KONOS is where the homeschool unit study begins: written in 1984 by two
Texas homeschool mothers, Jessica Hulcy and Carole Thaxton, and billed
(credibly) as the first curriculum written for homeschoolers rather
than adapted from school materials. Its founding move still defines the
genre: organize learning not by subject, literature, or chronology but
by biblical character traits (Attentiveness, Obedience, Wisdom,
Resourcefulness), and pull science, history, Bible, art, music, and
literature through each trait with hands-on, discovery-style activities
taught to all of a family's K-8 children at once. Three massive
teacher-manual volumes carry the elementary years, the History of the
World series extends the method to high school, and packaged In-A-Box
and In-A-Bag editions exist precisely because the original demands so
much of the mother. KONOS used "the entire library as a textbook," and
four decades of unit-study successors (Five in a Row through Gather
Round and Campfire) are versions of its idea with the labor
progressively removed.

## Reputation and rankings

- Cathy Duffy has reviewed KONOS across editions and treats it as the
  comprehensive unit study reference point, noting both the method's
  strength and the reason In-A-Box exists ("those who like the
  methodology... but feel overwhelmed with what it requires").
- Aggregated sentiment calls it the gold standard for hands-on unit
  studies and equally consistently names the cost: heavy parent
  preparation (library runs, supply gathering, planning), with burnout
  the standing risk; it is emphatically not open-and-go.
- Institutional honors: Hulcy received HSLDA's first Chris Klicka award
  at its 2010 national leadership conference, a founders-generation
  recognition; her 2009 near-fatal car wreck ended the heavy convention
  touring that once drove the brand.
- Trajectory: legacy. Still sold, still loved by kinesthetic-learning
  families and alumni, but mindshare has passed to modern unit studies;
  KONOS today is the name veterans cite as where the idea came from.

## Pedagogy and method

Character-first integration with discovery learning. Each unit takes
one trait for one to two months; the trait is studied in scripture and
then embodied across subjects. The famous Attentiveness example: study
the eye and ear (science, including dissecting a cow's eyeball), read
Helen Keller and Louis Braille biographies (reading, history), write
reports on blindness (health, writing), sketch and paint (art), sing
"Be careful little eyes" (music), practice attending to one another
(character). Method principles: children learn by doing (activities
over worksheets), all ages learn together at their own depth, the
parent is a "master teacher" orchestrating rather than lecturing, and
the library supplies the books. Math and phonics are deliberately not
covered; families run separate skill programs alongside. The KONOS
Compass provides K-8 grade-level checklists so a non-linear program
can still answer "are we covering everything," which is the earliest
version of the sufficiency-anxiety tooling this genre always ends up
needing.

## Structure and scope

- Volume I, II, III (K-8): each a 440-510+ page teacher manual holding
  about 2 to 2.5 years of units (Vol. I: 7 traits including
  Attentiveness, Obedience, Orderliness, Honor, Trust; Vols. II-III: 6
  traits each, including Wisdom, Courage, Cooperation, Honesty,
  Resourcefulness). Volumes are parallel, not sequential; families
  start anywhere.
- Timeline characters per volume (134-176 laminated-card figures) plus
  wall Century Lines: the signature KONOS artifact, placing every
  studied person on a physical timeline.
- KONOS Compass: overview plus K-8 skills checklists and planning
  sheets.
- In-A-Box: three 18-week single-trait semester kits (Attentiveness,
  Obedience, Orderliness) with daily lesson plans, resource books, and
  craft supplies included, KONOS's own answer to its prep burden, and
  more explicit in language arts than the original volumes.
- In-A-Bag: two 18-week culture units (Russia, Africa) in the same
  packaged spirit.
- History of the World (HOW), grades 9-12: four one-year volumes
  (Ancient, Medieval, Renaissance/Reformation/Revolution, American),
  integrating history, literature, composition, Bible, geography, and
  humanities for multiple credits per year, run as guided independent
  study with parent dialogue (the house acronym is RAID: rigorous
  research, activities, independent study, dialogue).

## A lesson up close

The mother preps the week from the volume: picks the trait unit's next
activities, requests library books, and gathers supplies (copper foil,
a cow's eyeball from the butcher, costume pieces). A KONOS morning is
two to three hours of shared work: Bible and trait discussion, a read
aloud, then the day's activity block, building a model ear, acting out
a Middle Ages scene in costume, mapping, or a nature walk, with each
child participating at their own level, older ones adding research or
writing. Timeline figures go on the wall as people are met. Math and
phonics run separately in short per-child blocks. Output is mostly
experience, conversation, and projects rather than workbook pages,
which delights retention-focused parents and terrifies record-keepers;
planning sheets and the Compass exist to translate the week back into
subject coverage.

## Materials and visual design

The volumes are dense, utilitarian teacher manuals: hundreds of pages
of activity text in plain typography, spiral or perfect bound, with
essentially no student-facing pages; the visual life of KONOS happens
in the home (timelines on the wall, costumes, models) rather than on
the page. Timeline characters are illustrated card figures. In-A-Box
ships as a sturdy carrying case with trade resource books and craft
materials. The aesthetic signals its 1980s origin: this is a tool for
a committed teacher-mother, not a lifestyle object, and it predates
(and visually contrasts with) the Instagram-era design language of
Gather Round or Campfire.

## Pricing

As of Jul 2026, from the publisher's product list: volumes $110 each
($148.95 as a combo with matching timeline characters; $190 with
century lines), Compass and starter bundles in the $160-460 range
depending on collection, In-A-Box kits $225 per 18-week unit
($75 curriculum only), In-A-Bag $99, HOW complete years $180 (I, II),
$99 (III, download), $160 (IV). The economics are strong for
committed families: one $110 volume teaches every K-8 child in the
house for over two years, making per-child-per-year cost among the
lowest of any paid program, offset by library dependence, craft-supply
spend, and the separate math and language arts purchases. Volumes are
non-consumable and resell steadily on the used market.

## Why families choose it

- The multi-age dream at its purest: one preparation teaches everyone,
  by design rather than adaptation.
- Retention through doing: the families it fits report kids who
  remember the eyeball dissection years later; kinesthetic learners
  who fail workbook programs thrive.
- Character formation as the organizing spine, not a supplement.
- Cheap per child per year, library-powered, reusable.

## Why families leave it

- The prep wall: planning, gathering, and orchestrating is a part-time
  job; burnout is the named killer, and In-A-Box only partially fixes
  it. Leavers go to open-and-go unit studies (Gather Round, Campfire,
  My Father's World) or structured boxes.
- Record anxiety: activity-based weeks resist gradebooks; portfolio
  states are manageable, checklist personalities are not.
- Skill-subject drift: with math and phonics always separate, the
  add-ons quietly become the curriculum and KONOS becomes the
  enrichment that gets skipped.
- Aging brand: sparse convention presence since 2009 and a dated web
  and materials experience against design-forward competitors.

## Competitive notes for Homa

- KONOS defined the unit-study labor problem every successor monetizes:
  the method sells itself, the preparation kills adoption. Homa can do
  for any topic what In-A-Box did for three traits (turn a theme into a
  planned, leveled, supplied week) without shipping a box.
- The Compass is the genre's original coverage-mapping tool: unit-study
  families have wanted "what did this actually cover, K-8" since 1984.
  Homa's progress and coverage surfaces answer the exact anxiety KONOS
  had to invent a companion volume for.
- The timeline wall is a materials-pattern lesson (like FIAR's story
  disks): ambient, physical, cumulative evidence of learning that
  children maintain themselves.
- Vocabulary: traits as unit names ("we're in Obedience"), Volumes
  I-III, the Compass, In-A-Box and In-A-Bag, HOW years I-IV, timeline
  characters. A KONOS family is veteran-coded, activity-first,
  worldview-committed, and running separate math and phonics that Homa
  should expect to find alongside.
