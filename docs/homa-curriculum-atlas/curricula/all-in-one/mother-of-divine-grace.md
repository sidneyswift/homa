# Mother of Divine Grace School (MODG)

## Snapshot

Mother of Divine Grace School is the classical wing of Catholic
homeschooling: a WASC-accredited distance school founded in 1995 by Laura
Berquist in Ojai, California, after her book Designing Your Own Classical
Curriculum (Ignatius Press) generated more implementation questions than a
book could answer. MODG serves over 6,300 students in 28 countries. The
product is unusual among enrollment schools: not a box of house-brand
textbooks but grade-by-grade syllabi that schedule carefully chosen
third-party books (Saxon math, Baltimore Catechism, TAN history texts,
Spalding's Writing Road to Reading, Berquist's own poetry anthology The
Harp and Laurel Wreath), wrapped in a consultation model where every
enrolled family works with an assigned educational consultant, often a
veteran MODG homeschool mother. Where Seton sells a registrar and Kolbe
sells flexibility, MODG sells a person plus a plan: classical method,
customized per child, with the school keeping records and issuing the
accredited diploma.

## Reputation and rankings

- Cathy Duffy reviews the K-8 syllabi warmly as the practical answer for
  parents who loved Berquist's book but needed day-by-day plans, comparing
  their format to Sonlight's instructor guides with classical memorization
  and Catholic religion in place of Protestant devotionals.
- Aggregated reviewer sentiment calls MODG the "gold standard for Catholic
  classical homeschooling," praising syllabus flexibility and the
  consultant relationship; the recurring warning, in the reviewers' and
  users' own words, is that doing everything in the syllabus is a recipe
  for burnout, and that families have left MODG over load that a syllabus
  adjustment would have fixed.
- Student reviews (Niche) are strongly positive on flexibility, the
  Catholic community, and discussion-based upper classes, with two honest
  critiques: the program is writing-heavy and light on STEM emphasis.
- Long-form user reviews add texture: Catholic All Year's widely shared
  "MODG hacks" post documents both deep loyalty across seven-plus years
  and the real-world adaptations large families make (combining kids onto
  one history syllabus, halving Saxon problem sets); Elizabeth Clare's
  review praises reusable texts and the four-day week for most subjects.
- Trajectory: stable to rising with the Catholic classical surge; MODG
  and Kolbe are the two names in that conversation, with Memoria Press
  the cross-denominational alternative (see `memoria-press.md`).

## Pedagogy and method

MODG implements Berquist's reading of the trivium: curriculum staged to
the child's development, aimed at "the tools of learning" rather than
early content coverage.

- Grammar stage (roughly K-6): memorization and observation. Poetry
  memorization from The Harp and Laurel Wreath runs through elementary,
  alongside catechism memory work, math facts, phonics, and nature study.
  Content subjects stay story-shaped: saints' lives, Greek myths,
  historical fiction.
- Logic stage (roughly 7-9): analysis. Formal grammar assumes a central
  place, editing exercises begin, history moves to source-based study
  with essay questions, and Latin grammar formalizes.
- Rhetoric stage (10-12): expression and judgment. Discussion-based
  humanities with primary texts, persuasive and interpretive writing, and
  senior-year philosophy. Upper-division courses can be taken as
  teacher-led online seminars.
- Composition grows out of content: through most levels students write
  about history, religion, and literature rather than doing a separate
  writing program (Cathy Duffy flags this as a distinctive).
- Parent role: teacher and discussion partner from a scripted daily
  syllabus. The consultant, not a curriculum manual alone, is the safety
  net: consultants help set the year's plan, adjust course selections to
  the child, and coach the mother through implementation. MODG's
  consultant culture describes the job as "more of a ministry than a way
  to make money."

## Structure and scope

- Levels: PreK through 12. One syllabus per grade in K-8 (plus teacher
  planners); high school runs subject syllabi (literature and history by
  period, Latin, sciences, religion, philosophy) assembled into a
  transcript plan with the consultant.
- The scheduled books are deliberately mixed-provenance: Abeka arithmetic
  in grades 1-3 (see `abeka.md`), Saxon Math from grade 4 upward (see
  `../math/saxon-math.md`), the St. Joseph Baltimore Catechism with
  Gospel study questions for religion, TAN Books history spines (The Old
  World and America), historical fiction novels like God King, The
  Hittite Warrior, and Mara, Daughter of the Nile (a shelf shared with
  other classical programs), Writing Road to Reading for spelling,
  Voyages in English or Easy Grammar for grammar, Wordly Wise
  vocabulary, English from the Roots Up, TOPS science kits or Exploring
  the Building Blocks of Science, Penguin historical atlases for map
  work, and Latin.
- Enrollment structure (the Teacher Review Program is the foundation):
  educational consultant, record keeping, quarterly assessment rhythm,
  college counseling, and the WASC-accredited transcript and diploma.
  Optional layers: Learning Support, Teacher Services (small online
  classes with grading, tutoring, and class resources), and a Special
  Services program for learning differences with planning consultations.
- Syllabi without enrollment: MODG sells its syllabi to anyone ($30-35
  per K-8 grade, $20-30 per high school subject, teacher planners $25),
  making it the rare enrollment school whose plan can be bought a la
  carte and run independently.
- Pace: most subjects are scheduled four days per week, math five; the
  syllabus covers a standard school year.

## A lesson up close

The parent's document is the grade syllabus: a spiral or PDF book that
opens with Berquist's rationale for the year, then lays out day-by-day
plans per subject. MODG's public sixth grade description shows the mix a
day draws from: a Baltimore Catechism lesson with scripture study
questions from Mark or Luke, a Saxon Math 76 lesson (warm-up drill, new
increment, mixed problem set), grammar in Voyages in English or Easy
Grammar plus an Editor in Chief editing exercise, spelling from the
family's Writing Road to Reading notebook, a poetry selection from The
Harp and Laurel Wreath being polished for recitation, history reading in
The Old World and America with discussion or a written narration, map
work in a Penguin historical atlas, and Latin. The parent teaches math
and grammar directly, listens to recitation, reads or assigns the history
pages, and leads the discussion questions; an older student runs more of
the checklist alone.

What lands on paper is modest by design in the grammar stage: math sets,
copywork and spelling notebook pages, short compositions tied to history
or religion, and memory work performed aloud rather than written. Family
practice (documented in the Catholic All Year review) is to run siblings
on one content syllabus while keeping math and skills individual, and to
trim Saxon problem sets when the scheduled 45 minutes stretches toward
two hours. Quarterly, the family reports progress and samples per the
enrollment rhythm, and the consultant call is where pacing problems get
fixed: the institutional answer to the burnout pattern the community
warns about.

## Materials and visual design

MODG itself prints only syllabi, planners, and a few house texts, so the
family bookshelf is a mosaic of publishers: Saxon hardbacks, TAN's dense
old-style history texts, historical-fiction paperbacks with painted
covers, Berquist's Harp and Laurel Wreath anthology, thin TOPS kit
booklets. The syllabi themselves are utilitarian: typed daily tables and
teacher notes, no illustration program. The aesthetic signal is a
libraried home rather than a branded box: non-consumable spines that
serve every sibling (a point users praise), with consumables limited to
math workbooks in the early grades and notebooks. Enrolled families get
online syllabi in the family account; paper copies are optional.

## Pricing

As of Jul 2026, per MODG's published 2026-27 pricing breakdown (the
pricing page is the live truth):

- Family tuition for the Teacher Review Program, set by the highest
  enrolled grade and covering all enrolled children in the family: PreK
  $368, K-2 $474, grades 3-5 $828, grades 6-7 $1,160, grades 8-12 $1,678.
  New families pay $100 more in year one.
- Registration: $100 per family ($150 on or after July 1). Transcript
  evaluation for new 10th-12th graders: $50 per completed outside year.
- Special Services: no fee PreK-5, $35 for grades 6-12 at the basic
  level; planning consultations $150, additional consultations $100.
- Teacher Services (teacher-led group classes, one-on-one support) are
  priced per course on top of tuition.
- Books are extra and family-sourced; MODG's bookstore sells the
  scheduled texts (enrolled families get a discount), and most spines are
  reusable across siblings.
- Syllabi only, no enrollment: $30-35 per grade level.

The family-based tuition is the notable economics: one fee covers all
children, so per-student cost falls fast for large families, the segment
MODG explicitly serves. A single high schooler is a $1,700-plus
commitment before books; a five-child family with an eighth grader at the
top pays the same $1,678.

## Why families choose it

- Classical with a Catholic spine: the trivium implemented by a founder
  whose book many of them already own, with the faith integrated rather
  than appended.
- The consultant: a named, experienced person who customizes the plan,
  answers the phone, and coaches through hard seasons. This is the moat
  no book or box replicates.
- Accreditation without house textbooks: WASC transcripts and a diploma
  while keeping real books and the freedom to substitute.
- Family economics: one tuition for all the kids, reusable spines, and
  syllabi purchasable outright for the enrollment-shy.
- A high school that ends in discussion seminars, with college
  counseling included in enrollment.

## Why families leave it

- Load and writing-heaviness: the same rigor that attracts wears down
  families who execute the syllabus literally; leavers cite burnout that
  veterans say adjustment would have prevented.
- STEM thinness: families wanting engineering-flavored science or
  accelerated math tracks supplement or exit toward providers with
  taught math and lab sciences.
- Parent intensity: MODG assumes a teaching, discussing, recitation-
  hearing parent; mothers needing self-running programs drift to worktext
  systems (Seton, CHC workbooks) or online course platforms (Homeschool
  Connections, Kolbe online).
- Dry texts for a particular child: TAN-era history spines and Saxon's
  no-frills pages lose kids who need color or narrative-modern design.
- Consultant variance: the model depends on the assigned consultant;
  community threads note the experience varies with the match.

## Competitive notes for Homa

- Vocabulary to know: syllabus (the grade plan), consultant, Teacher
  Review Program, Teacher Services, Learning Support, The Harp and Laurel
  Wreath, Writing Road to Reading spelling notebooks, stages (grammar,
  logic/dialectic, rhetoric). "We're MODG, third and fifth grade, one
  history" describes a family running combined content with individual
  skills, and the consultant is already in the loop.
- The consultant is the product lesson: MODG proves Catholic classical
  families will pay four figures for personalized, judgment-bearing help
  wrapped around a plan. Homa's assistance sits in the same seat between
  consultations: pacing repair, sibling orchestration, record assembly.
  Complement the consultant, never impersonate one.
- Multi-child single-topic orchestration is native here (one syllabus,
  several kids, per-child output expectations), the same shape Gather
  Round and Tapestry of Grace prove elsewhere; Homa's materials should
  offer per-child difficulty on one content spine for this family.
- Materials fit: practice for a MODG family should be plain, serious, and
  tied to their scheduled books (drill scaffolds for Saxon increments,
  recitation trackers, map-work blanks), never re-generated content that
  displaces the child's own narration, composition, or memory work: the
  classical landmine documented in
  `../../../homa-planning/icp-research/08-segments-curricula.md` §7 and
  the classical comparison (`../../comparisons/classical-charlotte-mason.md`).
- Cross-links: MODG shares its novel shelf and classical culture with
  Memoria Press (`memoria-press.md`), schedules Saxon
  (`../math/saxon-math.md`) and Abeka arithmetic (`abeka.md`), and its
  families are adjacent buyers of Story of the World as an audio
  supplement (`../history-geography/story-of-the-world.md`, documented in
  user reviews rather than the syllabi).
