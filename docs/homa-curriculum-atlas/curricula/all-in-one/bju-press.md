# BJU Press Homeschool

## Snapshot

BJU Press is the other legacy giant of Christian textbook publishing: a
complete PK-12 curriculum from Bob Jones University in Greenville, South
Carolina, producing K-12 materials for Christian schools and homeschools
for over 50 years. By the 1980s BJU Press and Abeka were the two dominant
Christian educational publishers in America, and the two are still
compared in nearly every Christian homeschool forum thread. BJU's
differentiation inside the same school-at-home category is pedagogical
tone: more critical thinking prompts, more conceptual explanation, and
warmer, more modern page design, versus Abeka's drill-and-memorize
tradition. Its second pillar is video: recorded courses taught by studio
teachers who speak directly to the student, delivered through the
Homeschool Hub platform, with an optional accredited record-keeping
academy on top. It is the premium-priced option among the traditional
boxes.

## Reputation and rankings

- Cathy Duffy characterizes BJU Press as conservative Protestant and
  educationally balanced, with strong teacher's editions that are also the
  program's known trap: homeschoolers who skip the teacher's editions
  "eliminate essential parts of many courses," and getting through their
  volume overwhelms families teaching several grades. She calls the video
  courses "a huge help because they do the teaching for us."
- Community sentiment is broadly respectful even from non-users: BJU is
  seen as the rigorous, college-prep Christian option. Praise clusters on
  the direct-to-student video teachers (one long-term reviewer describes
  them as feeling like a warm teacher talking one-on-one, in contrast to
  Abeka's filmed classroom) and the colorful worktexts. Complaints cluster
  on price, tests that feel harder than the taught material, screen-time
  volume in the video track, and the structured pace overwhelming relaxed
  families.
- The classic switching thread, "Abeka vs BJU," resolves consistently:
  Abeka for maximum structure and drill, BJU for analysis, discussion, and
  slightly less rote work. Families switching from Abeka to BJU cite
  writing instruction integrated with grammar and less repetition.
- Trajectory: stable. Like Abeka it is an institution with school-market
  distribution behind it, priced at the top of the market while the growth
  energy sits with cheaper, gentler programs.

## Pedagogy and method

- Traditional textbook instruction with an explicit critical-thinking and
  worldview-shaping layer: student texts pose discussion and analysis
  questions, and teacher's editions coach the parent on biblical worldview
  framing per lesson.
- Neither strictly mastery nor spiral: units build conceptually with
  review built in; reviewers place it between Abeka's drill spiral and
  mastery programs.
- The teacher's edition is the operating system in the print track:
  reduced student pages, scripted teaching moves, vocabulary flagged,
  activities, and answers. BJU has begun publishing simplified Teacher
  Edition Companions for busy homeschool parents.
- The video track moves instruction to recorded teachers: lessons walk the
  student through the material (not reading the textbook aloud), direct
  when to pause, read pages, or complete activity book pages. Parent role
  drops to scheduling, supervision, and grading (online assessments
  auto-grade for grades 5-12).
- Assessment is formal: reviews, activity manuals, tests; the Academy of
  Home Education layer adds official transcripts and a diploma.
- Worldview: conservative evangelical, young-earth creationist science,
  Bible integrated in every subject, slightly more academic in register
  than Abeka's devotional tone.

## Structure and scope

- Grades K3-12, all core subjects: English (writing and grammar), reading
  and literature, math, science, Heritage Studies (BJU's social studies
  brand), Bible, plus electives in upper grades.
- Print packaging: complete grade-level textbook kits (student texts,
  activity manuals, teacher's editions, tests, keys), subject kits, or
  individual pieces. Fourth-edition elementary texts are colorful
  softbound volumes (for example Science 1 at 272 pages).
- Video packaging: full-grade video kits (streaming with books, streaming
  only, or DVD) and single-subject video kits. Streaming access runs 16-20
  months from ship date through the Homeschool Hub, which bundles video
  lessons, eTextbooks, scheduling, and a gradebook; a refreshed Hub is
  marketed for summer 2026.
- Accreditation: the video courses themselves are not a school; families
  wanting accreditation enroll in the Academy of Home Education, which
  rides on BJU Press Distance Learning and issues report cards,
  transcripts, and an accredited diploma for an added annual fee.
- The old Distance Learning Online platform is discontinued; Homeschool
  Hub is the current delivery system (relevant when families mention
  "DLO").

## A lesson up close

An elementary video-track day:

- The parent checks the Homeschool Hub schedule, which lays out each
  subject's video lesson and assignments for the day; the calendar
  adjusts when life happens.
- The child watches the day's lesson per subject: a studio teacher speaks
  directly to camera, uses props and visual aids, and pauses the student
  to read a textbook passage or complete a workbook section before
  resuming. Elementary lessons run roughly 15-30 minutes per subject.
- Written work happens in the consumable activity manual or worktext:
  full-color pages mixing comprehension items, diagrams to label, short
  writing, and review sections keyed to the taught lesson.
- The parent grades from keys, or the student takes online assessments
  (grades 5-12) that grade themselves into the Hub gradebook.

In the print track the parent replaces the video teacher: the teacher's
edition shows the student page alongside teaching notes, vocabulary, and
worldview prompts, and the parent leads the discussion the TE scripts.
BJU days are full but less drill-heavy than Abeka's; the workload
complaint is more often "the schedule is relentless" than "the pages are
endless."

## Materials and visual design

- Student texts and worktexts are full color, photo-rich, and visually
  contemporary, closer to a modern classroom textbook than to Abeka's
  vintage Americana or TGTB's fine-art softness. Colorful maps, charts,
  diagrams, and STEM activity spreads.
- Teacher's editions are large spiral or bound volumes with reduced
  student pages; the physical footprint of a full grade (TEs plus texts
  plus manuals) is substantial and heavy on the shelf.
- The aesthetic signals professional school quality: this is the box for
  the family that wants home education to look and feel like an excellent
  private school.

## Pricing

All prices as of Jul 2026 unless noted.

- Full-grade video: about $1,149/year with printed books, or $979 online
  only with eTextbooks; K5 video kit about $589; single-subject video kits
  from about $339 ([reviewer cost breakdown](https://lovehomeschool.com/curriculum/bju-press)).
  A seasonal $200-off promotion on video grade kits runs Feb 1 to May 31.
- Print: complete grade-level textbook kits list around $1,300-1,450 at
  retail with common discounting to $845-1,010
  ([Christianbook kit listings](https://www.christianbook.com/page/homeschool/bju-press));
  review aggregators summarize the kit range as $600-1,500. Individual
  student worktexts run about $27-52 and teacher's editions from about $81.
  The repo's Jul 2026 market table records kits at $700-1,500 and video
  around $970-1,100 ([segments research](../../../homa-planning/icp-research/08-segments-curricula.md) §4).
- Academy of Home Education accreditation adds annual administrative fees
  on top of curriculum (a few hundred dollars per student; exact fees on
  the AHE pages).
- Consumables economics: activity manuals and worktexts are consumable per
  child; hardback texts and teacher's editions reuse well, and BJU texts
  stay in print long enough to have a healthy resale market. Video access
  expires (16-20 months), so the video spend does not resell at all.

## Why families choose it

- Rigor with warmth: college-prep reputation, critical thinking framing,
  and video teachers kids genuinely like.
- The teaching is done for you: the video track is the closest thing to
  hiring a Christian private school faculty into the living room.
- Paper trail: accredited diploma and transcripts through the Academy of
  Home Education, valued for college admissions and athletics eligibility.
- Worldview alignment with slightly less drill and slightly more "why"
  than Abeka.
- The Hub consolidates scheduling, video, and grades in one place.

## Why families leave it

- Price: the most expensive mainstream box, and it compounds per child
  since video subscriptions and consumables cannot be shared or resold.
- Screen and schedule fatigue: a full video grade means hours of video
  daily, and the structured calendar suffocates relaxed or interest-led
  families.
- Difficulty spikes: tests harder than expected and heavy TE apparatus
  push some families to lighter programs.
- The common pattern reviewers note: families keep BJU selectively (high
  school science, accredited courses) and assemble the rest elsewhere,
  which is churn by unbundling.
- Destinations: Abeka (more structure), CLE or LIFEPAC (cheaper,
  independent), TGTB or Masterbooks (gentler, cheaper), secular online
  programs for the record-keeping without the worldview.

## Competitive notes for Homa

- Vocabulary to know: Homeschool Hub, Distance Learning (legacy DLO),
  video kits vs textbook kits, Teacher's Edition and Teacher Edition
  Companion, Heritage Studies (means social studies), Academy of Home
  Education (the accredited layer). Ask video or print first; it defines
  the mom's day.
- BJU's Hub is the closest thing in the traditional market to Homa's
  surface area (scheduler, planner, gradebook, lesson delivery). Watch it:
  it is a legacy publisher building software. Its weakness is that it only
  works inside BJU's own catalog; Homa serves the eclectic stack.
- Materials lesson: BJU's teacher's editions model the coaching layer
  parents actually need (what to say, what misconception to catch). Homa's
  generated materials can carry a compact parent-coaching note in the same
  spirit without the 400-page TE.
- Gap Homa fills: the unbundlers. Families keeping one or two BJU
  subjects need someone to plan and reconcile the rest of the week around
  the Hub's fixed schedule, and to keep records that outlive the 16-20
  month video window.
- For accreditation-motivated families, Homa should know AHE exists and
  never overclaim: Homa's records are evidence, not an accredited
  transcript.
