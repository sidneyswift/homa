# International homeschool programs: comparison and market notes

Wave 2b synthesis. The atlas's first 129 profiles are US-centric; this
doc covers the international (anglosphere) homeschool market through
seven new profiles: three UK distance and online providers, one UK boxed
curriculum, two Australian all-in-ones, and one UK resource platform.
Canada gets ecosystem coverage here rather than a profile, and the
reason is itself a finding (see the Canada section). Prices are as of
Jul 2026 and dated in each profile; USD figures are rough conversions.

International coverage matters twice over: as expansion context for a
future non-US Homa, and because a meaningful slice of these products is
already in US families' stacks today.

## How the anglosphere structures home education differently

The US model, the one Homa is built inside, makes the parent the school:
state-by-state rules, parent-kept portfolios and transcripts, a
parent-issued diploma at the end. None of the three markets in this wave
works that way, and the differences are structural, not cosmetic.

The UK runs on exams, not records. Home education in England requires no
registration of a curriculum, no annual portfolio, and no state review
of content; the legal duty is simply to provide a suitable education,
with local authorities able to make informal enquiries. (Oversight is
tightening: local-authority data collection became mandatory in autumn
2024, and a statutory children-not-in-school register has been moving
through Parliament in the Children's Wellbeing and Schools Bill.) The
DfE counted 126,000 children in elective home education on census day in
autumn 2025 and 175,900 at some point during 2024/25, up steeply year
over year. Because there is no transcript concept, qualifications come
from the exam-board system: at roughly 16 a student sits GCSEs or
IGCSEs, at 18 A levels, as a private candidate at an exam centre, and
those certificates are the entire academic record a university or
employer will ever ask for. Everything in the UK provider market is
shaped by that terminal exam: Wolsey Hall's courses target syllabus
codes, Oxford Home Schooling publishes exam-centre cost guidance, and
"which board, which syllabus, which centre" is the UK equivalent of the
US "which state, which option" conversation. Years before exam age are
nearly unregulated, which is why the under-14 market is served by
alignment-flavored products (Structured Home Learning's National
Curriculum boxes, Twinkl's key-stage-coded printables) that families buy
for confidence and school re-entry insurance, not compliance.

Australia runs on registration, forward plans, and renewal. Every state
requires home educators to register, submit an education plan mapped to
the Australian Curriculum (ACARA) or the state syllabus, and face
periodic renewal with evidence of learning; the strictness varies by
state (NSW under NESA, then the Department from May 2025, is the
canonical strict case). Registrations are booming: NSW more than doubled
from 5,906 in 2019 to 12,762 in 2024, Queensland hit about 11,800 by
August 2025 with triple-digit growth rates, and the national count is
around 45,000. The NSW Audit Office found the system buckling, with
registration waits over ten weeks while children legally cannot leave
school. The market consequence is unique to Australia: compliance is a
paid product feature. Euka sells a 199 AUD Government Registration
Service and 249 AUD Premium Reporting; My Homeschool's core value is
Charlotte Mason practice pre-mapped to ACARA and NESA outcome codes with
editable plan templates. No US provider sells state paperwork this
explicitly, because no US state demands a forward plan the way
Australian states do.

Canada runs on provincial funding that converts homeschoolers into
enrolled students. The signature structure is British Columbia's
distributed learning (now "online learning") system: a family can
register as Section 12/13 homeschoolers with near-zero oversight and
near-zero support, or enrol in a public or independent online learning
school, in which case the child is legally a school student, the school
receives roughly 7,200 CAD per full-time student in provincial funding,
a certified teacher supervises, the program follows the BC curriculum
toward the Dogwood Diploma, and the family typically gets about 600 CAD
per year in a Student Learning Fund for approved expenses. The
incentives all point toward enrolment, and the big BC players (Heritage
Christian Online School, SelfDesign with about 2,000 learners, EBUS
Academy) are schools, not purchasable curricula. Alberta funds the
middle path: supervised home education under an associate board pays the
parent share of about 900 CAD per student per year (2025/26) as expense
reimbursement, while Ontario requires only notification and funds
nothing. This is why wave 2b profiles no Canadian product: no single
curriculum product dominates nationally, because in the funded provinces
the "provider" is a school that money flows through, and families spend
their funding on the same US and international curricula this atlas
already profiles. The BC and Alberta structures are the coverage, and
they live in this doc, as planned.

## What this means for a US-built assistant expanding later

The records engine cannot be translated; it has to be re-founded per
jurisdiction. The artifacts differ in kind, not in format:

- US: retrospective evidence. Portfolios, attendance, transcripts,
  parent-issued credentials. Homa's home turf.
- UK: almost nothing until exam season, then logistics. The valuable
  artifacts are a syllabus-coded study plan, mock results, exam-centre
  bookings, entry deadlines, and NEA component tracking. A "transcript
  builder" is meaningless; an exam-season project manager (which centre,
  booked when, which syllabus variant, what it will cost, board fee plus
  centre admin) is the product. Progress tracking that speaks syllabus
  codes (0620, not "chemistry") would slot straight into how UK families
  already think.
- Australia: prospective plans plus renewal evidence. The artifact is a
  forward-looking education plan mapped to ACARA or state outcome codes,
  then samples of work assembled at renewal. Homa's observation-to-
  evidence pipeline is nearly perfectly shaped for this, but it must
  emit outcome-coded plans in each state's expected structure, and the
  ten-week NSW registration queue means onboarding urgency is real.
- Canada: either the school owns the records (enrolled online learners),
  leaving Homa the family-side role of keeping a portable record the
  family owns when they switch schools or provinces, or compliance is
  receipts (Alberta's reimbursement paperwork), which is expense
  tracking, not portfolio building.

Two structural constants survive translation and are worth building on.
First, every market's providers monetise the compliance gap (Euka's
199-349 AUD add-ons, My Homeschool's plan templates, Wolsey Hall's SPM
schedule-keeping), which prices parents' willingness to pay for exactly
what Homa automates. Second, the churn shape is universal: relief entry
into a structured provider, boredom or busywork exit, and the family
keeps nothing when they leave. A family-owned, provider-independent
child record is the same unserved pain on three continents.

## Which international products US families actually buy

- Twinkl is the big one: a US-localised site, US-standards coding, and
  home educator memberships make it the subscription-model TPT
  alternative for US homeschool families. It competes for the exact job
  of Homa's materials studio (see the profile's competitive notes).
- Cambridge-track programs, led by Wolsey Hall Oxford, serve a small but
  distinct US segment: expat and military families keeping a portable
  credential (Wolsey Hall markets a US page and military-family stories),
  and rigor-shoppers who want externally examined qualifications instead
  of a parent transcript. US universities accept IGCSEs and A levels as
  evidence, and A levels can earn transfer credit, which makes "IGCSE
  instead of transcript" a live, if niche, US strategy.
- My Homeschool explicitly markets to US families (Cathy Duffy reviewed
  it; it offers a 50% CTC Math discount for US members), riding the
  Charlotte Mason revival.
- The reverse flow is bigger and older: Australian and UK families have
  long imported US curricula (Sonlight's missionary-family history, Math
  U See's Australian distributor, TGTB's international shipping), and
  Canadian funded families spend their allowances on US products. An
  atlas backlog note: Australian-origin products with real US mindshare
  (Reading Eggs, Mathspace, Maths Australia's Math U See localisation)
  are still unprofiled and belong to a future wave.

## Gaps Homa could fill per market

- UK: nobody owns the exam-season workflow. Providers teach courses;
  exam centres administer papers; the family project-manages the gap
  (centre finding, private-candidate entry deadlines, NEA arrangements,
  wildly variable admin fees of 25-300 GBP per subject). An assistant
  that tracks syllabus-coded progress from age 11 and then runs
  exam-season logistics would be the first product in the stack the
  family, not the provider, owns. Under-14, the gap is confidence:
  "are we covering enough" against key stage expectations, currently
  served only by buying alignment-branded content.
- Australia: registration and renewal as software. Families pay Euka
  199-349 AUD per year for humans to draft plans and reports; My
  Homeschool families still hand-translate a Mason education into
  ACARA-referenced evidence. Generating the state-specific plan from a
  family's actual stack and observations, then assembling renewal
  evidence automatically, is a sharper wedge in Australia than anywhere
  in the US, because the requirement is universal and annual.
- Canada: the family-side record. In funded provinces the school owns
  the child's data, and switching schools or provinces (or dropping to
  registered-homeschooler status) means starting over. A portable record
  that survives those transitions, plus Alberta reimbursement paperwork
  automation, is the Homa-shaped opening; curriculum itself is already
  saturated with funded options.
- Everywhere: the same positioning rule the US category docs reached
  (see `online-digital.md`). These are all curriculum and school
  products competing for the child's hours. Homa serves the parent
  running the operation, whichever provider or none the family uses,
  and that category difference is what survives border crossings
  unchanged.

Structural note for the expansion decision: the US remains the deepest
and least-served market for a parent-side assistant precisely because
US home education is the least institutionally supported (no funding,
no provider default, maximum parental burden). The UK's exam cliff,
Australia's plan-and-renew cycle, and Canada's funded enrolment all
shift burden off the parent and onto institutions or providers, which
shrinks the assistant-shaped hole. International expansion is real but
sequenced: Australia's compliance pain looks most Homa-shaped, the UK's
exam logistics most bounded, Canada's family-side gap most niche.
