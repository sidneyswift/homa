# Online and digital programs: comparison and market notes

Category synthesis for `curricula/online-digital/`. Profiles are the
source of truth for every claim here; prices are as of Jul 2026 and dated
in each profile. This category holds Homa's closest price-anchor
competitors: when a parent evaluates Homa's subscription, these are the
numbers already in her head.

## Courseware churn patterns

Families cycle through these products on a predictable arc, and the
profiles' churn sections tell one combined story.

The entry point is relief. A family arrives at Time4Learning, Power
Homeschool, or Miacademy at a moment of overwhelm: new to homeschooling,
a parent working, a school exit mid-year. The product's promise is that
someone else planned, teaches, and grades. This matches the school-at-home
newcomer profile in
[08-segments-curricula](../../homa-planning/icp-research/08-segments-curricula.md)
§7: the boxed-or-courseware default is where newcomers start, and only
about 5% of established homeschoolers still identify with it, because
people leave.

The exit is rarely one dramatic failure. It accumulates: the child gets
bored of the lesson-quiz loop (the single most repeated complaint across
Time4Learning, Miacademy, and Power Homeschool reviews), the parent
notices completion without retention, a price change breaks the value
story (Power Homeschool's 2025 tripling, Time4Learning's move to a flat
$39.95), or a platform change breaks trust (Time4Learning's 2026
transition complaints). Then the family either hops to the adjacent
courseware (the Time4Learning-to-Miacademy lane is documented in
Trustpilot complaints, and Acellus is the named "more mature" alternative
in older-kid threads) or exits courseware entirely into an assembled
per-subject stack, which is the drift toward eclectic that the research
literature calls one of homeschooling's most consistent findings.

Two structural facts drive the cycling. First, monthly billing makes
leaving free: unlike a $700 box, a courseware subscription dies with one
click, so every February burnout window and every bored-kid week is an
exit opportunity. Second, these products age out underneath the child:
loved at K-2, tolerated at 3-5, abandoned by middle school when depth
doubts and college-prep anxiety arrive. The categorical exception is the
supplement tier (IXL, Khan, Prodigy), which families demote rather than
cancel: from daily requirement to occasional tool, then a lapse at
renewal.

What families keep from a courseware year matters to Homa: a dashboard
of grades they cannot take with them, a vague list of what was covered,
and no durable record of the child. The churn is normal; the data loss
is the unserved pain (see the lock-in findings in
[05-competitor-user-voice](../../homa-planning/icp-research/05-competitor-user-voice.md)
§7).

## Screen-time guilt as a market force

Screen-time guilt is not a review footnote in this category; it is a
recurring cancellation reason and a positioning axis. The clearest
churn stories are explicit about it: the Time4Learning parent who calls
quitting "one of the best homeschooling decisions I made" after watching
a first grader hate screen school, and the Easy Peasy family that left
because "I didn't like my kids sitting in front of the computer for 3
hours a day" when togetherness was the reason they homeschooled.

The market has already priced this guilt in. Every screen product now
ships a paper escape valve (Time4Learning's printable worksheets, Khan
Kids' worksheet packs, Easy Peasy's purchasable workbooks), and reviewer
best practice for all of them is the same coaching: follow each online
lesson with something on paper or in hands. Meanwhile the strongest
growth aesthetic in the wider market is the anti-screen signal itself,
The Good and the Beautiful's print softness and the Charlotte Mason
revival documented in
[08-segments-curricula](../../homa-planning/icp-research/08-segments-curricula.md)
§3.

The guilt has a specific texture worth respecting: parents do not
believe screens are evil, they believe screens are what school-at-home
failure looks like. A kid alone with a laptop all day is the image they
left public school Chromebook culture to escape. Products in this
category manage the guilt; they cannot resolve it, because the screen
is the product. This is a structural opening for anything whose output
is paper, activity, and parent-child time rather than child-screen
time.

## The curriculum-or-supplement confusion

Half the pain in this category comes from products being used one tier
above their design. The confusion has three recurring shapes:

- Practice tools mistaken for teachers. IXL explicitly does not teach,
  and Khan explicitly does not hold kids accountable, yet both get
  deployed as the whole math program. The result is the IXL tears
  cycle (drilled on concepts nobody taught) and the Khan stall
  (motion without progress). The honest framing lives in the fine
  print; the marketing does not fight the misuse.
- Courseware mistaken for a school. Time4Learning and Power Homeschool
  are curricula, not schools: no accreditation, no transcripts, the
  parent remains teacher of record. The Acellus/Acellus Academy/Power
  Homeschool naming makes this actively confusing, and families
  discover the difference at transcript time.
- Enrichment mistaken for coverage. Prodigy, ABCmouse, Night Zookeeper,
  and much of Outschool are supplements that feel substantial because
  the child spends real time in them. Families report "we did school
  today" weeks that were actually reward-loop time.

The market's own answer is a quiet disclaimer economy: nearly every
review of these products contains a sentence like "best used as a
supplement alongside a primary curriculum." Nobody upstream enforces
it. A trustworthy assistant that knows the difference, and will say
"Prodigy is a treat, not your math program" or "these are complements,
not substitutes" (the same honesty pattern as the Heggerty correction
in [08-segments-curricula](../../homa-planning/icp-research/08-segments-curricula.md)
§5), is doing a job no product in this table does.

## AI entrants and how they change the landscape

The AI layer arrived in this category from four directions at once:

- Incumbent add-ons. Khanmigo ($4/mo, Socratic, parent-visible) is the
  reference implementation, and its public lesson is engagement: Sal
  Khan conceded that for many students it was "a non-event," with a
  redesign slated for 2026-2027. Time4Learning's 2026 "went AI" grading
  transition drew backlash for opacity. Outschool's stated 2026
  direction is AI personalization plus teacher-side AI tooling. The
  incumbents prove distribution does not solve the child-facing
  engagement problem, and that bolting AI onto grading without
  explainability burns parent trust.
- AI-native tutors. Synthesis Tutor (voice-first Socratic math for
  ages 5-11, $29/mo or $119/yr family plans, SpaceX-school lineage) is
  the strongest new brand; homeschool sentiment is cautiously positive
  but firmly files it as a supplement, not a curriculum. Its family
  pricing undercuts most paid math programs, which pressures the
  supplement tier's economics.
- AI curricula. A crowded 2025-2026 cohort claims full AI-taught
  programs: LittleLit (16 subjects, Texas ESA vendor approval),
  Sparkli (ex-Google founders, ages 5-12), BloomWise, FlowHomeschool,
  Ashbrook (classical K-5), TheHomeschoolingCompany ($49/mo child-facing
  tutoring). Nearly all are pre-review-scale, and the discovery layer
  around them is polluted with vendor-authored comparison content (the
  noise assessment in
  [05-competitor-user-voice](../../homa-planning/icp-research/05-competitor-user-voice.md)
  §2 stands).
- AI admin tools. Adjacent rather than in-category (Homeschool Fox,
  BloomWise's compliance claims, Homeschool Managed), but they share
  Homa's buyer and shape her expectations about what AI help costs
  ($12-30/mo).

What actually changes: the floor rises and the trust bar rises with it.
Free ChatGPT already handles generic worksheet generation and planning
questions, so "AI-powered" is no longer a differentiator (roughly 20
tools claim the label). Parents' documented posture is pragmatic
adoption with content-trust reservations: curated beats generated
unless the generated thing is tied to a specific observed need
([05-competitor-user-voice](../../homa-planning/icp-research/05-competitor-user-voice.md)
§6). And the deepest pattern in the evidence favors the parent side of
the screen: child-facing AI struggles to get opened (Khanmigo), while
the celebrated AI stories are parent-serving automation, like the
unschooling co-op mother praising AI that turns activities into state
standards paperwork
([08-segments-curricula](../../homa-planning/icp-research/08-segments-curricula.md)
§7 card 3). ESA rails (LittleLit's Texas approval, Time4Learning's ESA
marketing) are becoming a distribution channel that can mint winners
independent of organic trust; watch it.

## How Homa positions against all of it

Homa is not another entry in the table, and the whole argument is in
that sentence. Every product above sells the child's attention to a
screen: courseware teaches her, practice apps drill her, marketplaces
class her, AI tutors chat with her. Homa sells the parent leverage over
her own operation: the stack she already assembled, the evidence of
what her child can do, the paperwork her state wants, the next
material her child needs, generated from what actually happened this
week. The repo's positioning rule stands and this category is its
proof: Homa is not courseware that teaches the child, it is an
assistant that serves the parent's own stack
([08-segments-curricula](../../homa-planning/icp-research/08-segments-curricula.md)
§4).

Concretely, against this category:

- Price talk: expect "Time4Learning is $39.95, why is Homa similar?"
  The answer is that Homa is not a replacement for her curriculum
  spend, it replaces the invisible spend: her Sunday planning hours,
  the abandoned tracker, the $10 TPT panic buys, the record she
  rebuilds every reporting season. Comparing Homa to courseware is
  comparing a sous-chef to a restaurant.
- Churn harvest: the courseware exit moment (bored kid, price shock,
  platform change) is Homa's acquisition moment, because what the
  family loses at cancellation is exactly what Homa preserves: the
  child's record, portable and theirs. Import-as-trust
  ([05-competitor-user-voice](../../homa-planning/icp-research/05-competitor-user-voice.md)
  §7) applies doubly to courseware refugees.
- Guilt alignment: Homa's materials output should stay paper-first
  and activity-first on purpose. In a category defined by screen
  guilt, "less screen, more evidence" is a moral position parents
  already hold, waiting for a product to agree with it.
- The honesty niche: nobody in this table will tell a parent that a
  product (including itself) is a supplement, not a curriculum. Homa
  can, per product, per child, because it does not compete for the
  child's hours. That honesty is cheap for Homa and unaffordable for
  everyone else here.
- The AI lesson: never test the child, never gate her behind
  engagement, never obscure how a grade or judgment was formed.
  Khanmigo's non-event, IXL's tears, and Time4Learning's opaque-AI
  backlash are the three cautionary tales; Homa's observational,
  parent-facing, provenance-visible architecture is the direct
  answer to all three.
