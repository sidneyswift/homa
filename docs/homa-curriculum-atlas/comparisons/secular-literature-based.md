# Secular and literature-based multi-subject programs: comparison

Category notes for the assemble-it-yourself universe, the segment Homa's
ICP research names as the beachhead (secular-leaning eclectic families;
see `docs/homa-planning/icp-research/08-segments-curricula.md`).
Profiles referenced here live in `../curricula/all-in-one/`. Snapshot
date 2026-07-13; re-verify prices before parent-facing use.

## The field at a glance

| Program | Model | Grades | Price band | Parent load | Standout trait |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| BookShark | Boxed literature curriculum (secular Sonlight sibling) | PK-10 | $$$$ | High | The full box experience without explicit Christian content |
| Oak Meadow | Waldorf-inspired coursebooks, optional accredited distance school | PK-12 | $$$$ | High | Accreditation and main-lesson-book gentleness in one brand |
| Moving Beyond the Page | Boxed interdisciplinary unit studies with online assessment | PK-8 (ages 4-14) | $$$$ | Medium | Gifted-leaning integration with built-in Learning Gates quizzes |
| Timberdoodle | Curated multi-publisher kits, Classic and Nonreligious editions | PK-12 | $$$$ | Medium | Curation as a product; scheduler turns a kit into checklists |
| Blossom and Root | Authored PDF guides, nature and STEAM | PK-6 | $$ | High | Gentle secular science and nature study with a strong aesthetic |
| Torchlight | Authored PDF guides, Socratic and Humanist | PK-5 (ages 4-13) | $ | High | Questioning Trees; the most explicitly secular-Humanist option |
| Build Your Library | Authored PDF schedules, secular Charlotte Mason | K-12 | $ | High | Full K-12 runway at $30-60 a level, by a SEA co-founder |
| Five in a Row | Picture-book unit study manuals | PK-6 (ages 2-12) | $ | High | Row-a-book five-day rotation; story disks on the world map |
| Wildwood Curriculum | Free web-published secular Charlotte Mason | 1-8 (Forms I-III) | free | High | Faithful PNEU-style CM, free, with inclusive booklists |
| Global Village School | Accredited distance school with values-forward guides | K-12 | $$$$ | Medium | WASC accreditation plus social justice identity |

Two price universes are visible even inside this one category, matching
the segment research: a boxed and institutional universe at $500-1,900
per year (BookShark, Oak Meadow, MBTP, Timberdoodle, GVS enrollment)
and an assembly universe at $0-200 (BYL, Torchlight, Blossom and Root,
FIAR, Wildwood) where the family's labor and the public library replace
the box.

## The secular-certification problem

Secular buyers cannot take "secular" on a label's word, because the
word is contested and the failure mode (hidden religious framing
discovered mid-year) is exactly what they left other channels to avoid.
The vetting behavior looks like this: check whether science teaches
evolution and climate change plainly (the bright-line test), check the
history spine's framing, check health and puberty materials for
inclusivity, and check who owns the company. BookShark is the canonical
case: it markets itself as faith neutral, its own FAQ says science
teaches neither evolution nor creationism, and strict secular reviewers
have documented residual Christian-influenced content inherited from
Sonlight, so SEA-adjacent circles treat it as "secular-ish, vet book by
book." Timberdoodle gets a parallel treatment: Christian-owned, with a
genuinely secular Nonreligious kit line that still gets audited
component by component.

The taxonomy distinction that decides purchases is secular (teaches
evolution and standard science) versus neutral (omits both religion and
contested science). Torchlight, Blossom and Root, Build Your Library,
Wildwood, MBTP, Oak Meadow, and GVS pass the strict secular test;
BookShark and Timberdoodle are neutral-to-mixed and are bought by
secular families with eyes open.

SEA Homeschoolers (Secular, Eclectic, Academic) functions as the
segment's certification authority in practice: a 100,000+ family
community with vetting norms, a store, and conferences (scale documented
in the segment research §2). Build Your Library's author co-founding SEA
is why BYL carries near-official trust, and "is it SEA-secular?" is the
question Homa should expect this segment to ask about any material,
including Homa's own.

## The PDF-plus-library-books economy

The assembly universe runs on a specific economic machine: the author
sells the plan (a $30-160 PDF), the public library supplies the content
(30-50 trade books a year), and the family supplies printing, binding,
and scheduling labor. Federal data has two-thirds of homeschool families
using the library as a curriculum source (segment research §2), and
every PDF publisher designs for it: BYL flags which books are spines to
buy versus borrow, Torchlight maintains Bookshop lists and coaches
buyers on the Library Extension add-on and interlibrary loan, FIAR
assumes library sourcing outright. License policing replaces consumable
sales as the revenue defense (Torchlight's copy-protected PDFs and
controlled resale rules are the sharpest example), and per-child
marginal cost collapses to printing, which is exactly why multi-kid
eclectic families concentrate here. The trade is money for labor: the
$45 guide costs a year of library holds, print runs, and substitutions
when a title is checked out. That labor is unpriced in the market today,
and it is the single most automatable thing in this category.

## Charter and ESA funds as a secular market driver

Public charter programs and ESA wallets are quietly reshaping this
category, because state money cannot buy sectarian materials in charter
contexts. That constraint manufactures demand for certified-nonreligious
product lines: Timberdoodle explicitly labels its Nonreligious kits
charter-school-friendly, BookShark markets itself as an approved vendor
with many homeschool charters and runs a state-by-state ESA explainer,
and Oak Meadow notes that enrollment or vouchers may be fundable
depending on state. The effect is twofold. First, the boxed universe's
price ceiling matters less when a charter allotment pays the bill,
which props up $900 boxes that many assembly families would never pay
cash for. Second, vendors converge on
neutral rather than strictly secular content, because neutral can be
sold in both the charter channel and the relaxed-religious channel at
once, which is exactly the posture strict secular buyers distrust. The
regulatory side lives in
`docs/homa-planning/icp-research/06-regulatory-esa-landscape.md`; the
atlas-level takeaway is that "can I buy it with charter funds?" is now a
standard question in this segment's purchase decisions, and eligibility
is a real acquisition wedge.

## Curation versus creation

The category splits by where the intellectual property lives:

- Creators author content: Blossom and Root, Torchlight, Build Your
  Library, FIAR, Wildwood, Oak Meadow, and MBTP write their own guides
  and lessons. Their moat is voice and trust; their ceiling is one
  author's output pace (Torchlight ships one level a year; Blossom and
  Root ends at level 6; Wildwood is volunteer-paced).
- Curators assemble other people's content: Timberdoodle is the pure
  case (kits of other publishers' products plus a scheduler), and
  BookShark is a hybrid (authors its guides and history-science
  packaging, outsources math to Math-U-See, Saxon, Singapore, or
  RightStart, and bundles Handwriting Without Tears and Spelling You
  See).

The deeper point for Homa: the eclectic family is herself a curator.
The defining behavior of the beachhead segment is per-child stack
assembly (79% of families use no single package; segment research §6),
which means every family in this category is doing unpaid Timberdoodle
work: choosing components, reconciling their schedules, and noticing
when one fails. Timberdoodle proves people pay real money for that
labor; the PDF creators prove people pay for taste and trust. Nobody
yet sells curation that continues after purchase.

## Switching paths

Recurring moves this category sees, from the profiles' churn sections:

- Into the category from Christian boxes: Sonlight and Abeka leavers
  land on BookShark first (same box shape, no Bible), then often
  discover the vetting debates and hop again to BYL or Torchlight.
- Box to PDF (cost and control): BookShark to Build Your Library is the
  classic downshift, keeping the literature-spine day while cutting the
  price by an order of magnitude and swapping in the library.
- PDF to box (labor): the reverse move happens when the printing,
  sourcing, and planning labor of the PDF economy overwhelms; those
  families buy BookShark or Timberdoodle "so it just arrives."
- Gentle to structured (academic anxiety): Oak Meadow, Blossom and
  Root, and FIAR families around grades 3-4 add or switch to systematic
  math and phonics, and gifted-leaning ones move to MBTP for rigor with
  integration.
- Structured to gentle (burnout): MBTP's writing load and BookShark's
  read-aloud grind push families toward Blossom and Root's lighter
  rhythm or pure eclectic assembly.
- Aging out: Blossom and Root ends near level 6, Torchlight near age
  13, FIAR's core near age 9-12; BYL and Oak Meadow are the two
  in-category landing zones that run through grade 12, and many
  families otherwise exit to assembled per-subject stacks in middle
  school.
- Free to paid and back: Wildwood serves as both the trial run before
  buying BYL and the refuge when budgets tighten.

Mid-year switching is normalized in this world (segment research §6),
so every one of these paths is also a February event, not just a
summer one.

## Gaps Homa can fill

This category is the beachhead, and its gaps map one-to-one onto Homa's
shape:

1. Stack assembly is unserved after purchase. Every vendor sells a
   starting configuration; none adapts it once real children collide
   with it. The family who runs BYL 3 plus Singapore plus a Torchlight
   science unit has no product that sees the whole stack. Homa's
   per-child planner and curriculum knowledge can be that layer, which
   is precisely the defining behavior of the segment.
2. The labor tax of the PDF economy: library sourcing, substitutions,
   print prep, and week-planning are hours of unpriced work per month.
   Automating even the planning and substitution halves of it is felt
   value at $29-49 a month.
3. The skills hole: BYL, Torchlight, Blossom and Root, FIAR, and
   Wildwood all omit math and systematic phonics on purpose. Per-child
   generated practice that matches the family's content spine (a
   counting page themed to this week's rowed book) is a printable no
   vendor in this category can make.
4. Evidence without betrayal: these families reject tests but still
   face state paperwork and their own "is this enough" anxiety. FIAR's
   story-disk map and Oak Meadow's learning assessment forms show the
   accepted shapes: ambient, beautiful, narrative. Homa's observational
   progress model fits where every box's quiz culture does not.
5. Secular-vetting answers: an honest, sourced "does this title or
   program carry religious framing" capability serves the segment's
   deepest trust ritual, and Homa can answer it per book where SEA
   threads answer it per brand.
6. Switching support: since mid-year hops between these programs are
   normal, the assistant that carries context across the hop (what the
   child already covered, what maps to what) converts the category's
   churn into Homa's retention, the same story as the school-at-home
   February drift in the segment research.
