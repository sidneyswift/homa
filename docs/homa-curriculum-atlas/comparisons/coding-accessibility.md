# Coding and accessibility: comparison and market notes

Wave 2b closes two backlog gaps in one cross-cutting doc, because they
share a structure: both aisles run on free or gated infrastructure that
no one orchestrates. Part 1 covers coding for kids
([Scratch](../curricula/electives/scratch.md),
[CodeCombat](../curricula/electives/codecombat.md),
[Tynker](../curricula/electives/tynker.md),
[CodaKid](../curricula/electives/codakid.md),
[Kodable](../curricula/electives/kodable.md), plus the typing duo
already profiled in
[typing-platforms.md](../curricula/electives/typing-platforms.md)).
Part 2 covers the audiobook and accessible-text ecosystem
([Learning Ally](../curricula/online-digital/learning-ally.md),
[Bookshare](../curricula/online-digital/bookshare.md), and the
commercial audiobook culture around Audible and Libby, which gets no
profile because it is a behavior, not a curriculum). Snapshot dated
2026-07-13; per-profile front matter is the source of truth for prices.

Unprofiled but load-bearing context: Code.org (the school-sector free
curriculum, common in co-op classes), Khan Academy's free programming
courses (profiled at
[khan-academy.md](../curricula/online-digital/khan-academy.md)), and
Roblox Studio and Minecraft themselves, which function as unsupervised
coding on-ramps in millions of homes.

### Free-first economics

This aisle's floor price is zero, and the zero is high quality: Scratch
is a donation-funded nonprofit with a billion projects created,
Code.org is philanthropy-funded, Khan's CS courses are free, Experience
CS (the Raspberry Pi Foundation's Scratch curriculum that replaced
Google's retired CS First in 2025) is free, and the typing duo is free
because schools pay. Paid consumer platforms therefore live in a
squeeze: they must beat free defaults on structure while competing for
the same screen hours as Roblox. The squeeze shows in the exits. BYJU'S
paid a reported $200 million for Tynker in 2021 and CodeHS bought it
out of bankruptcy for $2.2 million in 2025, a 99 percent write-down on
the thesis that families would pay content prices for kids coding.
What still sells is not content: it is packaging (Kodable's school-day
free tier converts to home subscriptions), franchise adjacency
(Minecraft modding at Tynker and CodaKid), and accountability
(CodaKid's $249-per-month tutoring, CodeCombat's live classes), which
is the same content-is-free, orchestration-sells pattern the electives
comparison already documented for music and typing
([languages-electives.md](languages-electives.md)). One more artifact
of the squeeze: lifetime deals everywhere (Tynker $468, Kodable
$199.99, CodeCombat's periodic $99 offers), which read as good
multi-kid math but price in the sector's own churn risk, as the Tynker
ownership story shows.

### The Scratch-to-Python cliff

The category has a shared failure point around ages 11 to 13: the move
from blocks to typed code. Block coding removes syntax as a failure
mode; typed code reintroduces it all at once, along with typing itself
(the quiet dependency the typing stub covers). Homeschool guidance
converges on readiness signals (multiple self-built projects, hitting
platform limits, age 11+) and on Python as the destination, but the
market's bridges are partial: CodeCombat types real code from day one
and still earns the community's standing "cliff of difficulty"
complaint when scaffolding thins mid-catalog; Tynker's ladder shows
block and text side by side; CodeCombat Junior now does the same for
K-5; CodeHS justified buying Tynker precisely as block-to-advanced
continuity. What makes the cliff structural in homeschools: no state
requires elementary CS, so when the transition frustrates, the subject
can simply stop, with no transcript consequence until a family wants a
high school CS credit years later. The cliff is where most homeschool
coding quietly dies, and no product owns the recovery moment.

### Parents cannot evaluate quality

Unlike math or phonics, where a mom can read the page and judge, most
homeschool parents have no way to evaluate coding instruction, and the
market knows it. The award circuit that disciplines other elective
categories barely exists here; Cathy Duffy coverage is thin; and the
comparison content parents find is substantially written by the
vendors themselves (CodaKid's blog dominates "best coding for kids"
searches, ranking its own product first). So parents buy on proxies:
"real languages" claims, school-adoption statistics (Kodable's
self-reported use in over half of US elementary schools), franchise
logos, and above all the child's visible enthusiasm. Enthusiasm is a
genuinely useful signal, but it selects for game layers over
instruction, and it cannot distinguish a child assembling solutions
from a child pattern-matching hints, a gap reviewers flag at both
Tynker and CodeCombat. The result is a market where the loudest
quality signal a parent gets is whether the subscription gets used,
which is retention, not learning.

### Productive screen time and the guilt loop

Coding is the only elective that converts screen-time guilt into
virtue. The pitch across this aisle, explicit or implied, is that an
hour in the platform is the redeemed version of the hour the child
would have spent in Roblox anyway; Tynker's Minecraft modding and
CodaKid's Roblox courses literally relocate the game obsession inside
a curriculum. This gives coding unusual skip-resistance for an
elective (the child asks for it, reversing the guilt-subjects dynamic
in [languages-electives.md](languages-electives.md)), but it also
creates a drift problem unique to the category: the same screen that
hosts the lesson hosts the temptation, and "doing Scratch" can mean
playing other kids' games, grinding CodeCombat gear, or watching
YouTube beside the editor. Parents sense this, cannot verify it (see
the evaluation gap above), and oscillate between pride and suspicion.
The products that thrive are the ones that give parents an artifact
that settles the question: a mod running in the child's own Minecraft
world, a game a sibling can play, a dashboard percentage. Artifacts,
not minutes, are how this category proves itself.

### Gaps Homa can fill (coding)

1. Structure around the free default. Scratch ships no sequence, no
   records, no assessment, on purpose. A weekly plan (one technique,
   one project), generated prompts tied to the child's interests, and
   a progress log turn the free tool into a course without competing
   with it. This is the highest-leverage move because it serves the
   majority who will never pay for a platform.
2. Own the cliff. Homa can see the blocks-to-text transition coming
   from age plus stack, normalize the difficulty spike, suggest the
   bridge (typing fluency first, a first Python project that remakes
   something the child already built), and catch the two-week stall
   that currently ends coding in silence.
3. Evidence and credit. Nothing in this aisle exports proof: Scratch
   has no records, CodeCombat's dashboard stays in CodeCombat, Tynker
   certificates are not transcript language. Logging sessions,
   attaching project links and screenshots, and emitting hours and
   course descriptions turns game time into the CS elective credit
   high school will eventually demand, across brands, which no vendor
   can do.
4. Be the evaluator parents cannot be. The atlas-level knowledge
   (what each platform actually teaches, where its cliff is, what the
   free alternative covers) answers "is this actually teaching him
   anything" honestly and neutrally, which vendor SEO content
   structurally will not.
5. Referee the drift respectfully. Homa can distinguish making from
   consuming by asking for the artifact (show me what you built this
   week) rather than surveilling minutes, matching the
   artifact-over-minutes rule the category itself proves.

## Part 2: audiobooks and accessible text

### Comparison table

| Service | What it is | Gate | Library | Narration | Price | Standout trait |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Learning Ally](../curricula/online-digital/learning-ally.md) | Nonprofit accommodation library (1948) | Certified print disability | ~80,000, textbook-heavy | Human, visuals described | $$ ($135/yr household) | The dyslexia ecosystem's audiobook layer, VOICEtext read-along |
| [Bookshare](../curricula/online-digital/bookshare.md) | Federally funded accessible ebook library (2002) | Certified print disability | 1.4M+, all formats | Synthetic TTS, braille, large print | free for US students | Free at federal scale; one text, many renderings |
| NLS BARD (not profiled) | Library of Congress braille and talking books | Certified print disability | Large (not independently sized here) | Human | free | The blindness-community backbone; certifies reciprocally |
| Libby (not profiled) | Public-library lending app (OverDrive) | Library card | Varies by library system | Human (commercial) | free | The homeschool default for family audiobooks; holds and waitlists |
| Audible (not profiled) | Amazon's commercial audiobook store | None | Commercial catalog | Human (commercial) | about $14.95/mo for 1 credit | Owned forever, no waitlists; the read-aloud culture's paid tier |

### Qualification is the defining line

The table's real axis is legal, not commercial. Learning Ally,
Bookshare, and NLS exist under the Chafee Amendment (17 U.S.C. § 121,
extended internationally by the Marrakesh framework), which lets
nonprofits distribute copyrighted works in accessible formats only to
people with certified print disabilities. That is why these services
gate membership behind documentation while Audible and Libby gate
nothing. The gate's practical shape matters for homeschoolers:
dyslexia qualifies, an IEP or 504 is explicitly not required
(Bookshare says so loudly, because school families assume otherwise),
certification can come from a broad professional list (teachers,
tutors, reading specialists, psychologists, SLPs, OTs, physicians),
the services accept each other's certifications, and the homeschooling
parent cannot self-certify, so one outside professional must touch the
process. That last clause quietly reproduces the pattern the
special-needs comparison documented: many of these families never buy
a formal diagnosis, running instead on free screeners and symptom
checklists, and the certification step is where that informality
collides with federal copyright law. Families stall there, on
paperwork for a free benefit they qualify for, which is a navigation
gap rather than a product gap.

### Ear reading as accommodation culture

Homeschooling already has a huge audiobook culture with no
accommodation framing at all: the Read-Aloud Revival wing treats audio
as family literature (morning basket, car schooling, Audible credits
spent on beloved narrators, Libby holds timed to the term), and in
that culture audio is simply how a busy family gets more books in. The
accommodation culture runs the same behavior with different stakes.
For a dyslexic child, audio is access: decoding is the impaired skill,
comprehension is intact, so "ear reading" (the term of art in dyslexia
advocacy) delivers grade-level vocabulary, knowledge, and story while
years of remediation proceed. The research consensus backs it,
mainstream outlets now say listening counts as reading, and experts
specifically recommend bimodal reading (eyes on highlighted text, ears
on narration, the exact mechanic of Learning Ally's VOICEtext and
Bookshare's karaoke highlighting) for dyslexic readers. But the
"does it really count" stigma persists (a reported 40-plus percent of
Americans consider audiobooks lesser reading), and these families
field the question from co-op teachers, grandparents, and their own 2
a.m. doubts. So the accommodation culture has trust rituals, the same
ones the special-needs comparison found across that whole segment: the
membership card and its gate function as proof the need is real, the
"reading with your ears is still reading" affirmation is repeated like
liturgy (Bookshare puts it on its library page), and the nonprofit,
endorsement-bearing institutions (a 1948 founding, a CASE endorsement,
a Department of Education award) carry the credibility that burned
buyers demand. Ear reading is simultaneously a research-backed
accommodation and an identity claim, and products that treat it as
merely a feature miss half of what the family is buying.

### How it interacts with the special-needs stack

Cross-link [special-needs-intervention.md](special-needs-intervention.md)
for the segment's full economy; the audiobook layer slots into it
precisely. The escalation ladder (gentle phonics, then AAR, then
Barton, then clinical) is a multi-year timeline, and audiobooks are
what keep the child's education whole during it: Barton explicitly
forbids running other reading or spelling programs alongside, but
audiobooks for content subjects are the sanctioned lane (the
[Barton profile](../curricula/language-arts/barton-reading-and-spelling.md)
names them the safe support), so Learning Ally or Bookshare beside
Barton is the standard severe-dyslexia stack. The same families carry
Libby and Audible for pleasure listening, which means a single child
may run three audio services with three different meanings (access,
school, joy). And the qualification gate interacts with the segment's
diagnosis-avoidance: the free screeners that stand in for evaluations
do not satisfy Chafee certification, so the audiobook layer is often
the first place an undiagnosed-by-choice family needs a professional's
signature, a small crisis of identity as much as logistics.

### Gaps Homa can fill (accessibility)

1. Qualification navigation. Know the map cold: who qualifies, that
   IEPs are not required, who can certify, that certifications
   reciprocate, that Bookshare is free for homeschooled students and
   Learning Ally waives fees for hardship. Walking a family through
   the stall point without diagnosing anyone (the lane-keeping rule)
   converts a stalled intention into a working accommodation.
2. Records where ear reading counts, structurally. Book logs and
   progress evidence should record format neutrally (read, listened,
   bimodal) and never rank it, extending the growth-only display law.
   For these families, a record that treats ear-read books as read
   books is not a feature, it is the product agreeing with their
   liturgy.
3. Materials that separate content from rendering. Bookshare's one
   text, many renderings is the pattern: Homa-generated materials
   should ship print, low-clutter, large-type, and audio-forward
   renderings of the same lesson so the ear-reading child and print
   siblings stay in one conversation, with shared reference points.
4. Stack-aware behavior. "We have Learning Ally" or "we have
   Bookshare" states an accommodation stack; Homa should infer the
   likely remediation program beside it, respect exclusivity rules,
   and route content-subject reading through the family's audio layer
   instead of suggesting print workarounds.
5. The same meta-gap as Part 1. Both halves of this doc are free or
   cheap infrastructure missing an orchestration layer: nobody
   structures Scratch, and nobody navigates Chafee. Homa's shape (the
   assistant above the stack) is the missing layer in both, and
   neither gap requires Homa to make content it would have to defend
   against a free default.
