## The worldview split

Science is the subject where worldview decides the purchase before pedagogy
gets a vote. The market segments into four postures, and families rarely
cross the outer boundaries:

1. Young-earth creationist: Apologia and Berean Builders. Creation is
   taught as science, evolution is argued against, and buyers actively want
   that integration. This is the largest single self-identified segment in
   Christian homeschooling, and Apologia owns its shelf. Within the
   segment, the differentiators are author loyalty (Jay Wile's Berean
   Builders vs post-sale Apologia), price, and format, not doctrine.
2. Christian but datable-claims-neutral: The Good and the Beautiful. God
   is credited as creator, but the age of the earth is deliberately left to
   parents, which lets one product serve young-earth and old-earth
   Christians at once. This posture trades depth of conviction for breadth
   of market, and it works: worldview-anxious buyers get safety without
   sectarian commitment.
3. Neutral: Real Science-4-Kids, Elemental Science, Noeo, Sassafras. No
   religious content, mainstream terminology, and contested topics (origins,
   deep time) omitted, optional-ized, or deferred. This is the co-op
   compromise posture, and it is commercially real precisely because co-ops
   and mixed-conviction families need materials nobody has to veto. It is
   also contested ground: secular purist organizations argue neutral is
   creationism-by-omission ("neutral science isn't neutral" is a standing
   SEA Homeschoolers argument), while convinced creationists find neutral
   materials too silent. Neutral programs get attacked from both flanks and
   bought by the middle anyway.
4. Secular: Mystery Science, BFSU, Generation Genius (plus REAL Science
   Odyssey and Blossom and Root beyond this wave). Evolution, the fossil
   record, and deep time are taught as settled science. Secular
   homeschoolers vet hard for this (the r/secularhomeschool litmus test is
   usually evolution coverage), which is why "is it really secular"
   threads dominate that community's curriculum talk.

The practical consequence: a science recommendation engine that ignores
worldview is broken on arrival. The same family that would love Mystery
Science's lesson design will return it over one natural-selection unit, and
the reverse family will bounce off one "God designed" caption.

## The experiment-supply problem

Science is the most commonly deprioritized core subject in homeschools.
Supercharged Science's survey of roughly 3,000 homeschool parents found most
spend under two hours a week on science, with lack of time the top reason
and not feeling qualified second; community writing adds two more honest
reasons: experiments make messes, and experiments need supplies that are
never on hand the morning of the lesson. Every program in this category has
a strategy for this problem, and the strategies define the market:

- Household-items engineering: Mystery Science, Berean Builders, BFSU, and
  TGTB design activities around what a kitchen already contains, and say so
  loudly in marketing. Mystery Science goes furthest with auto-generated
  per-lesson supply lists.
- Kits in the box: Noeo bundles nearly all experiment materials into the
  purchase, and charges $$$ for the privilege. Elemental sells optional
  kits; third parties (Home Science Tools) sell per-course kits for
  Apologia and Berean Builders, which effectively outsources the problem
  for the big Christian programs.
- Video as lab substitute: TGTB films every lab, Apologia's Self-Paced
  courses film the experiments, and Generation Genius's videos are
  demonstrations by design, so the family that skips the hands-on part
  still sees the phenomenon. This is the quiet concession that many
  families will not actually do the experiment.
- Structural avoidance: literature-based options (Sassafras, and Noeo's
  narration core) let reading carry the load, making hands-on work
  enrichment rather than the spine.

## Switching paths

- Within young-earth: Apologia and Berean Builders trade families
  constantly; Wile loyalty, edition changes, and price (Berean is roughly
  half the cost at elementary) drive the traffic. Both publishers'
  audiences treat the other as the only serious alternative.
- Christian lightening-up: Apologia's reading load pushes elementary
  families to TGTB units (shorter, prettier, family-style); TGTB's
  thinness pushes middle schoolers back to Apologia or Berean Builders when
  high school credibility starts to matter.
- The secular ladder: Mystery Science is a complete program only to about
  grade 2 (Cathy Duffy's line, echoed everywhere), so secular families
  layer or switch to a spine (REAL Science Odyssey, BFSU, Elemental) for
  grades 3-5, then hit the well-known secular middle school gap and
  assemble courses from BFSU Volume III, textbooks, or online classes.
  Mystery Science usually survives the switch as the fun layer.
- The prep-load axis: BFSU gains families who decide depth is worth
  teaching for and loses them to Mystery Science and REAL Science Odyssey
  when prep collapses; Noeo and boxed kits absorb families fleeing
  supply-hunting; Elemental loses spine-edition hunters to self-contained
  programs.
- Neutral as a waystation: RS4K and Elemental collect families exiting
  both directions (secular families fleeing explicit creationism, Christian
  families fleeing explicit evolution), which is why the segment persists
  despite being attacked from both sides.

## Gaps Homa can fill

1. Worldview-respectful behavior as a first-class family setting. The
   single most consequential fact in this category is that worldview
   decides purchases and trust. Homa should carry an explicit per-family
   science-worldview setting (young-earth, neutral or avoid-contested,
   secular) sourced at onboarding from the family's named curriculum, and
   generated science materials must stay inside it: no deep-time framing
   for an Apologia family, no design language for a BFSU family, silence on
   origins for the neutral middle unless asked. Getting this wrong once
   likely ends trust; getting it right is a differentiator no big publisher
   can copy, because each is locked to one posture.
2. Experiment supply logistics. The most-skipped subject is skipped over
   time, confidence, mess, and supplies. Homa can generate week-ahead
   supply lists from whatever curriculum the family already uses (per
   module for Apologia, per lesson for Berean Builders, per Mystery for
   Mystery Science), suggest substitutions, and offer a no-supplies
   fallback activity when the list fails the morning of. This is cheap for
   Homa and directly attacks the reason science dies in homeschools.
3. The grades 3-5 secular depth gap and the middle school cliff. Mystery
   Science families need sequence, depth, and written output from grade 3;
   nearly every K-8 program (RS4K, BFSU, Elemental, Noeo, TGTB) dead-ends
   before high school. Homa can generate the bridge: sequenced units,
   notebooking pages, and records that carry a family from a video
   supplement to transcript-grade work without a publisher switch.
4. Make the parent-intensive programs usable. BFSU is beloved and barely
   usable; Elemental requires edition-hunting; Berean Builders assumes the
   parent runs the notebook. Lesson-prep digests, question banks, spine
   substitutions, and per-child differentiated notebook pages (Berean's
   three-level review is the pattern to copy) turn Homa into the missing
   teacher's aide rather than a competing curriculum.
5. Records where the category has none. Mystery Science, TGTB units, and
   BFSU produce little or no gradeable output. Homa already tracks child
   progress; attaching lightweight science records and portfolio artifacts
   to whatever program the family runs fills a need every one of these
   publishers has left open.
