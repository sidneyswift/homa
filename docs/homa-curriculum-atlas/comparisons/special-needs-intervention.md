## Why special-needs families homeschool

The repo's demographic research already establishes this segment's scale
and trajectory, so link rather than re-derive: NHES 2023 found 21 percent
of homeschool parents rate "child has other special needs" an important
reason (7 percent call it the single most important), with physical or
mental health problems at 15 percent; the WaPo-Schar 2023 comparison shows
both figures rising sharply against 2019, and homeschooled kids indexing
well above non-homeschooled peers on ADHD (29 vs 16 percent), autism (13
vs 7), and anxiety or depression (31 vs 24). See
[`01-market-size-demographics.md` §5a](../../homa-planning/icp-research/01-market-size-demographics.md).
The community-voice research adds the texture: the dominant newcomer
origin story in 2024-2025 threads is the pulled kid, with bullying,
anxiety, and ADHD/autism mismatch as the backstories
([`02-community-voice-pains.md`](../../homa-planning/icp-research/02-community-voice-pains.md)).

What the atlas adds: this category's publishers are made of the same
story. Susan Barton left a computer career after her nephew reached tenth
grade reading at a second-grade level and the school said to accept it.
Nessy exists because four schools failed the dyslexic founder, his
teacher mother homeschooled him, and her card games became software.
Marie Rippel built All About Reading teaching her dyslexic son. Cheryl
Swope wrote Simply Classical after homeschooling twins with autism and
schizophrenia through high school. TouchMath began with seven students a
classroom could not teach to add. The segment's defining fact for Homa:
these families did not choose homeschooling as a philosophy, they
arrived mid-crisis after institutional failure, often carrying an IEP
folder and a trauma story. Marketing, product design, and assistant
behavior that assume a cheerful lifestyle choice will misfire.

## The Orton-Gillingham economy

Structured literacy runs on a certification economy. Mainstream OG
practice gates delivery behind multi-week trainings, practicum hours, and
credential tiers; Susan Barton describes schools unable to afford
"multiple weeks of training... often several summers in a row." Certified
tutoring is priced accordingly: a real parent write-up prices local OG
tutoring at $70 per session, about $560 per month per child at the
standard two-a-week dose, and Lindamood-Bell's clinic intensives
reportedly run $90 to $167 per hour behind a $495 to $1,150 evaluation,
with five-figure totals normal. For a multi-year intervention, that
economy excludes most families.

The scripted home programs are best understood as arbitrage against that
economy. Barton's entire design (word-for-word scripts, five to six hours
of demonstration video per level, screenings instead of credentials,
a three-tier certification that is optional and aimed at professionals)
exists to move OG delivery from certified specialists to parents at $350
to $400 per level, roughly one month of tutoring per level. AAR performs
the same move one intensity band lower and adds storybook warmth. Nessy
digitizes the specialist so no adult delivers at all. The community's
famous resale culture (Barton levels resold at $50 under retail through
Facebook groups) functions as informal mutual aid inside the same
economy. The escalation ladder is legible and worth internalizing:
school-issued or gentle phonics, then AAR or Logic of English at first
struggle, then Barton when severity shows, then LiPS or a clinic when
even Barton's screening says not yet. The language arts comparison's
science-of-reading section
([`language-arts.md`](language-arts.md)) explains the wave lifting all
of these.

## Diagnosis-driven shopping

This aisle is organized by diagnosis, not by subject, and the lanes are
unevenly mature:

- Dyslexia is the developed lane: cheap screeners (Nessy's Dyslexia
  Quest; Barton's free student screening), a graded product ladder, a
  dedicated influencer ecosystem (Homeschooling with Dyslexia), and
  vocabulary parents actually use (OG, structured literacy, decodable).
  Notably, many families never buy a formal diagnosis; free screenings
  and symptom checklists stand in for evaluations that cost thousands,
  and reviewers say so plainly ("I never paid for an official
  diagnosis").
- Dyscalculia is the developed lane's little sibling, a decade or two
  behind: awareness is UK-led, the authority is one author (Ronit Bird),
  the school method (TouchMath) has retreated from the consumer market,
  and most families land on a mainstream mastery curriculum (Math-U-See)
  rather than an intervention.
- Autism and developmental delay shop for whole ecosystems, not
  programs: a full adapted curriculum (Simply Classical, essentially
  alone in its niche), a social-cognition slot (Social Thinking, now
  culturally contested by the neurodiversity movement), a
  cognitive-training slot (Equipping Minds, evidence thin), and a
  therapy schedule that the school plan must bend around. Simply
  Classical's built-in "therapies" slot is the honest design here.
- ADHD is not a lane but a filter applied to everything: short lessons,
  low-stimulation pages, movement, and game bursts. It is why Memoria's
  plain black-and-white pages, MUS's one-topic years, Nessy's
  ninety-second videos, and Teaching Textbooks' low-pressure tone all
  advertise well to this crowd without being interventions at all.

## The emotional economics

These families pay the most and are burned the most, and the two facts
compound. The spending pattern is a stack of failures: reviewers describe
"tons of money on less expensive curricula that helped a little bit"
before the program that worked, and the working program is itself the
expensive one ($400 Barton levels, $516 LiPS kits, four-figure therapy
packages, $399 sound-therapy add-ons in the cognitive-training world).
Time costs dwarf money: two to five years of daily one-on-one delivery,
where the parent is the intervention and parent burnout, not product
dissatisfaction, is the real churn mechanism.

Burn shows up on the evidence side too. The market's claims run ahead of
its independent evidence (the What Works Clearinghouse found no Barton
studies meeting its standards; Social Thinking's reach exceeds its
research base; cognitive training and its alternative-therapy fringe are
contested), while marketing sells rescue to exhausted people ("This
program is impossible to fail"). The market knows its buyers are burned
and has evolved trust rituals in response: money-back guarantees,
free pre-purchase screenings that talk families out of wrong purchases,
free founder advice lines, and the resale culture. Any product entering
this segment inherits those expectations: prove trustworthiness before
asking for money, and never add to the pile of things that did not work.

## How wave 1 programs position for this segment

The mainstream programs already profiled earn their special-needs
credibility three ways, and the cross-links matter because these are
what a family owns before they reach the intervention aisle:

- Origin stories: [All About Reading](../curricula/language-arts/all-about-learning.md)
  leads with its founder's dyslexic son and OG fidelity; it is the
  segment's default first purchase and the top of the escalation ladder
  described above. [Sequential Spelling](../curricula/language-arts/sequential-spelling.md)
  carries the same heritage in spelling.
- Clinical design: [Handwriting Without Tears](../curricula/language-arts/handwriting-without-tears.md)
  is occupational-therapist-designed and owns the fine-motor and
  dysgraphia slot; its double lines and wood pieces are therapy tools
  that pass as school supplies.
- Shame-removal mechanics: [Math-U-See](../curricula/math/math-u-see.md)
  built the pattern this whole atlas keeps finding, skill-based levels
  with no grade labels (Greek letters), one concept mastered at a time,
  manipulatives into the teen years; its "rescue status" for strugglers
  is wave 1's own finding. [Teaching Textbooks](../curricula/math/teaching-textbooks.md)
  sells the same relief through tone and audio support.
- And the bridge case: [Memoria Press](../curricula/all-in-one/memoria-press.md)
  is the only mainstream publisher that built a full special-needs line
  (Simply Classical) instead of an accommodations page, which is why it
  owns the classical special-needs niche unopposed.

## Gaps Homa can fill

1. Pacing without shame, structurally. Every successful program here
   de-grades its levels (Barton levels, MUS Greek letters, Simply
   Classical readiness levels, Nessy islands) because the segment's
   emotional core is a child who is chronologically twelve and
   developmentally seven. Homa's display law already matches (growth-only
   visual language, no red, no "behind," ever, per
   [`13-mr-prd.md` FR-7](../../homa-planning/13-mr-prd.md); never compare
   siblings, per the repo's copy laws). The atlas-level addition: Homa
   should also translate between level systems without ever rendering
   them as grades, so "Barton 4" or "Simply Classical Level 2" resolves
   to skill positions, never to "three years behind."
2. Regeneration at the child's level, with the child's dignity. The
   hardest materials problem in this segment is age-respecting content at
   skill level: a twelve-year-old decoding CVC words needs practice text
   that is controlled to taught patterns but not babyish. Print products
   cannot personalize this; a constraint-respecting generator can (the
   Barton profile details the controlled-text engine; the Ronit Bird
   profile warns when worksheets themselves are the trauma trigger and
   game boards should be generated instead).
3. Progress evidence that celebrates non-linear growth. This segment's
   progress is strategy change (counting gives way to deriving), scaffold
   fade (dots disappearing from numerals), and regressions that are
   normal, not linear percentages. Homa's progress evidence can narrate
   exactly that, and it doubles as the artifact these parents need for
   skeptical spouses, evaluators, and their own 2 a.m. doubts.
4. Stack orchestration including therapies. The real schedule is phonics
   plus OT plus speech plus a co-op, and only Simply Classical's manual
   acknowledges it. A planner that treats therapy appointments as
   first-class citizens beside lessons describes these families' actual
   weeks.
5. Honest lane-keeping. Respect program exclusivity rules (Barton forbids
   concurrent reading programs), answer complements-vs-substitutes
   truthfully (Nessy beside Barton is fine as review; a second phonics
   program is not), and never play diagnostician: Homa can know the
   escalation map and the free screeners, and can gently point at them,
   without ever labeling a child.
6. Cost mercy. Per-child consumable taxes hit hardest here (multiple
   levels, multiple kids, resale friction). Homa-generated per-child
   practice sidesteps the tax entirely, which lands differently for a
   family already five programs and several thousand dollars deep.
