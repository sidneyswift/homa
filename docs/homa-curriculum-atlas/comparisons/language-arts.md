## How families assemble LA stacks

Language arts is not bought like math. A family buys one math program; the
same family typically buys language arts in pieces, because the category is
really four or five skills with different best-in-class specialists and
different daily time slots. The archetypal early-elementary stack is four
purchases: a reading program (AAR, LOE, 100 Easy Lessons), a spelling
program (AAS, Sequential Spelling, Spelling You See), a writing program
(WWE, Brave Writer, WriteShop Primary), and a handwriting workbook (HWT,
Zaner-Bloser), often with a practice layer (Explode the Code) and a
supplement (Heggerty, Bob Books) underneath. The federal data the repo
already cites (families report 4 to 6 concurrent curriculum sources,
`08-segments-curricula.md` §6) is mostly this category's doing.

Two products in this set exist precisely to collapse the stack: Logic of
English integrates reading, spelling, handwriting, and early grammar into
one program, and TGTB integrates everything including literature and art
into one course book. The market divides on that choice: integrators trade
away per-skill best-of-breed and per-skill visibility; assemblers trade
away simplicity and pay four times. Publishers know the stack is the unit
of thought: IEW bundles Fix It! into its writing packages, AAS is designed
to ride beside AAR on the same tiles, and Brave Writer explicitly assumes
someone else taught phonics.

For Homa: when a mom lists her LA setup, expect a stack, not a name.
"AAR 2, AAS 1, HWT, and we just started Jot It Down" is one child's normal
language arts. Onboarding, progress, and materials generation should treat
the stack as the first-class object, and the crosswalk file already
translates each piece's level into skill positions.

## Switching paths

Switching is normal in this market (mid-year changes are community-endorsed
routine, per the segments research), and LA switches follow legible paths:

- Reading, struggle-driven: integrated or lighter programs (TGTB phonics,
  100 Easy Lessons, school leftovers) feed into AAR or LOE when a child
  struggles; AAR feeds into Barton or tutoring in severe dyslexia cases.
  Direction of travel is always toward more explicit, more OG.
- Reading, boredom-driven: the reverse flow. Kids who click with reading
  exit AAR or LOE early (too slow, too many components) into plain readers
  plus ETC for reps, or into TGTB where reading is one strand of one book.
- Writing, the great pendulum: IEW and Brave Writer are each other's churn
  destinations. Formula-fatigued IEW families (voice strangled by
  checklists) land at Brave Writer; structure-starved Brave Writer families
  (adrift without a sequence) land at IEW; WriteShop and WWS catch families
  wanting middle ground. WWE families hitting the Level 3 boredom wall or
  the WWS difficulty jump split between IEW (structure) and Brave Writer
  (joy).
- Grammar: FLL's repetition sends families to Fix It! for story-based
  editing; Fix It's lack of diagramming sends classical purists back to
  FLL or analytical grammar. Many families simply stop buying grammar
  after elementary, betting on absorption.
- Spelling: AAS's teacher load and price send families to Sequential
  Spelling or Spelling You See; rule-hungry strugglers travel the other
  way. TGTB families get spelling free inside the course and rarely shop.
- Handwriting: HWT to Zaner-Bloser or TGTB handwriting on looks; most
  families exit handwriting programs entirely once fluency lands,
  folding practice into copywork.

The pattern for Homa's comparison engine: almost every LA switch is a
trade between explicitness and joy, or between integration and precision.
Naming the trade honestly ("you are buying structure and giving up voice
for a season") is more useful than a winner.

## The science-of-reading wave

The loudest current in this category. The public-school reading wars went
mainstream via Emily Hanford's Sold a Story podcast (2022); balanced
literacy and three-cueing became discredited, 40-plus states legislated
evidence-based reading instruction, and a federal Science of Reading Act
was introduced in the 2025-2026 Congress. Homeschool families absorbed the
vocabulary: structured literacy, phonemic awareness, decodables, OG.

Who rides the wave: the Orton-Gillingham lineage was positioned decades
early. AAR and AAS (OG made scriptable), Logic of English (OG plus
linguistics), Barton (OG for severe dyslexia, wave 2 backlog), and the
supplement layer (Heggerty for phonemic awareness, decodable readers like
Bob Books) all get to say "we always taught this way," and their marketing
now leans hard on science-of-reading language. Direct Instruction (100
Easy Lessons) shares the evidence halo from the older Project Follow
Through literature, giving the $20 book new respectability. Explode the
Code benefits passively as systematic-phonics practice.

Who has to answer for it: whole-word and leveled-reader approaches
(Hooked on Phonics historically straddled; balanced-literacy-flavored
school materials) and any program vague about decoding. Notably, the
literature-first writing cultures (Brave Writer, Charlotte Mason circles)
are not anti-phonics, but their audiences increasingly bolt an OG phonics
program underneath, which reinforces the stack pattern above. TGTB's
integrated phonics is systematic but not OG-branded; its strugglers leak
to AAR, and the complements pattern (TGTB plus AAR) is common.

For Homa: science-of-reading vocabulary is now parent vocabulary. Homa
should speak it accurately (phonemic awareness vs phonics, decodable vs
predictable text), respect the OG brand loyalty, and, per the crosswalk's
porting note, exploit the happy alignment that the biggest phonics
publishers already de-grade their levels: skills-first placement is the
market norm, and it is also Homa's map philosophy.

## Gaps Homa can fill

1. Stack orchestration. Nobody owns the four-purchase problem: four
   products, four pacing schemes, four daily slots, no shared progress
   view. Homa can be the layer that sees AAR 2 plus AAS 1 plus HWT plus
   Jot It Down as one child's language arts and schedules, tracks, and
   reports it coherently. This is the category's structural gap and it is
   product-shaped, not content-shaped.
2. The practice layer, personalized. ETC's whole niche (cheap independent
   reps) is generateable: skill-targeted practice pages in varied formats,
   starring the child's interests, on demand, with no per-child consumables
   tax. Same for double-line handwriting pages using the child's own
   spelling words, and decodable passages constrained to the exact
   phonogram inventory a family's program has taught so far. Original
   patterns, never clones of any publisher's expression.
3. Stall support inside someone else's curriculum. Every profile's "why
   families leave" list starts with a stall: the AAR lesson that will not
   click, the IEW source text that bores, the WWE passage rhythm going
   stale. A five-minute targeted assist matched to the family's exact
   program and position (the crosswalk makes position legible) retains
   families in the curriculum they already bought, which is Homa's
   stated posture: build off what she uses.
4. Honest complements-vs-substitutes answers. The Heggerty finding
   generalizes: much of this category is complements masquerading as
   competitors (ETC beside AAR, Fix It! beside IEW, Heggerty under
   everything). A comparison engine that sometimes answers "both, they do
   different jobs" earns trust no roundup site can.
5. Progress visibility without grades. The no-grade-labels norm (AAR, LOE,
   ETC, Brave Writer, WWE all refuse grade equivalence) means parents
   chronically wonder where their child really stands. Homa's map can
   answer that in skill terms while honoring each program's own framing,
   per the crosswalk porting notes: never render level numbers as grades,
   and keep ahead-framing gentle.
6. The post-completion moment. Programs with endpoints (100 Easy Lessons
   lesson 100, AAR Level 4, WWE Level 4) create predictable what-now
   moments that no publisher monetizes well. Homa can own the transition
   recommendation.
