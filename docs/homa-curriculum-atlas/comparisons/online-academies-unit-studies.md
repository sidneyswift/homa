# Online academies and unit studies: comparison and market notes

Category synthesis for the wave 2b lane: live-class online academies and
unit-study systems. Profiles are the source of truth for program claims;
prices are as of Jul 2026 and dated in each profile. The two families of
products look unrelated but answer the same constraint from opposite
ends. The constraint is the parent's teaching capacity: academies sell
the family a hired teacher per subject, unit studies sell the family a
way for one parent to teach every child at once. One buys capacity, the
other compresses demand, and many families do both in the same year.

## The live-class academy model

The academy product has four load-bearing parts, and every provider in
the table makes a different trade across them.

Cohort schedules. Academies run school years: August to May, fixed
weekly meeting times, spring registration seasons, seat limits, wait
lists. Wilson Hill classes meet live twice a week for 90 minutes;
Excelsior meets weekly for 90 (math twice for 60); TPS requires regular
live attendance for credit and relaxes it only for AP and dual-credit
recordings. For the family, each enrolled course re-inserts a piece of
school rhythm into the homeschool week, which is simultaneously the
value (external structure a teen cannot negotiate with) and the cost
(the flexibility the family homeschools for drains away one course at a
time). Recordings exist everywhere as an absence valve, not a delivery
mode; the sold product is the live seat.

Teacher-graded work. The real product is evaluation. TPS states
outright that individual feedback is where its teachers put most of
their time and designs grading so no single assignment can sink a GPA;
Excelsior reviewers lead with the extensive graded feedback; Wilson
Hill's seminar essays come back from a teacher who knows the student.
This is what distinguishes the academy dollar from courseware: an
outside human's judgment, which (per the transcript economy in
[high-school.md](high-school.md)) is also worth more to a registrar
than a parent's grade.

Accreditation postures. The lane splits into two theologies of proof.
One pole institutionalizes: TPS is accredited as a school by the Middle
States Association and offers a diploma partnership; Wilson Hill holds
Cognia (SACS CASI) accreditation and a graduation track; Excelsior
holds ACS WASC accreditation for its courses while refusing to grant
diplomas. The other pole refuses on principle: Scholé Academy issues no
official transcripts and affirms the parent's right to prepare them;
Homeschool Connections is unaccredited because accreditation would hand
parental authority to an agency. Both poles sell to the same anxiety
documented in [high-school.md](high-school.md): accreditation is the
market's most monetizable emotion, and the refusers are selling the
counter-identity (homeschool purity) as deliberately as the accreditors
sell officialness. TPS threads the needle with per-course credit
choice: the same course can be an accredited-transcript line or an
uncredentialed enrichment, family's pick.

Price points vs co-ops. Academy tuition runs about $500-700 per course
at TPS, $596 typical at Excelsior, $625-775 at Scholé, $750-1,200 at
Wilson Hill: a five-course load lands between roughly $2,500 and
$5,500 per student per year before books. Against that,
[Classical Conversations](../curricula/all-in-one/classical-conversations.md)
communities charge roughly $400-750 for a full Foundations year and
$800-2,400+ for Challenge, meaning one academy course can cost more
than a year of co-op community. The difference is what the money buys:
co-ops sell parent-tutored community and keep the teaching burden
distributed among the parents; academies sell credentialed subject
teachers and remove the burden entirely. The budget poles bracket the
lane: SchoolhouseTeachers at $389 per family per year is what
outsourcing costs when no human teaches, and Homeschool Connections'
recorded library at $429.97 per family is what taught instruction
costs when the teaching is on tape.

## How academies compete with dual enrollment

The academy's structural rival above grade 10 is not another academy;
it is the community college. Dual enrollment is documented in
[high-school.md](high-school.md) as the dominant modern exit: 2.8
million students, growing double digits, state-subsidized in much of
the country, double-counted credits, and an external college
transcript that admissions offices trust more than anything an academy
can issue. On price, a subsidized dual-enrollment course beats a $750
academy seat outright.

The academies' counters are specific:

- Worldview and environment. The community college classroom is an
  adult space with adult content; the academy is a curated peer
  cohort inside the family's worldview. For many families this alone
  justifies the price through grade 10, and for some through 12.
- Age fit. Dual enrollment is structurally an 11th-12th grade play;
  academies own grades 6-10, the on-ramp years when the parent first
  hits the algebra and essay walls but the child is too young for a
  college campus or roster.
- Absorbing the threat. The academies increasingly resell college
  credit inside their own wrapper: TPS runs dual-credit courses
  through Belhaven University, Excelsior partners with LeTourneau
  for live dual-credit sections taught by its own adjunct-credentialed
  teachers, and Veritas Scholars Academy advertises dual enrollment
  through partner colleges. AP is the other proof currency: TPS is
  College Board-authorized for certified AP courses and Wilson Hill
  runs an AP slate, both letting a family buy college-signaling
  without leaving the worldview.
- NCAA. For athletes, approved academy courses (TPS, Excelsior, Mr.
  D's live core, Wilson Hill) sidestep the eligibility complexities
  that credit-by-exam strategies create.

The observed family pattern stitches them: academy-heavy 7th through
10th grade, then dual enrollment claims part or all of 11th and 12th,
with the academy keeping the worldview-sensitive subjects (Bible,
humanities, sometimes English) to the end. Both halves of that pattern
put the parent in the same seat, aggregator of record across providers,
which is the seat [high-school.md](high-school.md) already identified
as Homa's.

## The unit-study lineage, and why it keeps being reinvented

The lineage runs Konos to Five in a Row to Gather Round to Campfire,
with Amanda Bennett's PDFs as the digital bridge, and it is best read
as one repeated transaction: the format's appeal is permanent, its
labor cost is structural, and each generation sells the appeal with one
more layer of labor removed.

The appeal never changes. One topic taught to every child at once is
the only way a one-teacher household scales past two students;
integrated topics produce the retention stories that make unit-study
families evangelists; and interest-led topics recruit the child's own
motivation. Konos proved all three in 1984 with character-trait units,
hands-on discovery, and the whole library as textbook.

The labor never disappears; it migrates. Konos demanded the mother
plan, gather, level, and record everything, and its own product line
confessed the problem: In-A-Box (a semester in a carrying case, crafts
included) sold prep relief, and the KONOS Compass sold coverage
checklists against the "are we covering everything" anxiety. Five in a
Row shrank the unit to one picture book and five discussion lenses,
collapsing prep to a library visit at the price of academic scope.
Amanda Bennett digitized the format into $13-19 PDFs with the research
pre-done and links embedded, making unit studies an impulse purchase.
Gather Round removed the leveling labor: one teacher's guide plus six
readiness-leveled student notebooks, in Instagram-era design, family
bundle pricing. Campfire removes the record-keeping and the printing:
tiered open-and-go units mapped to Carnegie-credit fractions, no
printer required, with topics (camping, survival, trades) that finally
recruit fathers into the format.

Reinvention is also re-skinning. Each generation wears its era's
aesthetic and its era's parent identity: Konos is 1980s character
formation, FIAR is library-culture gentleness, Gather Round is
beauty-forward Instagram motherhood, Campfire is outdoorsy
self-sufficiency with dad in frame. The underlying product is nearly
identical; the register is the differentiator, which is why the genre
never consolidates. Units are cheap, founder-voiced content products
with no platform moat, so a new voice with a new register can always
restart the cycle.

And the churn is structural, which guarantees demand for the next
cycle. Every system in the lineage excludes math and, mostly, phonics;
every one triggers sufficiency anxiety as the oldest child ages; the
supplements families add quietly become the spine; and the family
exits to structured programs, telling warm stories about the unit
years. A new cohort of parents with young kids rediscovers the format
every five to seven years, and a new product is always waiting.

## Gaps Homa can fill

The two halves of this lane produce the same two unserved jobs:
coordination for the outsourcers, labor absorption for the
unit-study families, and records for both.

- Schedule orchestration across the academy-plus-home stack. An
  academy family's week is anchored by immovable live blocks (two per
  Wilson Hill course, one to two per TPS or Excelsior course, per
  child, sometimes across time zones), and the home-taught subjects
  must flow around them, including seminar prep time. Homa's planner
  should treat outsourced classes as fixed anchors with prep-time
  shadows, and treat the enrollment layer (spring registration
  windows, wait lists that auto-bill on conversion at TPS, tuition
  increments due July 15 and November 15, drop deadlines, placement
  and readiness submissions) as a calendar-and-paperwork stream Homa
  carries so the parent stops tracking five providers' policies in
  her head.
- One child record above the providers' five gradebooks. TPS emails
  weekly assignment and grade reports every Friday; Excelsior grades
  live in Canvas; Wilson Hill grades live in its portal; the co-op
  and dual-enrollment lines live elsewhere again. Nobody compiles it,
  which is the identical finding as [high-school.md](high-school.md),
  and the fix is the same: ingest provider-reported evidence with
  provenance into the child's record, then compile transcript-grade
  output on demand.
- Unit-study planning labor, absorbed. What In-A-Box did for three
  Konos traits, Homa can do for any topic on demand: turn "we're
  obsessed with volcanoes" into a leveled week plan with a library
  list, a supply list, and per-child response pages pitched at each
  child's level (the Gather Round ladder, trace to word bank to
  sentence to paragraph to essay, is the cleanest difficulty grammar
  in the market and the one to emulate). The unit theme is a declared
  interest signal; generated practice for the separate math and
  phonics blocks should ride it.
- Sufficiency mapping, the Compass modernized. Unit-study families
  have needed "what did this unit actually cover, and what did it
  miss" since 1984; Homa's skill graph can answer it per child and
  per unit, and quietly convert activity-based weeks into
  compliance-ready records in portfolio states, which is the exact
  anxiety that drives families out of the format they love.
- Honesty about the lane. Homa competes with nobody in either table:
  it does not teach live, and it does not publish units. It is the
  registrar above the academies and the planning labor beneath the
  unit studies, both of which are seats the parent currently occupies
  at 11 pm. When a family asks whether an academy course, a co-op, or
  dual enrollment fits their kid and their budget, Homa should know
  these providers cold and answer like the guidance counselor the
  homeschool never had.
