where Homa meets its youngest families, usually in their very first

## The tot-school and playful-learning culture

This category's culture was built on blogs and finished on Instagram.
"Tot school" was coined in 2008 by Carisa Hinson of the blog 1+1+1=1
(a former kindergarten teacher whose 6-year-old declared his toddler
brother was doing school too), and the term now names a whole genre:
intentional, playful, tray-and-bin activities for ages 1-3, shared
photo-first. The pipeline it created is the category's distribution
system: a mother with classroom credentials builds an audience sharing
free activities, the audience asks for a plan, and the plan becomes a
$20-50 PDF. Playing Preschool (Busy Toddler, 3M+ followers), Blossom
and Root, The Peaceful Preschool, and This Reading Mama all came up
this pipeline; Teaching Mama's The Preschool Journey (a 26-week
alphabet-centered ebook, sometimes confused with Busy Toddler's
products) is the same story one tier down in reach.

Susie Allison is the culture's most explicit ideologue: Busy Toddler's
public identity is "an advocate for appropriate early childhood
education," anti-worksheet, anti-flashcard, anti-pressure, and her
curriculum monetizes the position without betraying it (no worksheets
anywhere, "play is enough" as pedagogy). That stance is the emotional
center of gravity for the whole secular side of the category: buyers
are not choosing between scopes and sequences, they are choosing
which trusted internet mom's philosophy of early childhood to adopt.
The aesthetic (taped butcher paper, dot stickers, sensory bins on a
kitchen floor) is itself a signal of belonging, and it photographs on
purpose: sharing the activity back to Instagram is part of the loop.

## Play-based versus skills-based

The category has one real fault line. On one side, play-based programs
(Playing Preschool, Before Five in a Row, The Peaceful Preschool,
Blossom and Root) teach through activities, books, and conversation,
and treat early academics as emergent; several refuse worksheets as a
matter of identity. On the other, skills-based programs (Horizons
Preschool, TGTB Preschool, Abeka's K4, ABCmouse's lesson paths, and
the letter-of-the-week printable economy) run a visible academic
sequence with pages completed as proof. The Christian market leans
skills-based earlier (workbook preschools are a Christian-publisher
genre), while the secular market's center of mass is firmly play-based,
but the line is not clean: ABCJesusLovesMe is Bible-first and
play-based, and Reading the Alphabet is secular and skills-based.

Two things blunt the fight in practice. First, nearly everyone shares
the same skeleton: a letter of the week, a theme, a book pile, counting
somewhere. The disagreement is about the medium (bin versus page), not
the content. Second, real families hybridize constantly: the
documented pattern in reviews is a play-based spine with a skills
patch added late (a handwriting workbook alongside Playing Preschool
Year 2, Reading the Alphabet after letters are known, TGTB Preschool
for the child who asks for "real school"). Switching direction runs
skills-to-play as often as play-to-skills: workbook-preschool families
whose 3-year-old cries at the table are told by every community to go
play, and play-based families with an eager almost-5 get pushed toward
kindergarten materials. The tension is resolved by aging out: at 5,
everyone buys phonics.

## What "preschool curriculum" means to the buyer

The product is not academics. Every serious voice in this market,
including the sellers, repeats that preschoolers need play, books, and
conversation, and the recurring community question "do I even need a
curriculum for a 3-year-old" reliably gets answered no, even on seller
blogs. What the buyer is actually purchasing, and what the successful
products all deliver, is structure for the adult: a daily rhythm, a
told-what-to-do plan, a supply list, and the feeling of being a
competent teacher-parent. Cathy Duffy's category is "early learning,"
but the buyer's category is closer to "a plan for our mornings."

This explains otherwise odd facts. It is why activity plans built on
household items (Playing Preschool) and book lists with menus (Before
Five in a Row) outsell academic content at the same price. It is why
Experience Early Learning can charge daycare money for pre-bagged
crafts: it sells the most complete version of "someone else planned
this." It is why Lovevery functions as curriculum-adjacent: its play
guides answer "what should this age be doing," which is the same
question. And it is why the category's real competitor is free
Pinterest: what the paid products add to the free idea pile is
sequence, confidence, and the permission to stop searching.

## The free-and-cheap price ceiling

Preschool is the cheapest aisle in homeschooling, and the ceiling is
structural. The free tier is credible, not scraps: ABCJesusLovesMe's
full weekly plans, Reading the Alphabet's systematic pre-reading,
Khan Academy Kids on the app side, plus the entire blog-printable
economy. The paid core clusters at $22-70 one-time (Blossom and Root
Vol 1 at about $22, Playing Preschool at $39.90, guides from Peaceful
Press and Five in a Row at $49, the two-year Playing Preschool bundle
at $69.80). Above that sit only products selling something other than
curriculum: Horizons' $180 box sells school-at-home identity,
Lovevery's roughly $480 per year sells curated toys, and Experience
Early Learning's roughly $1,000+ per year sells prep labor, priced
against daycare rather than against PDFs.

The ceiling exists because the whole market keeps saying the quiet
part out loud: you do not need any of this. A product priced above
about $70 has to beat not just competitors but the buyer's own
awareness that the library is free. The consequences for anyone
selling to this segment: per-month pricing gets compared to a $40
forever purchase, sibling reuse is assumed (PDFs are the norm partly
because relicensing per child would be revolt-inducing), and the
willingness to pay concentrates entirely in saved time and saved
doubt, not in content.

## Kindergarten-readiness anxiety is the purchase trigger

Almost nobody buys preschool curriculum for preschool's sake. The
purchase moment is anxiety about what comes next: the child is 3.5
or 4, peers are enrolling somewhere, a relative asks about school,
and the parent needs to know the gap to kindergarten is covered.
Every product in the category points its marketing at exactly this
nerve. Playing Preschool was designed backward from what a
kindergarten teacher wished incoming students had, and says so; its
FAQ leads with kindergarten readiness. ABCJesusLovesMe's origin story
is a mother's "overwhelming burden to prepare him for kindergarten."
Horizons and TGTB sell "prepare your child for kindergarten" on the
box; Experience Early Learning sells school-readiness domains and
assessment; even anti-academic Before Five in a Row is bought as
"readiness" via its Treasury section.

The readiness question is also where this market's two anxieties
collide: fear that the child will be behind, and fear of pushing too
hard too young. The winning products speak to both at once, which is
the real genius of the play-based pitch: rigorous-sounding readiness
(sequenced skills, teacher credentials) delivered in a form that
absolves the parent of the pressure guilt. Readiness checklists
circulate constantly in this segment, and the checkable items are
social and practical (listens to a story, holds scissors, separates
from parent) as much as academic, which most parents only discover
after buying letter drills.

## What Homa can do for its youngest families

This category is Homa's front door: the 2-4 band is inside Homa's
core 2-8 range, most of these families have never bought curriculum
before, and their loyalties formed here (gentle, play-based,
Instagram-native, price-shy) persist into elementary. The gaps:

- Activity generation from household items is the category's core
  product, and Homa can do it better than any static PDF: this child,
  this week's theme, what is already in the cupboard, sized to a
  15-minute attention span. The bar is Playing Preschool at $39.90
  forever, so Homa's version must win on personalization and zero
  planning, not on content volume. It must also inherit the form
  factor: bins, hunts, games, and cards, never a worksheet by
  default for under-fives.
- Readiness reassurance without drill is the deepest unmet need. No
  product in the table can tell a parent "based on what you two
  actually did this month, here is where she stands on the things
  kindergarten teachers care about, and here is the one gentle thing
  to try next." Homa's observational, no-testing progress model is
  built for exactly this, and the reassure-before-inform voice rule
  is the right register for readiness anxiety.
- Absorb the prep loop the cheap tier imposes: free and $40 programs
  outsource printing, library holds, supply gathering, and looking
  ahead to the parent. Homa can compress that Sunday hour into a
  generated week plan plus a library list, which is the entire value
  proposition Experience Early Learning charges $100+ per month for.
- Solve the sibling hour: most preschool-curriculum buyers in
  homeschool families are simultaneously teaching a 6- or 8-year-old.
  Tot school exists so the toddler is occupied during the older kid's
  lessons. Homa's planner should treat "meaningful preschool activity
  that runs parallel to big-sibling school" as a first-class request.
- Keep the record they do not know they want: none of these programs
  produces any account of the child's early years. A quiet timeline
  (what she built, counted, listened to, asked) becomes both the
  kindergarten-readiness evidence and the keepsake this segment
  photographs anyway, and it seeds the ChildProgress map before
  formal academics begin.
- Respect the worldview split: the Christian half expects Bible woven
  in (ABCJLM families will ask Homa for verse-linked activities), and
  the secular half screens hard for its absence. Homa's generated
  preschool materials must stay worldview-neutral by default and
  accommodate faith content on explicit request, the same posture the
  atlas records for neutral publishers.
