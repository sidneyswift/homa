# All-in-one traditional Christian publishers and boxes

Comparison notes for the category profiled in
`../curricula/all-in-one/`: the legacy Christian publishers and boxed
programs that define "buying a curriculum" for most newcomers. Profiles
carry the detail; this file compares and interprets. Prices are summarized
from the profiles, all as of Jul 2026; the sourced market table lives in
[08-segments-curricula.md](../../homa-planning/icp-research/08-segments-curricula.md) §4.

Reading the table: parent load splits the category into three postures.
Teacher-led boxes (Abeka print, MFW, Sonlight, HOD) sell the mom a
teaching script; worktext systems (CLE, LIFEPAC, ACE, Master Books upper
levels) sell her a child who works alone; video and online lines (Abeka
Academy, BJU Hub, Monarch) sell her a replacement teacher. Price tracks
posture: the more the product replaces her labor with staff or paper, the
more it costs, except TGTB, which broke the category's pricing physics
with free PDFs.

## The box as safety net

The newcomer psychology this category serves is documented in the repo's
segment research: school-at-home is the default first shape because it is
the only model a school-formed parent knows, and crisis-onboarding
guidance literally prescribes a boxed curriculum to rebuild rhythm
([08-segments-curricula.md](../../homa-planning/icp-research/08-segments-curricula.md)
§7 card 6). The box's real product is not pedagogy, it is the transfer of
responsibility: someone credentialed has already decided what a good year
contains, so the mom's job shrinks from "design an education" to "keep up
with the plan." Every publisher in the table sells this safety in its own
dialect: Abeka's 170 numbered lesson plans, Sonlight's week grid with
every page pre-assigned, MFW's teach-once family manual, CLE's diagnostic
placement that tells her exactly where the child stands, HOD's
check-the-boxes daily guides. The anxiety being purchased against is "am
I ruining my child," and the box answers it with completeness, brand age,
and paper trail rather than with evidence about her actual child. That is
the emotional slot Homa competes in, and the reason Homa's reassurance
must be evidence-shaped rather than plan-shaped.

## Accreditation and paper as a selling point

Two publishers turned legitimacy itself into a product line: Abeka
Academy's accredited track (FACCS, MSA CESS; report cards, transcripts,
diploma) and BJU's Academy of Home Education riding on its video courses,
with AOP's Ignite Christian Academy and ACE's Lighthouse Christian
Academy as the workbook world's equivalents. CLE sells a lighter version
(Homeschool Plus record-keeping), and even non-accredited players compete
on paper: Sonlight's IG checkboxes and HOD's guides double as the
family's legal records. The pattern to notice: accreditation is bought
for narrow, real reasons (high school transcripts, college admissions,
athletics eligibility, skeptical relatives, strict states), it costs
$50-600 per year on top of curriculum, and it locks the family's academic
history inside the publisher's registrar. For most K-8 families the need
is not accreditation at all, it is defensible records, which is why
record-keeping is the honest generalization of this selling point, and it
is a thing Homa produces as a side effect of normal use rather than as a
paid lock-in.

## The burnout-and-drift lifecycle

The category's churn is not a secret; it is one of the most consistent
findings in homeschool research. Newcomers buy the box in August, run it
faithfully into autumn, hit the documented exhaustion points (November
and February), start skipping the seatwork, and by spring are either
loosening into eclectic use of the box's remnants or shopping its
replacement; the standing share of strict boxed-curriculum families is
only about 5% precisely because the box is a stage, not a destination
(evidence and sources in
[08-segments-curricula.md](../../homa-planning/icp-research/08-segments-curricula.md)
§2, §6, and §7 card 6). Each profile's "why families leave it" section is
this lifecycle wearing a brand name: Abeka's worksheet drowning, BJU's
relentless schedule, Sonlight's read-aloud fatigue and behind-ness guilt,
MFW's spring monotony, CLE and LIFEPAC's dryness. The publishers' own
mitigation is telling: Abeka veterans preach skipping pages, Sonlight
sells a 4-day schedule, Master Books builds the lighter week into the
product. The drift's destination is eclectic assembly (the market's
plurality), which is why the second half of many curriculum biographies
reads "we kept the math, dropped the rest, and pieced together our own."

## Consumables economics for multi-kid families

The category splits cleanly on what happens when the second and third
child reach the same grade. Consumable-heavy systems (Abeka worktexts,
TGTB course books, Master Books, CLE LightUnits, LIFEPACs, ACE PACEs)
charge a per-child tax every year: roughly $60-100 per subject-year for
the booklet systems and several hundred dollars per child for Abeka's
student kits, and written-in books recover almost nothing at resale.
Reusable-spine systems flip the math: Rod and Staff's hardbacks serve
every sibling for decades, Sonlight's book stacks resell at strong rates
through dedicated buy-sell-trade groups, and MFW built its whole pitch on
one core package teaching the entire family at once. TGTB is the hybrid
disruptor: the course book is consumable, but the free PDF license means
the second child costs toner instead of money. Two Homa implications:
family-style economics (MFW, TGTB history) is a purchase driver worth
matching in generated materials, and a generator of per-child printables
directly deletes the consumables tax, which the segment research already
flagged as a real, priced pain
([08-segments-curricula.md](../../homa-planning/icp-research/08-segments-curricula.md) §4).

## Gaps Homa can fill

- Permission and pruning. The boxes overload by design (classroom
  seatwork, 70% review ratios, relentless grids), and their veterans'
  universal advice is "skip things." Nobody in the category tells a
  specific mom what is safe to skip for her specific child. Homa can be
  that adjudicator, and it converts the category's biggest churn driver
  into a retention moment.
- Records that survive the box. Every publisher's paper trail dies when
  the family leaves (Abeka's registrar, BJU's Hub window, checked-off
  IGs in a binder). A curriculum-neutral, evidence-based record that
  follows the family through the documented drift is the category's
  missing continuity layer, and it is Homa's strongest answer to the
  accreditation instinct short of accreditation itself.
- The per-child practice layer. The consumables tax and the
  one-size-fits-all page are the same weakness: fixed print runs cannot
  adapt to a specific child or a third sibling. Generated, per-child,
  curriculum-toned materials (Abeka-style drill when wanted, TGTB-gentle
  when wanted, never busywork for a Sonlight family) sidestep both.
- The February moment. The lifecycle above says exactly when these
  families break: the product that helps her relax the model instead of
  abandoning the year captures the drift the boxes lose. Homa should be
  the thing she keeps when she drops the box: the box was the on-ramp,
  Homa is the road.
- Respect the worldview settings. This entire category is bought partly
  as worldview control (young-earth science, denominational nuances, the
  TGTB-LDS debate, Mennonite distinctives). Homa serves these families
  only if generated content never contradicts the family's settled
  choices uninvited; provenance and content controls, not statements of
  faith, are the trust currency (segment research §7 card 5 and §8).
