# Printable marketplaces: comparison and market notes

Category synthesis for `marketplaces/`. Profiles are the source of truth
for profiled platforms; prices are as of Jul 2026 and dated in each
profile. This lens covers the printable-supplement economy as a whole,
including Twinkl (profiled with the international wave, see
[twinkl.md](../marketplaces/twinkl.md)) and the Gumroad and Shopify
direct-sales creator economy (deliberately covered here rather than
profiled, since it is a pattern, not a platform). This category is
competitively load-bearing: these platforms collectively do the job
Homa's materials studio does, supplying the printable layer of the 4-6
concurrent-source stacks documented in the ICP research.

## The printable-economy stack

The buyers sort themselves by identity, and each platform holds the slice
whose identity it serves. The classroom teacher, and the homeschool mom
in her teacher role (planning a unit, patching a curriculum gap, hunting
decodables), buys on TPT: search runs on grade, standard, and skill
vocabulary, and school purchasing money flows there. The same woman
shopping as a mom (occupying the toddler, installing a morning ritual,
theming the season) buys on Etsy, where search runs on problem and
aesthetic and the product photographs as an object in her home. Twinkl
captures the volume consumer: whoever prints enough sheets per week that
per-item pricing feels like a meter running, including UK-influenced and
early-years families. The small marketplaces hold almost no buyers of
their own; they exist because sellers multi-home costlessly. And the
direct-sales tier holds the loyalists: a mom who trusts a specific
creator (a Gentle + Classical, a Harbor and Sprout, a Busy Toddler
follower) buys from that creator's own Shopify or Gumroad store, often
never comparison-shopping at all.

The same person occupies different slots in the same week, which is the
practical meaning of the 4-6 source assembly pattern: Singapore math as
spine, a TPT decodable packet, an Etsy morning menu, a creator-brand
seasonal unit, a free blog printable. Nobody in this economy owns the
whole stack; everybody owns a mood.

## Seller-brand trust vs platform trust

No per-item marketplace in the table vets pedagogy, so on those
platforms trust has nowhere to live except in seller-brands, and the
market has organized itself around that fact. On TPT, 81% of sales go to the top 1% of stores, and buyers learn
store names (The Moffatt Girls, Miss Giraffe) the way earlier homeschool
generations learned publisher names. On Etsy, the same concentration
expresses itself as shop archaeology: years on Etsy, cumulative sales
counts, review volume, Star Seller badges. The direct-sales tier is the
endpoint of the gradient: a seller-brand strong enough to leave the
platforms entirely, taking its audience with it and keeping the margin
(Harbor and Sprout on its own storefront, The Gentle + Classical Press
running its own shop with TPT as a satellite, blog-pipeline preschool
brands selling PDFs off their own sites, documented in
[preschool.md](preschool.md)).

Platform trust, meanwhile, is thin everywhere and diverging. TPT's
platform promise is breadth plus social proof, and the research record
(70% of best-selling history resources moderate or low quality) shows
the proof measures satisfaction, not soundness. Etsy's platform promise
is its stated mission, "Keep Commerce Human," which is exactly the
promise the AI flood attacks. The subscription players are the only ones whose platform brand
carries any quality claim at all: Twinkl because it authors in-house,
Teach Simple because it reviews submissions. It is not a coincidence
that those two are the platforms buyers name rather than the seller,
and that the per-item marketplaces are the ones where buyers name the
store.

## The AI wave and what it does to buyer trust

The AI-content flood hit this economy asymmetrically. Etsy is the
epicenter: image-generation collapsed the production cost of
premium-looking printables, flooded categories (wall art, planner
inserts, SVGs, and educational printables adjacent to them), crashed
prices toward $1-3 in the flooded segments, and forced a July 2024
policy (Creativity Standards) that legalizes "seller-prompted AI
creations" behind a disclosure line while banning prompt packs. Trade
reporting describes human sellers in digital-print categories losing
roughly 40% of monthly views between Jan 2025 and Jan 2026 (reported
via Digital Commerce 360; treat as trade-press, not verified). TPT is
partially insulated by its teacher-seller culture and
education-specific search, and its response is quieter: its public
content guidelines carry nothing like Etsy's formal AI standards, while
seller-community reporting describes a 2025 disclosure expectation for
substantially AI-generated resources plus search de-ranking of
undisclosed AI floods (single seller-blog source; treat as unverified).
Either way its flood arrives with far weaker labeling than Etsy's. The subscription libraries are
the most insulated, since their pitch is exactly "a human institution
stands behind every page" (though Twinkl is now shipping first-party AI
generation tools of its own, which will test how far institutional
trust stretches).

The buyer-side effect is the strategic fact: buyers are not learning to
reject AI-made materials, they are learning to reject anonymous
materials. The observed adaptations (shop-age archaeology, "human
touch" premiums, retreat to known seller-brands, subscription libraries
as safe harbors) are all versions of the same move: substitute a
trusted author for an untrustable feed. For education printables the
stakes are higher than for wall art, because a subtly wrong decodable
or letter-formation page fails invisibly, and the Etsy mom, unlike the
TPT teacher, usually has no training to catch it. The wave therefore
does not commoditize Homa's generation; it commoditizes anonymous
generation, and raises the value of generation with a name, a stated
pedagogy, and accountability attached.

## Subscription vs per-item

The two pricing models sell different feelings. Per-item (TPT, Etsy,
the small marketplaces) monetizes the moment of need: $4 feels free at
11pm when tomorrow's math needs a supplement, and the sale culture
(anchor prices, 65% off, bundles against a $135 "value") exists to keep
each moment feeling like a win. Its failure mode is the meter: heavy
users accumulate death-by-$4 spend and a folder of orphaned PDFs, and
every purchase re-runs the 45-minute search.

Subscription (Twinkl at real scale, Teach Simple as the micro version,
TPT's School Access as the district version) monetizes the standing
habit: about $90 a year buys the right to stop deciding, which is why
Twinkl leads with "saving you A LOT of time" rather than any content
claim. The trade-offs run the other way: a flat fee has to survive the
"do I still use this enough" audit every month (Teach Simple's
cancellation complaints show how badly that can go), in-house authorship
caps catalog breadth, and unlimited downloading does nothing about
finding and leveling the right page, so the search cost survives the
pricing model. The two models are converging from both ends (TPT sells
bundles and site licenses; Twinkl sells some standalone products), but
the underlying split remains: per-item sells the perfect moment,
subscription sells the absence of moments.

For Homa the instructive point: Homa is subscription-priced but does
not sell access to a pile, it sells the disappearance of the pile.
That is a third category the table does not contain.

## Homa positioning against each

The search-cost wedge from the TPT profile generalizes across the whole
category: every platform in the table ends with the mom doing the last
mile herself, finding, judging, leveling, and adapting a generic PDF to
one specific child. Homa's materials studio deletes the last mile.
Against each:

- Against TPT: the wedge as stated in the profile. Generate the
  exactly-right page from the child's actual state in seconds, in the
  ritual formats TPT proved (morning work, spiral review), with visible
  pedagogical provenance where TPT offers star ratings.
- Against Etsy: match the aesthetic bar, absorb the assembly tax
  selectively (print-ready and low-ink by default, cut-and-laminate as
  a chosen craft, per the busy-binder segment that enjoys it), and take
  the personalization crown: Etsy personalizes the child's name, Homa
  personalizes the child's skill state.
- Against Twinkl and the subscription libraries: the honest competitor
  framing is "unlimited access" vs "exactly right." A million-item
  library still hands the mom a search box; Homa's subscription
  includes the librarian, the leveler, and the printer-ready output.
  Where Twinkl's floor is in-house authorship, Homa's floor is stated
  pedagogy per generated artifact, which is auditable per family.
- Against the small marketplaces: no direct response needed. They are
  evidence that distribution and trust, not commission math or catalog
  mechanics, are the scarce goods in this economy.
- Against the direct-sales creator brands: do not fight the loyalty,
  join the stack. These brands are spines and seasonal color, not
  per-child practice engines; Homa should generate materials that sit
  beside a Gentle + Classical or Harbor and Sprout unit without
  aesthetic whiplash, and treat "we follow this creator" the way the
  atlas treats any named curriculum. The creator economy also sets
  Homa's trust bar: the unit of trust in printables is a named author
  with a face and a philosophy, and Homa's brand must carry that same
  weight, family by family.

One caution the category teaches: buyers now sniff for AI-generic. The
materials studio's output must be indistinguishable in warmth and craft
from the best seller-brand pages, and its provenance must be legible
(what this page practices, why this child got it). Anonymous-feeling
generation would place Homa on the losing side of the trust
realignment the AI wave started.
