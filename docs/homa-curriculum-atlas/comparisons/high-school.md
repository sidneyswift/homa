# High school and upper grades: comparison and market notes

Category synthesis for the wave 2 high school lane. Profiles are the
source of truth for program claims; prices are as of Jul 2026 and dated
in each profile. This lane is different from every other comparison doc
in the atlas because the product being bought changes at grade 9: below
it, families buy teaching; above it, they buy teaching plus proof. Every
purchase decision from algebra onward is shadowed by a transcript, a
credit count, and an exit plan (college, trade, sport, work), and the
programs that win this lane all answer the proof question somehow.

## The transcript and credit economy

The structural fact of homeschool high school is that the parent is the
school. In nearly all US arrangements the parent is the issuing
institution: they assign credits, compute the GPA, sign the transcript,
and confer the diploma, and this is normal, legal, and accepted.
HSLDA's guidance is blunt that the parent-made transcript is "just as
official" as a counselor's, and college admissions practice treats
parent-issued transcripts as a standard document class, with selective
schools requesting supporting evidence (course descriptions, test
scores, external grades) rather than rejecting the format. The working
credit currency is the Carnegie unit: one credit for a full-year course,
commonly backed by 120 to 180 instructional hours when anyone asks,
which almost nobody does unless the NCAA or a skeptical registrar is
involved.

Everything else in this lane is priced against that fact. Accreditation
anxiety is the market's most monetizable emotion: umbrella schools,
accredited academies, and WASC-style provider accreditations (Mr. D
Math's, for example) all sell the feeling that someone official stands
behind the record, even though for most families the parent-issued
document works fine. The vendors' credit vocabulary is part of the same
economy: Shormann and VideoText sell integrated courses that yield
multiple transcript lines (three credits from two Shormann algebra
courses, three claims from one VideoText Algebra), Notgrass sells one
course that produces history, English, and Bible credits, and 7Sisters
sells the meta-product, coaching the parent that her own course designs
and honors labels are defensible. Every one of these products includes
documentation explaining what to write on the transcript, because the
publisher knows the mom is the registrar.

The failure mode this economy produces is records debt: families
homeschool 9th and 10th grade loosely, then discover at 11th that they
must reconstruct two years of course titles, hours, and grades from
memory and a box of workbooks. The advice literature's fix is "build
the transcript as you go," which is exactly the behavior that fails
without tooling.

## Dual enrollment as the dominant modern exit

The modern homeschool high school increasingly ends at the community
college. National dual enrollment hit 2.8 million students in 2023-24,
up 12.7 percent in one year, with 71 percent of those students at
community colleges and growth in 47 states; states are actively paying
for it (California's CCAP-driven expansion with over $700 million
invested and a third of the class of 2025 participating, Texas's HB 8
performance funding tying community college money to dual-credit
completion). Homeschoolers ride this wave with structural advantages:
flexible schedules, freedom to enroll at whatever age the college
allows, and a culture that already treats the parent as guidance
counselor.

The pattern in practice: 9th and 10th grade at home (often with the
providers in the table above), then 11th and 12th partially or fully at
the community college, with courses double-counted as high school
credits and college credits. For the transcript, the college course
gets listed at home with the college named, and the college's own
transcript rides along as the external validation that admissions
offices trust most; for some families two years of dual enrollment
becomes an associate degree finishing alongside the diploma. Dual
enrollment beats AP in this culture on certainty (a B in the course is
credit; an AP exam is one high-stakes day), and beats everything on
subsidized cost in the states that fund it. State rules and ESA-fund
interactions vary widely; the repo's state-by-state treatment is
`docs/homa-planning/icp-research/06-regulatory-esa-landscape.md` (§5
master table, §6-7 for ESA funds as the payment rail), and several ESA
programs can pay for dual-enrollment tuition and provider courses.

## The outsourcing curve

Families who taught everything at the kitchen table in 3rd grade
outsource by 10th, and the curve has a shape: it starts at the algebra
wall (the first subject the average parent will not teach) and spreads
outward through lab science, upper writing, and foreign language. The
market has built a price ladder for it, visible in the table: free and
self-assembled (Guest Hollow, Khan), cheap self-graded content ($45
Fascinating Education, $99-155 Shormann and Teaching Textbooks tiers),
auto-graded courseware with named teachers ($169-199 Thinkwell),
human-graded distance teaching ($292-585 per year Derek Owens), live
online classes ($537 Mr. D live), co-op and academy classes, and
finally dual enrollment, where the state often pays. Each rung buys
more human attention and more external proof; what stays constant is
that the parent remains the aggregator of record, stitching four or
five providers into one child's year.

Two consequences matter. First, by grade 11 the typical stack is
majority-outsourced, so the family's real operating problem is
coordination: five logins, five gradebooks, five progress reports, one
transcript nobody is compiling. Second, provider grades become the
transcript's credibility layer (an outside teacher's B is worth more to
a registrar than a mom's A), which is why families pay Derek Owens for
grading or Mr. D for accreditation even when the teaching alone could
be had cheaper.

## AP, CLEP, and dual-credit decision culture

The three college-credit currencies have distinct homeschool mechanics,
and families trade them off explicitly:

- AP: the label is regulated; only courses passed through the College
  Board's AP Course Audit may be titled "AP" on a transcript, so most
  homeschool families teach AP-aligned content under another name
  ("Calculus with AP exam" patterns) and let the exam score carry the
  proof. Exam access is the real friction: parents cannot order AP
  exams, so the family must find a participating school's AP
  coordinator willing to seat an outside tester, starting the search in
  August or September against a mid-November ordering deadline, paying
  roughly $98 per exam. Providers sell into this: Thinkwell's
  AP-compatible courses are content-aligned but unaudited, and a small
  number of homeschool parents complete the audit themselves to earn
  the label.
- CLEP: the homeschool favorite for credit volume. No host school is
  needed (testing centers, plus remote proctoring), exams are cheaper
  than a course, and the frugal wing of the culture (the Homeschooling
  for College Credit community is its institution) treats CLEP as the
  best credit per dollar, with voucher programs periodically making
  exams free. Curricula advertise CLEP alignment as a feature: Shormann
  bundles CLEP Professor prep and markets "up to 14 college credits,"
  Thinkwell sells CLEP prep courses outright.
- Dual credit: the certainty play described above, increasingly
  state-funded, and the only currency that also produces a second
  institutional transcript.

The decision culture is pragmatic rather than prestige-driven: AP for
selective-admissions signaling, CLEP for cheap general-education
credits at credit-friendly colleges, dual enrollment for guaranteed
credit plus external validation. The one hard rule families learn late:
each target college's credit policy, not the credential itself,
determines what any of it is worth.

## NCAA eligibility gotchas

For the athlete subset, the NCAA Eligibility Center is the one
institution that audits a homeschool transcript line by line, and its
rules surprise families:

- There is no preapproved homeschool curriculum, ever. Each core course
  (English, math from Algebra 1 up, science, social science, world
  language, comparative religion or philosophy) must be documented on
  the Eligibility Center's own Core-Course Worksheet, parent-signed,
  with an administrator statement affirming the homeschool operated
  under state law.
- Credit-by-exam does not count: audited, CLEP, or credit-by-exam
  courses are not NCAA-approved core courses, which quietly breaks the
  CLEP-heavy strategy for athletes.
- Since August 2024 the separate nontraditional-program rules are
  gone; online and homeschool courses are evaluated under the general
  core-course criteria, which still expect real instructor-student
  interaction and evaluable student work, so fully self-graded
  courseware sits uneasily.
- Outsourced online courses are evaluated through the provider's own
  NCAA status (Mr. D Math's live core math sequence is NCAA-approved;
  many self-paced programs are not), so which seat the family bought
  matters, not just which brand.
- Dual enrollment counts only if it appears on the homeschool
  transcript with grade, credit, and the college's name and location
  clearly designated.

The practical gotcha is timing: families discover these rules in 11th
grade recruiting conversations, after two years of coursework that
was never documented to worksheet standard.

## Gaps Homa can fill

Every pain in this doc is a records problem before it is a teaching
problem, which is Homa's home turf:

- Transcript generation from logged work. Homa already accumulates the
  evidence (what was done, in what subject, for how long, with what
  outcomes); at high school that log should compile into a
  transcript-grade record: course titles, credit values with
  Carnegie-hour backing, grades with provenance, GPA, signed and
  exportable. The families doing this today do it in spreadsheets at
  11 pm, or pay umbrella schools for the feeling of officialness.
- Credit planning. A four-year credit plan (state graduation
  expectations, college-admission norms, NCAA core counts for
  athletes) that fills in as work is logged, warns about gaps early
  (the 11th-grade lab science hole, the missing personal finance
  requirement), and knows the credit semantics of real curricula: that
  Shormann Algebra 1 plus 2 yields a geometry credit, that Notgrass
  Exploring America is three lines, that a VideoText completion is
  claimable as three math credits.
- Four-year plans that survive switching. Curriculum churn does not
  stop at 9th grade, but every switch now has transcript consequences.
  Homa's crosswalk knowledge (which program covers what, where a child
  actually is) should translate mid-course switches into coherent
  credit records instead of lost semesters.
- Outsourcing coordination. By 11th grade the family runs four or five
  providers; Homa should ingest their gradebooks and progress emails
  into the one child-level record, then draft the course descriptions
  and provider attributions that selective admissions and the NCAA
  ask for.
- Exit-ramp guidance. Dual enrollment, AP seating deadlines
  (find a coordinator by fall, order by mid-November), CLEP
  strategy, and NCAA worksheets are all calendar-and-paperwork
  problems Homa can carry, referencing the state specifics in
  `docs/homa-planning/icp-research/06-regulatory-esa-landscape.md`
  rather than making the parent re-research them.

The positioning consequence mirrors the online-digital doc: Homa does
not compete with any provider in the table, it becomes the registrar
layer above all of them, which is the seat the parent already occupies
and hates.
