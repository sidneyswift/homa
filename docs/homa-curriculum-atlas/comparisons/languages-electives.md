# World languages and electives: comparison and market notes

Cross-curriculum view of the wave 2 electives profiles in
`../curricula/electives/` plus the Classical Academic Press publisher
profile in `../curricula/all-in-one/classical-academic-press.md`.
Snapshot dated 2026-07-13; per-profile front matter is the source of
truth for prices and tags. Electives here means the subjects moms shop
as their own aisle: world languages, art, music, logic, and typing,
with coding, PE, and personal finance still on the backlog.

## Electives are the guilt subjects

Every one of these products is sold against the same confession: art,
music, language, and logic are always in the September plan and are the
first things skipped by November. The testimonial language across the
profiles is uniform ("one of the biggest holes in our homeschool," "no
more dropping the ball on art"), and the product designs all attack the
same three skip-causes: prep time (You ARE an Artist needs two
supplies; SQUILT needs a speaker; Linney needs a paperback), parent
skill anxiety (video teachers at Hoffman and CAP; author commentary
audio from Linney; "no musical background needed" from SQUILT), and
scheduling weight (10-20 minute lessons everywhere, morning-time and
car-ride formats). This is the same dynamic the science comparison
documented (`science.md`: the most-skipped core subject is skipped over
time, confidence, mess, and supplies), one notch further down the
priority ladder: science gets skipped guiltily but electives get
skipped structurally, because when a week shrinks, the family triages
toward math and reading. The consequence for anyone building for this
market: an elective product is really selling skip-resistance, and the
ones that win (Duolingo streaks, Nana's two-supply lessons, Hoffman's
free videos) are the ones that made restarting after a dropped week
cost nothing.

## The co-op outsourcing pattern

Electives are the first subjects families outsource to a co-op, and the
publishers know it. Art, music, and PE lead the outsourcing because
they benefit from groups (audiences, ensembles, teams), because mess
and equipment amortize across families, and because they are the
subjects moms feel least qualified to teach. The product evidence runs
through this whole category: You ARE an Artist and SQUILT both publish
explicit co-op licensing (SQUILT caps a membership at five families
viewing together), CAP materials are staples of classical co-op
schedules and its Scholé Communities network is co-op infrastructure by
design, The Fallacy Detective markets itself as a co-op discussion
course, and Hoffman is the at-home fallback precisely for families
without a local lesson option. For Homa the practical point is that
"we do art at co-op on Thursdays" is a normal, complete answer about an
elective: the family's stack includes institutions, not just products,
and scheduling, records, and materials requests need to treat the co-op
class as a first-class stack element.

## Foreign language: the app-vs-curriculum confusion

Foreign language is the elective where families most often believe they
are doing a subject they are not doing. The Duolingo profile documents
the uniform reviewer verdict: loved as a supplement, insufficient as a
curriculum, because streaks measure consistency, not proficiency, and
the app teaches no grammar system, no writing, and no conversation. A
family can carry a 300-day streak and no ability to order lunch. The
confusion persists because nothing forces a reckoning until high
school, when transcript rules (two-plus years of one language for
college admissions, 120-150 documented hours per credit) force the
decision the family deferred: keep the app as daily practice and add
something credit-worthy (a textbook program, Rosetta Stone plus a
grammar supplement, a tutor, an online class), or drop the pretense.
The market has sorted itself around that fork: Duolingo owns the habit
layer, Rosetta Stone sells parent-light immersion that families do
count as primary content (with a grammar bolt-on), Linney's books own
the "are we serious about this" trial stage, and textbook programs and
live classes own the credit stage. Elementary-years language is
therefore mostly exposure theater, and honest reviewers say so; the
buying moment that matters is 8th-9th grade.

## Latin's special status

Latin is the one language that plays by different rules. In classical
homes it is not an elective at all but the spine subject, begun as
early as grade 1 (CAP's Song School Latin, Memoria's Prima Latina) and
carried, in theory, to AP. It needs no conversational payoff because
its promised outcomes are formation: English grammar and vocabulary
through roots, the discipline of an inflected language, and membership
in the classical tradition. That status produces market structures no
modern language has: two full competing K-12 ladders from rival
publishers (CAP's chant-and-color path vs Memoria's drill-and-form
path, the busiest single comparison decision in classical
homeschooling), a dedicated cheap on-ramp (Getting Started with Latin
as the "try Latin" book), National Latin Exam prep lines, and real
churn stories, because Latin is also the spine most often abandoned:
Latin fatigue is the documented first crack in both CAP and Memoria
stacks, and when Latin goes, the publisher's gravity weakens. Homa
should read "we do Latin" as a statement of identity and stack
architecture, not just a subject line, and treat a wobbling Latin habit
as an early signal the family's whole classical structure is under
load.

## Award-circuit economics

Electives are where homeschool award programs matter most, because they
are low-consideration purchases: a mom will research a math curriculum
for weeks, but a $15 logic workbook or a $60 art book gets bought on
badges. Two publishers in this category are the market's best award
machines. The Critical Thinking Co. stacks decades of seals on every
product page (Cathy Duffy Top Picks, Mensa, Old Schoolhouse, Practical
Homeschooling placements from 2014 through 2025, parenting-media
awards) and markets "200+ award-winning titles" as its identity.
Classical Academic Press converted the 2025 Practical Homeschooling
Reader Awards into a sweep (eight first places across Latin, Greek,
grammar, logic, public speaking, song-based, and middle school
science) and banners it on its homepage. The mechanics behind this are
documented in `../market-overview.md`: reader awards skew to the
traditional-Christian core, winners advertise wins for years, and
awards lag growth rather than predict it. Two corroborating details
from this wave: Cathy Duffy's current 103 Top Picks dropped electives
categories entirely for space (so elective publishers keep citing her
101 and 102 editions), and the genuinely fastest-growing elective
products (Duolingo, Hoffman, You ARE an Artist) have essentially no
award presence at all, because free tiers and social distribution do
the work awards used to do.

## Gaps Homa can fill

1. Elective scheduling that survives busy weeks. The category's core
   failure is structural skipping, and no product owns the recovery
   moment. A planner that treats electives as compressible rather than
   skippable (shrink piano to two ten-minute reviews, swap the art
   lesson to the unit already being studied, keep the Duolingo streak
   as the language touch) turns the guilt cycle into a managed rhythm.
   Everything in this aisle restarts badly by hand; Homa can make
   restart cost zero.
2. Turning interests and habits into elective credit records. The
   evidence layer is uniformly missing: Duolingo produces streaks, not
   hours; Hoffman produces skill, not a music credit; a chalk-pastel
   binder is a portfolio nobody dated; typing certificates live in a
   browser account. High school makes this acute (fine arts credits,
   language credits, the 120-150 hour documentation norm), and several
   publishers already gesture at it (You ARE an Artist's credit
   tracker, Rosetta Stone families logging supplemental grammar).
   Homa logging elective sessions as they happen, attaching artifacts,
   and emitting course descriptions and hour totals converts the
   family's actual life into transcript lines, which is clerical work
   no elective publisher will ever own across brands.
3. The app-to-curriculum bridge in language. The 8th-9th grade fork
   documented above is a predictable, high-stakes transition Homa can
   see coming from the family's stack and the kid's age, and can
   answer neutrally from atlas knowledge: what the streak actually
   covered, what a credit requires in their state, which programs
   bridge from where the child is.
4. Interest-to-elective conversion. Electives are where children's
   actual interests (drawing, Minecraft, piano by ear, a fixation on
   Greek myths) meet the curriculum market. Homa sits on interest
   signals from the whole family relationship and can propose the
   cheap, proven on-ramp at the right moment (a Linney book, a Mind
   Benders volume, a Hoffman unit), then quietly do item 2 so the
   interest accrues into a record.
5. Respecting the layer each product owns. The pattern across this
   aisle: content is increasingly free (Hoffman, Duolingo, typing);
   what sells is orchestration (Hoffman Premium's practice sessions,
   SQUILT's calendars, You ARE an Artist's monthly plans) and
   community (co-ops, member events). Homa should never compete with
   free elective content; the orchestration-and-records layer above
   all of it is exactly Homa's product shape, and it is the layer no
   single-subject publisher can build across the whole stack.
