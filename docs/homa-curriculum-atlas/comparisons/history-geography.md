# History, geography, and social studies: category comparison

Cross-curriculum notes for the history-geography category. Profiles live in
`../curricula/history-geography/`. Written 2026-07-13; re-verify prices and
product lineups before quoting anywhere parent-facing.

## The four-year cycle as market infrastructure

The four-year chronological cycle (ancients, medieval, early modern, modern,
one year each, repeated with more depth in grades 5-8 and again in 9-12) is
the closest thing this category has to a standard. Susan Wise Bauer's The
Well-Trained Mind defined the periods (5000 BC-400 AD, 400-1600, 1600-1850,
1850-present) and The Story of the World turned them into four purchasable
volumes; that volume structure is now load-bearing market infrastructure the
way Saxon's grade numbering is in math. Everyone else positions relative to
it. BiblioPlan states outright that it uses the same time divisions and even
sells a Cool History Classics workbook keyed to SOTW chapters. Curiosity
Chronicles keeps the same four eras while rejecting the cycle's Eurocentric
defaults (and stretches to five-plus years because early modern and modern
each outgrew one volume). History Quest compresses to a three-year world
cycle plus a US year, and its stopgap Modern History Bridge literally
schedules SOTW volumes 3 and 4 as spines. The Mystery of History keeps
four chronological volumes but moves the era boundaries to fit a biblical
frame (Creation to the Resurrection is year one, and volume three is
1455-1707). The main structures that opt out entirely are the era-course
model (Notgrass sells one-year surveys per school level, aimed at credits,
not cycles) and the pack model (Beautiful Feet sells eras a la carte).
Practical consequence for any product in this space: "we're in year two" or
"SOTW 3" is a complete, meaningful statement of where a family is, and tools
should resolve cycle position, not just grade level. A second consequence:
the cycle synchronizes siblings by design, which is why family-style
teaching (below) is the norm here.

## Read-aloud and audiobook culture

Elementary history in this market is consumed by ear. SOTW normalized it:
the text is written to be read aloud, and the Jim Weiss audiobook recordings
(8 to 13 hours per volume) made "history in the minivan" a standard
homeschool practice; Weiss's voice is a generational touchstone, and the
25th anniversary edition treated the new narrator (Katherine de Leiros) as
headline product news. The pattern is now table stakes: Mystery of History
ships author-narrated MP3s by the quarter (with or without music beds),
History Quest and Curiosity Chronicles both sell professionally produced
audiobooks as first-class components (Curiosity Chronicles uses a voice
cast for its dialogue format), BiblioPlan sells Remember the Days audio,
and Drive Thru History is the pattern taken to its endpoint, video as the
whole product. Two design implications. First, the audio is the content
delivery channel and the paper is the response channel (narration, maps,
timeline, projects), so materials must work for a child who heard, rather
than read, the lesson. Second, audio creates skip-friction: MOH families
reported abandoning the audio in later volumes because they could not edit
mature content on the fly the way a reading parent can. Controls over what
plays matter.

## The secular versus providential split

History is the worldview battleground subject of homeschooling. Math
publishers can stay neutral; history publishers cannot, because the subject
itself forces decisions about Genesis, dating (MOH uses Ussher's 4004 BC
creation chronology), whether scripture is a historical source, and whose
story the narrative centers. The market has responded by splitting into
camps with almost no neutral middle. On the providential side: Mystery of
History (Bible as history on one timeline), Notgrass (a Bible credit inside
the history course), TruthQuest (history as what God does first), BiblioPlan
and Drive Thru History. On the secular side, a newer generation built in
explicit reaction: Curiosity Chronicles and History Quest both lead their
marketing with the word secular and treat religions as historical facts to
study. The Story of the World sits in the crossfire: Cathy Duffy classifies
it "secular but Christian friendly", secular families object to the weight
and framing of its Bible narratives, and Christian families supplement it
or leave for MOH. Beautiful Feet occupies the soft middle (gently Christian
with charter-approved secular variants), which is itself instructive: the
same books with religious titles removed is a productized worldview toggle.
There is no single history curriculum a mixed-conviction co-op can agree
on, and families switching curricula across this line must often re-buy an
entire era. Any assistant serving this market needs the family's worldview
stance as first-class data before generating a single history material.

## Family-style teaching

History in this market is taught to the family, not to the child. Math
programs put each sibling on their own level; history programs are built so
one era, one lesson, one read-aloud serves everyone at once, and the
differentiation happens in the output, not the input. The engineering for
this is the category's most distinctive feature: MOH's Companion Guide
splits every lesson's activities into Younger (K-3), Middle (4-8), and
Older (9-12) tracks; BiblioPlan runs four grade-band columns (K-2, 3-5,
5-8, 8-12) through every week of its plan plus four Cool History workbook
levels; SOTW's activity guides span a grade spread with narration scribed
for the youngest and outlining added in volume 4; TruthQuest annotates
every book recommendation by reading level; Notgrass's elementary and
middle courses advertise pick-the-assignments flexibility across a four
grade span. The economics follow: one $80-160 purchase covers three kids,
which is why per-family pricing dominates and reproducible student pages
are standard. The failure mode is also predictable: the multi-age promise
breaks when the content itself ages up (MOH volumes III-IV outpace young
siblings) or when output demands diverge too far. But the core insight
holds: a history product that forces per-child content silos fights the
market's grain; one shared moment with per-child artifacts is the pattern
families actually run.

## Switching paths

Observed movement between programs, from community threads and reviews:

- SOTW to Mystery of History: wanting Christ-centered chronology instead
  of secular-but-friendly. The reverse path (MOH to SOTW) happens over
  later-volume content objections and audiobook editability.
- SOTW to Curiosity Chronicles or History Quest: secular families hitting
  the Bible-narrative weighting; this is the largest documented exit.
- Curiosity Chronicles and History Quest trade families over voice: banter
  versus calm narrative; and History Quest families hit the modern-history
  gap and patch with the Bridge, Curiosity Chronicles Modern, or SOTW 4.
- Elementary cycle programs (SOTW, MOH, History Quest) to Notgrass at
  grades 5-9: the transcript turn, when families stop wanting a cycle and
  start wanting named credits. History Quest also routes its own graduates
  to History Odyssey.
- Beautiful Feet and Sonlight trade literature-first families on scope and
  price (BFB is history-only and cheaper); BFB families needing assessment
  structure move to Notgrass.
- TruthQuest and BiblioPlan absorb DIY-inclined families from all of the
  above who want to keep their spines but need book lists (TruthQuest) or
  a multi-age weekly plan (BiblioPlan) around them.

The recurring trigger events: a worldview mismatch discovered mid-year, a
new baby collapsing read-aloud time, the oldest child hitting grade 5-6
output expectations, and the arrival of high school transcripts.

## Gaps Homa can fill

1. Cycle-aware scheduling. The standard pains are mechanical: 42 chapters
   into 36 weeks (SOTW), restart-after-a-move, syncing a new kindergartner
   into year three of the family's cycle. Homa can hold cycle position as
   state and generate the catch-up or slow-down plan, which no publisher
   does.
2. Per-child artifacts from one shared lesson. MOH and BiblioPlan prove
   the demand with static three-and-four-track menus; Homa can generate the
   narration prompt, the outline assignment, the map, and the essay prompt
   from the same chapter, tuned to each actual child, on demand.
3. Worldview-calibrated generation. The category's deepest split is
   unserved in the middle: Homa should treat worldview stance (and its
   nuances: creation dating, scripture-as-source, political register) as
   family configuration and generate history materials that respect it,
   including for the family using a curriculum across the line from their
   own convictions.
4. The modern-history hole. Both major secular lines are incomplete at the
   modern end (History Quest's Modern Times unreleased, Curiosity
   Chronicles Modern volumes 2-3 in progress), and SOTW 4 stops at 2000
   with religion thinned out. Generated, worldview-calibrated 1900-present
   units have no strong incumbent.
5. Output-side support the guides skip: grading and feedback on the heavy
   written work (Notgrass high school), assessment for programs that have
   none (BFB, History Quest, Curiosity Chronicles) when a state or charter
   requires evidence, and turning notebooks, Travel Logs, and timelines
   into portfolio documentation automatically.
6. Audio-aware materials. Since families consume lessons by ear, Homa
   materials should assume listen-first (review questions and maps that do
   not require the text in hand) and offer content controls the static
   audiobooks cannot (the MOH later-volume problem).
