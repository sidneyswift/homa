# Catholic publishers and programs: comparison and market notes

Companion to the eight wave 2 profiles in `../curricula/all-in-one/`:
Seton Home Study School, Mother of Divine Grace, Catholic Heritage
Curricula, Kolbe Academy, Homeschool Connections, TAN Academy, Sophia
Institute's Spirit of Truth, and Our Lady of Victory School. Profiles
carry the detail; this file compares the programs and interprets the
Catholic market as a whole, especially where it behaves differently from
the Protestant market wave 1 mapped in
[all-in-one-traditional-christian.md](all-in-one-traditional-christian.md)
and [classical-charlotte-mason.md](classical-charlotte-mason.md). Prices
are summarized from the profiles, all as of Jul 2026.

Scale, for orientation: analyses from The Pillar estimate 400,000 to
500,000 Catholic children are homeschooled in the US, roughly one for
every three of the 1.68 million students in Catholic schools (NCEA
2024-25), and the sector's flagship event, Seton's IHM National Catholic
Homeschool Conference, runs over 200 vendor tables with confessions
ongoing during the sessions. This is a full parallel market with its own
publishers, schools, reviewers, and conference circuit, not a Catholic
shelf inside the Protestant one.

Reading the table: four of the eight are enrollment schools, and that is
the story. The Protestant market's center of gravity is the publisher
(Abeka, BJU, TGTB) with accredited academies as a premium add-on; the
Catholic market's center of gravity is the school (Seton, MODG, Kolbe,
OLVS) with pure publishers (CHC, TAN) and platforms (Homeschool
Connections) positioned explicitly for or against that default.

## How the Catholic market differs from the Protestant one

Enrollment-school culture is the deepest difference. Catholic
homeschooling was built by families whose reference institution was the
parochial school, and its major products reproduce that institution at
home: report cards, transcripts, diplomas, counselors, registrars,
diocesan recognition, and accreditation (MSA-CESS at Seton, WASC at
MODG, Cognia and NAPCIS at Kolbe, NAPCIS at OLVS). In wave 1's
Protestant category, accreditation was a narrow-purpose add-on bought
for transcripts and skeptical relatives; here it is closer to the
default posture, and the dissenters have to argue their case. CHC
devotes FAQ space to research showing parent-signed transcripts satisfy
top colleges, and Homeschool Connections publishes a manifesto for why
it refuses to become an academy. Both arguments only exist because the
default runs the other way. The practical consequence for anyone
serving this market: the family's academic record often lives inside a
school's registrar, quarterly submission rhythms structure the year
(Seton's report forms, Kolbe's portal samples, OLVS's tutor packets,
MODG's quarterly assessments), and "will this satisfy my school" is a
live question Protestant-market families rarely ask.

Sacramental preparation is a curriculum feature with no Protestant
equivalent. The First Reconciliation and First Communion year (grade 2
in every major program's sequence) and the Confirmation year (Seton
places it in grade 7; dioceses vary) are family milestones that the
curriculum is expected to carry: Seton builds them into Religion 2 and
Religion 7 with imprimatur-bearing texts, CHC schedules Faith and Life 2
with its own Preparing to Receive Jesus activity supplement, and Sophia
sells a dedicated Spirit of Truth sacramental prep line. Because
sacraments are administered by the parish, not the homeschool, these
years force coordination with diocesan requirements, and diocesan
relations are a real variable: The Pillar's reporting on the Diocese of
San Diego restricting homeschool groups from meeting at parishes shows
the institutional friction this market navigates that Protestant
congregational homeschoolers mostly do not.

The trust test is magisterium fidelity, not creation science. Wave 1
documented young-earth stances as the Protestant market's litmus
(Apologia and Master Books lead with it; TGTB gets interrogated over its
founder's faith). The Catholic market runs a different test: is the
content faithful to the Magisterium, does the religion text carry an
imprimatur, is the provider on the Cardinal Newman Society's recommended
list (Kolbe advertises this), is the teaching "loyal to the Magisterium"
(Homeschool Connections' pledge, TAN's framing). Science is largely not
the battleground: Catholic programs teach standard-age science with a
providence frame (CHC's Behold and See markets "up-to-date science,"
MODG schedules secular TOPS kits and Building Blocks texts, Kolbe uses
mainstream publishers), so the young-earth vetting Protestant buyers
perform gets replaced by doctrinal vetting, translation preferences
(Douay-Rheims versus modern Catholic translations marks the
traditionalist spectrum), and catechism lineage (Baltimore Catechism
versus post-conciliar catechetical series).

## The classical-Catholic overlap: MODG and Kolbe versus Memoria

Classical education has deep Catholic roots, and the Catholic market
has two native classical schools with different centers: MODG's is the
consultant (Berquist's trivium staging plus a person who customizes it
per family), Kolbe's is the institution (Ignatian method, honors
designations, diploma tiers, substitution rights). The third player in
Catholic classical carts is not Catholic at all: Memoria Press
([memoria-press.md](../curricula/all-in-one/memoria-press.md)), whose
"mere Christianity" stance, best-in-market Latin, and finished
curriculum manuals draw substantial Catholic use (Memoria's own staff
describe the student body of its school as roughly one third Catholic,
and its forum threads show Catholic families choosing it because
explicitly Catholic classical programs feel less rigorously classical
to them, while much Protestant classical material reads anti-Catholic).
The friction points are specific and documented on Memoria's forums:
its Christian Studies strand uses a KJV-based children's Bible without
the deuterocanonical books, so Catholic users swap in Catholic Bible
histories and Douay-Rheims memory verses; and Memoria publicly
considered, then declined to publish, a Catholic supplement to preserve
its ecumenical identity. The practical shape of Catholic classical
stacks is therefore: MODG or Kolbe as the school, or Memoria as the
curriculum with the family supplying catechesis, plus shared classical
furniture across all three (Saxon math, Latin as identity subject,
Great Books lists, Bethlehem Books historical fiction).

## Where Catholic families buy secular or Protestant materials

The pattern, visible across every profile: skills subjects travel across
worldview lines, content subjects do not, and religion never does. CHC
states the community's own justification while pushing back on it: some
Catholic homeschoolers use secular or Protestant materials, "arguing
that there is no such thing as 'Catholic' grammar, spelling, or
geography" (CHC FAQ).

- Math is the free-trade zone. MODG schedules Abeka arithmetic for
  grades 1-3 (a Pensacola Baptist publisher; see
  [abeka.md](../curricula/all-in-one/abeka.md)) and Saxon from grade 4
  ([saxon-math.md](../curricula/math/saxon-math.md)); Seton ships
  Teaching Textbooks pre-algebra in grade 8 and offers Saxon Geometry in
  high school ([teaching-textbooks.md](../curricula/math/teaching-textbooks.md));
  Kolbe offers Sadlier, Singapore, and legacy Saxon tracks; CHC sends
  buyers to Christianbook.com, a Protestant retailer, for the math it
  deliberately does not publish.
- Writing leans on IEW ([iew.md](../curricula/language-arts/iew.md)).
  Catholic recommendation lists circulate the fact that founder Andrew
  Pudewa is himself Catholic, which removes the vetting question, and
  families running Catholic unit studies like RC History's Connecting
  with History commonly pair IEW for the composition assignments.
- History splits by design: Story of the World
  ([story-of-the-world.md](../curricula/history-geography/story-of-the-world.md))
  appears in Catholic homes as audio and supplement (MODG family reviews
  document exactly this use), while TAN's Story of Civilization exists
  precisely to be the Catholic-authored alternative with the Church kept
  in the narrative, and Catholic unit studies (Connecting with History)
  schedule Catholic spines with SOTW as an optional extra.
- Spelling, phonics, vocabulary, and logic move freely: Writing Road to
  Reading, Wordly Wise, English from the Roots Up, Editor in Chief, and
  Critical Thinking Co. materials sit inside MODG syllabi without
  comment.

What gets swapped out when Protestant materials cross the line:
devotional and doctrinal content. The Memoria Bible swap above is the
canonical example; Protestant memory verses get replaced with Catholic
translations; young-earth science (Apologia, Master Books) is the one
Protestant category Catholic families largely avoid rather than adapt,
since it fails their science expectations without offering their
doctrine. The Good and the Beautiful debate runs in reverse: wave 1
documented Catholic reviewers using TGTB happily while some Protestant
reviewers warn against its LDS founder, a reminder that each market
runs its own trust test on the same product
([the-good-and-the-beautiful.md](../curricula/all-in-one/the-good-and-the-beautiful.md)).

## The traditionalist-versus-mainstream spectrum

The Catholic market sorts along a liturgical-doctrinal spectrum that has
no precise Protestant analog. At one pole, Our Lady of Victory School
teaches from deliberately pre-1960 texts, and TAN Books' catalog was
built reprinting pre-conciliar classics for the Latin Mass constituency.
Seton is conservative-mainstream: Baltimore Catechism lineage and
traditional aesthetics, but diocesan-recognized, quoting John Paul II
and the Second Vatican Council in its materials. MODG, Kolbe, CHC,
Homeschool Connections, and Sophia are mainstream and
magisterium-forward, with the Newman Guide network as their shared
quality signal. Buyers navigate the spectrum through proxies: which
catechism (Baltimore versus contemporary series), which Bible
translation (Douay-Rheims versus RSV-CE and kin), whether the program's
saints and history carry a pre-conciliar frame. Two practical notes:
the spectrum is about content vintage and liturgy, not homeschool
method (traditionalists and mainstream families both run
school-at-home); and products cross it more easily than institutions
do (OLVS recommends Sophia's mainstream Spirit of Truth; TAN supplies
spines to mainstream MODG).

## Gaps Homa can fill

1. Worldview-respectful assistance without pretending to be Catholic.
   This market's trust runs on institutional signals Homa cannot and
   should not fake: imprimaturs, Newman Guide listings, magisterium
   pledges. The wrong move is generated doctrine or a simulated Catholic
   voice; the right move is provenance: know the family's program and
   catechism, source religious content from their own chosen texts
   rather than composing it, respect sacramental-prep years as sacred
   family territory where Homa assists logistics (scheduling, parish
   coordination reminders, memory-work tracking) and never writes the
   catechesis. The same content-control principle wave 1 established
   for Protestant worldview families applies, with the axis rotated
   from creation science to doctrinal fidelity.
2. Record-keeping that satisfies enrollment schools. Four of the eight
   profiled programs run on quarterly submission mechanics: Seton's
   report forms with home grades and scanned samples, Kolbe's portal
   grades with work samples per subject, OLVS's mailed packets behind
   lesson plans, MODG's quarterly rhythm with a consultant. Assembling
   those packets is pure clerical burden on the mother, and Homa's
   evidence log maps onto it almost exactly: capture the work as it
   happens, then emit the packet each school expects. For the
   anti-accreditation half (CHC, Homeschool Connections), the same log
   becomes the parent-made transcript both publishers tell families to
   build themselves. One capability, two postures, the whole market.
3. Pruning and pacing against "Seton stress" and syllabus burnout. The
   two biggest Catholic programs share the Protestant boxes' churn
   driver: overload by completeness, with the publishers' own veterans
   preaching adjustment (Seton's motto is adjusting the program to fit
   the child; MODG reviewers warn that doing everything in the syllabus
   burns families out). An assistant that knows which Seton assignments
   are required versus optional, or how MODG veterans actually trim
   Saxon problem sets, converts the market's top churn moment into
   retention, exactly as the wave 1 boxes comparison argued.
4. Multi-age orchestration is native here. TAN's schoolhouse model,
   MODG's combine-the-siblings syllabus practice, and the large-family
   economics running through every pricing section (per-family tuition
   at MODG, five-student subscriptions at Homeschool Connections,
   sibling discounts at Seton and OLVS) all say the same thing: the
   median serious Catholic homeschool has several students. One-topic,
   per-child-difficulty materials generation and cross-child scheduling
   are worth more in this market than anywhere outside it.
5. The AI door is already open, with a posted boundary. Homeschool
   Connections publishes guides for using ChatGPT in homeschool planning
   while warning against tools that do the child's thinking, the same
   boundary the repo's segment research documents across worldview
   families. Homa's clipboard-not-child positioning is not just
   compatible with this market; its largest course platform has already
   articulated it to its own customers.
