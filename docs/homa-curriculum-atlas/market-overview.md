# Market overview: how curricula win

Research snapshot 2026-07-13. How big the market is, who ranks curricula,
how families actually discover and choose, and which names hold the most
mindshare. Deep sizing and segment evidence already lives in
`docs/homa-planning/icp-research/` (especially `01-market-size-demographics.md`
and `08-segments-curricula.md`); this file adds the curriculum-market lens
and does not re-derive what those files established.

## The shape of the market

Established by the repo's ICP research (see links above, all sourced there):

- 1.9M (federal floor) to 3.4M (NHERI ceiling) homeschooled students in the
  US, growing about 5% a year post-pandemic, with roughly 6% of school-age
  children now homeschooled.
- Eclectic assembly is the plurality behavior: only 21% of parents in the
  best available study used any curriculum package, and only 5% used one
  package as their sole source. Families report 4 to 6 concurrent
  curriculum sources.
- Two price universes coexist: the $500 to $1,900 box and community
  universe (Abeka, BJU, Sonlight, Classical Conversations) and the $0 to
  $200 per subject assembly universe (The Good and the Beautiful, PDF
  curricula, per-subject math), and the assembly universe is where the
  plurality lives.
- Mid-year curriculum switching is normalized; resale recovers 40 to 60%
  of retail on non-consumables through Facebook BST groups and
  homeschoolclassifieds.com.

Market-research firms size the "homeschool curriculum market" anywhere from
about $0.5B to $8B depending on what counts as curriculum (packages only vs
the whole ecosystem of courseware, tutoring, and services). Example: one
2026 report puts homeschool curriculum at $3.8B in 2025 growing 9.2% CAGR,
with complete packages the largest slice at 38.5% and North America 45% of
revenue ([marketintelo](https://marketintelo.com/report/homeschool-curriculum-market),
accessed 2026-07-13). Treat all of these as directional: definitions vary
wildly across reports. The reliable claims are the enrollment counts and
the per-family spend range (roughly $600 to $2,500 per student per year
across sources, matching the ICP research's price universes).

A RAND nationally representative survey found 88% of homeschooling parents
use additional support beyond their core approach: over 40% use online
resources, nearly a quarter enroll a child in an online school, 10% use a
tutor ([The 74 coverage](https://www.the74million.org/article/exclusive-most-homeschoolers-also-use-an-array-of-resources-data-shows/),
accessed 2026-07-13). The stack, not the box, is the unit of analysis.

## Who ranks curricula (the awards landscape)

There is no Nielsen for homeschool curricula. Mindshare is built through a
few recurring institutions, each with a different mechanism and bias:

| Ranker | Mechanism | Bias to know about |
|---|---|---|
| Cathy Duffy Reviews / "103 Top Picks" | One veteran reviewer, thousands of long-form reviews since the 1980s, periodic Top Picks book (current edition: 103 Top Picks, 2022) | The de facto industry standard; broad but single-voice; strong with traditional and Christian publishers |
| Practical Homeschooling Reader Awards | Reader satisfaction voting since 1998, annual, category-by-category | Skews to the magazine's traditional/Christian readership; publishers advertise wins for years |
| Old Schoolhouse Blue Ribbon Awards | Homeschool Influencer Network members vote on products they reviewed that year | Review-crew model: vendors supply products for review, so the candidate pool is pay-to-play adjacent |
| homeschool.com "Back to Homeschool" awards | Reader/community voting run by a media property | Marketing-heavy |
| SEA Homeschoolers community | Secular community vetting (100K+ families) | The trust source for secular buyers; a "secular" stamp from SEA circles matters more than any award |
| Reddit and Facebook groups | Ambient recommendation culture | Where the honest churn stories live; r/homeschool threads are the closest thing to uncensored review data |

Award wins matter as social proof but they lag the market. Example: the
2025 Practical Homeschooling Reader Awards gave Heart of Dakota eight
firsts including History and High School
([Heart of Dakota announcement](https://www.heartofdakota.com/2025/05/15/thank-you-for-voting-for-us-in-the-2025-practical-homeschooling-reader-awards/),
accessed 2026-07-13), and Apologia claims top science rankings from 2011
through 2026 ([Apologia awards page](https://www.apologia.com/awards/),
accessed 2026-07-13). Meanwhile the fastest-growing publisher of the era
(The Good and the Beautiful) built its position with a free-PDF strategy
and social media, not awards. The 2025 Blue Ribbon winners
([Old Schoolhouse](https://theoldschoolhouse.com/homeschool-help/homeschool-curriculum-awards-2025-curriculum-recommendations-and-family-favorites/),
accessed 2026-07-13) skew to small vendors (Learn Math Fast, JAM with
Latin, CTCMath), which shows the review-crew candidate-pool effect.

Reading rule for the atlas: awards tell you who serves the traditional
core well; community sentiment and growth signals tell you who is winning
the next cohort. Profiles record both.

## How families actually discover and choose

The discovery funnel, assembled from the ICP research and this pass:

1. Facebook groups and word of mouth: the ambient default. Per-curriculum
   groups double as support and resale channels.
2. Review infrastructure: Cathy Duffy first, then blog reviews and
   YouTube flip-throughs (video page-turn reviews are the online
   substitute for touching the product).
3. Conventions: the hands-on channel. Great Homeschool Conventions runs
   the largest circuit with explicit tracks (Classical, Charlotte Mason,
   special needs) and a vendor hall where 20 to 40% convention discounts
   close sales ([GHC](https://greathomeschoolconventions.com/), guide
   coverage at [homeschoolstartguide](https://homeschoolstartguide.com/blog/homeschool-convention),
   accessed 2026-07-13). The convention circuit is worldview-gated
   (explicitly Christian organizers), which is why secular buyers route
   around it online (established in ICP research §8).
4. Philosophy-quiz culture: "what's your homeschool style" quizzes and
   Cathy Duffy's learning-styles-and-approach charts are the standard
   onboarding ritual for newcomers. The market teaches moms to identify a
   philosophy first, then buy within it.
5. Placement tests: free publisher placement tests (Saxon, Singapore,
   Math Mammoth, Teaching Textbooks) are the standard entry mechanic for
   skill subjects and a real data asset for publishers.

Seasonality: buying concentrates May through August (convention season
through back-to-school), with a second wave in December-January and a
churn-driven "rescue purchase" wave around the documented
November/February burnout points (ICP research §7 card 6).

## Top-mindshare names per category

Triangulated from the study data (curriculum packages named by parents:
Sonlight, Abeka, My Father's World, Heart of Dakota, Classical
Conversations), reviewer consensus, and roundup convergence
([NHERI study](https://nheri.org/home-school-researcher-parent-perspectives-curriculum-and-homeschooling-approaches/),
[homeschoolstartguide roundup](https://homeschoolstartguide.com/blog/popular-homeschool-curriculum),
accessed 2026-07-13). This is mindshare, not measured market share; no
public dataset measures actual unit share.

- Math: Saxon (most-used reputation, especially elementary), Teaching
  Textbooks (independence), Math-U-See (manipulatives), Singapore
  (Dimensions Math and Primary Mathematics, the conceptual standard),
  Beast Academy (advanced/puzzle), CTCMath and Khan (online).
- Reading and LA: All About Reading dominates phonics mindshare, IEW
  dominates writing, The Good and the Beautiful dominates budget LA,
  Explode the Code and Handwriting Without Tears own their niches.
- Science: Apologia dominates the Christian side; Mystery Science
  dominates the secular elementary side; Real Science-4-Kids and BFSU
  serve the rigor-focused.
- History: Story of the World is the category king; Mystery of History is
  the Christian counterpart; Notgrass owns upper grades.
- All-in-one: Abeka and BJU (traditional), Sonlight and BookShark
  (literature), The Good and the Beautiful (budget open-and-go), My
  Father's World and Heart of Dakota (Christian blends), Memoria Press
  and Classical Conversations (classical), Ambleside Online (free CM).
- Online: Time4Learning (default courseware), Khan Academy (free), 
  Outschool (live classes), Miacademy and Power Homeschool (school-at-home
  screens), IXL (practice supplement).

Per-curriculum evidence lives in the profiles; this list is the map key.

## What wins, structurally

Patterns visible across everything above, for the implications doc:

1. Free-plus-beautiful beats cheap: TGTB's free PDFs built the fastest
   brand of the decade. Free placement tests and samples are the standard
   top-of-funnel everywhere.
2. Open-and-go is the killer feature word: the market's chronic constraint
   is mom's prep time, not money. Scripted lessons sell.
3. Independence is the second killer feature: anything that lets a child
   self-run (Teaching Textbooks, CLE light units, courseware) wins large
   families and working parents, at a known cost in depth.
4. Philosophy is the store layout: moms are taught to shop by approach
   (classical, CM, traditional) before comparing products. Any product
   that ignores the philosophy vocabulary reads as ignorant.
5. Community is a moat: Classical Conversations sells belonging; Facebook
   groups keep switchers inside a brand's gravity; review crews and
   influencer networks are the distribution.
6. The stack is the real product: 79% assemble. The market's actual
   competitor to any single curriculum is the mom's mix, which is exactly
   the layer Homa operates at.
